* The chapter

"I can do the drill," Maren said. "That isn't it."

It was late on Friday afternoon and the Performance Laboratory was emptying
round them; she could hear doors along the corridor. Three days of the new
exercise had taken her from thirty-five percent to forty-one. It was moving
again. She had come anyway.

"Then what is it?"

"I don't know what I'm drilling /toward/." She had tried to write this down
on the bus and failed. "You asked me for a Tuesday and I gave you one. The
mezzanine, Herr Brandl, nobody looks at me. But that's from outside. That's
what a camera would see. I've never once in my life followed two minutes of
spoken German. I don't know what it's like to be the person who can. When
Frau Reinholt listens she isn't doing what I do, only quicker. She's doing
some other thing, and I can't imagine it. I don't know whether she
translates as she goes or whether it just arrives meaning something." She
pressed her hands together. "I'm practising toward a room I can't picture
myself in. Every morning I sit down and it's like feeling along a wall in
the dark for a door somebody's told me is there."

Keane did not answer.

He was quiet for so long that she thought she had said something foolish.
Then he got up, went to the door, and closed it. He had never closed it
before.

He opened the bottom drawer of the filing cabinet and came back with the
stack of soft-cornered cards she had seen him riffling by the kettle in
January, and sat down with them on his knee.

"I said your twenty might have a second use. I have spent six weeks
deciding whether to tell you what." He did not look at her. "You wrote to
me because I was the careful one. I'd like to stay the careful one in your
eyes, and I don't think I shall."

He laid the cards out on the low table, face up, in four rows. There were
twenty-three. Each had a few lines in his upright hand, some so old the ink
had browned.

She read the nearest without meaning to. /The slip at Carrick. The boat is
in the water and I am stepping the mast./

"They're scenes," she said. "Like mine."

"The same rules. I gave you my rules. Things I want, each written as though
I had it. When I made each one I spent a while inside it. Five minutes,
more. What I could see and hear, what was in my hands. Made it as real as I
could, the once."

"And then?"

"And then I don't dwell on them. I run through them. One after another,
quickly, then quicker, until they aren't pictures any more, just a sort of
flicker with all of them in it. A few minutes. Every morning, and whenever
I think of it. When I'm pleased. When I'm furious. Alongside whatever I'm
feeling; I don't try to feel anything else."

"How long have you —"

"Nineteen years."

Maren looked at the man who had spent thirty-five years asking
professionals to account for their practice hour by hour.

"That's visualization," she said. "That's — picture it and it will come.
I've got three books about that in the box. I put them at the bottom."

"Yes."

"You're a scientist."

"I am. This isn't science. I'd like you to hear me say both of those in one
breath." He turned a card over and back. "The people I had it from used it
for something else entirely, which I'm not going to defend to you either.
One of them is a sociologist called Bengston. He has published
experiments; I've read them more than once. They're on mice. They concern
healing. They are more careful than you'd expect, and I do not know what to
make of them. And they have nothing whatever to say about whether running
pictures through your head does anything for a mind."

"So what's the evidence that it does?"

"There isn't any. Not weak evidence. None. Nobody has looked." He met her
eyes at last. "If Ines asked me for a reference I should have nothing to
hand her. I have a habit, and nineteen years of being unable to tell
whether it's done me any good."

"Then why are you showing me?"

"Because of what you've just said. I had the same complaint at forty-four.
I'd watched the best people in a dozen trades and I hadn't the first idea
what it was like to be one of them. I couldn't aim at it. You can't specify
a thing you've never been." He touched the card with the boat. "This
doesn't tell you what the room is like from inside. Nothing can; you'll
know when you're in it. What I think it does, and I've no right to more
than 'think', is let you have stood there. Often. So that it stops being a
wall you feel along and becomes somewhere you've been. You don't have to
understand a place to be used to it."

"And if it does nothing?"

"Then it costs you ten minutes a morning and my good opinion of myself."

She almost laughed.

"I'll tell you the rest, since you've come this far." He sat back.
"Everything that's now in the textbook was once in somebody's bottom
drawer. Most of what is in bottom drawers is rubbish. Both of those are
true, and nobody can tell you in advance which this is."

He squared two of the cards that did not need squaring.

"If you go looking beyond where the evidence stops, you'll be wrong most of the time, and
people you respect will laugh at you, and they will be right to ask what
you think you're doing. You'd have to decide whether you can live with
that. I've only half managed it. You've seen where I keep them."

Maren looked at the twenty-three cards for some time.

The old Maren would have gone home and read about it for a month. The one
from Wednesday the eleventh would have started that night and told Pri it
was working. She found she was neither, quite. She was thinking about
envelopes.

"If I do it," she said, "I do it the way I'd do anything else now. It can't
be scored. Fine. But I can fix what it is. Ten minutes, first thing, before
the hour, the twenty I've got. The same every day, so I'm not fiddling with
it."

"Go on."

"And I can write down the same things each morning. Did I do it. Could I
get through all twenty without looking. How fast, in my own words. That's
only me reporting on me. But it's the same questions every day."

"And what would count against it?"

"That's the hard one." She worked at it. "It isn't going to show up on
Matthias's sheet in a way I could separate from the drill. But there's one
thing in my log I can count. Since I changed the exercise I've done the
hard one four days out of seven. The other three I found a reason. If
standing in the room every morning does what you /think/, I ought to dodge
it less. So: by the twelfth, six days in seven. I'd say I'm fifty percent
sure. And if nothing in my log looks any different from the three weeks
before, I write down that it did nothing I can see, and I say so to you."

Keane had gone very still.

"That is more than I have done in nineteen years," he said.

There was a knock, and the door opened on it.

"Ambrose, I need the review file before I — oh." Dr. Albrecht stopped. She
looked at the closed door she had just opened, at Maren, and at the table.
"What are those?"

Nobody spoke. She came nearer and read one. Then another.

"Ambrose. What are these?"

He told her. He did it in four sentences, standing up, and he left nothing
out, including the nineteen years.

Dr. Albrecht's face did something complicated. It was not contempt. It
would have been easier if it had been.

"You have a review in April," she said. "My name is on four of your papers.
And you're giving /this/ to someone from my study?"

"Your study finished a week ago. And I haven't given it. I've shown it."

"I locked a prediction in my desk for her last Friday because I thought
this was a laboratory." She turned to Maren, and her voice dropped. "It's
not you. Your check is the best thing I've seen in this building all year
and you'll have your envelope on Thursday; I keep my word. But I can't be
anywhere near this. I can't." At the door she stopped, with her back to
them. "I don't even know that it's nonsense. That's what I can't forgive
you for. You've had nineteen years to find out."

The door closed.

"That's the price," said Keane, after a while. He began to gather the
cards. His hands were not quite steady. "I did tell you there was one."

She told Pri on Saturday, because she had learned something about telling
people.

"You're adding /what/?"

"Ten minutes in the morning. Running through some pictures. There's no
evidence for it. The other fifty minutes are the drill, and on Thursday
there's a number that somebody else gives me."

"I told Dunmore you were training with a university laboratory." Pri put
down her coffee. "Maren. That's wishing. That's what my aunt does with a
candle."

"It might be."

"And you're doing it anyway."

"For twelve days. With a note of what would show it's nothing."

Pri looked at her for a long moment, shook her head, and picked the coffee
up again.

"Thursday," she said. "Thursday is the only thing I'm listening to."

* What Maren has just learned

Maren cannot imagine being the person who can do what she is practising
for, and Keane's answer is a practice he has kept secret for nineteen years
and for which, he says plainly, there is no evidence at all. She does not
decide whether it works. She fixes what it is, what she will report each
day, and what countable thing would show it had done nothing, before she
starts. Being seen to do it costs Keane a colleague's trust.

* Your practice — Step 13: Give the unscorable a routine

/Twenty minutes./

This step is for any practice that produces no score: one you cannot be
tested on, or one from a field with no teachers and no studies. You may have
none. If so, read the step and keep it for the day you do.

1. Write what the practice is, exactly: what you do, in what order, for how
   long, when. Fix it so that you are not adjusting it from day to day.
2. Write what is known about it. If the honest answer is "nothing", write
   /nothing/. Do not borrow evidence from something that merely resembles
   it.
3. Choose three or four things you will report after each session, the same
   ones every time, in your own words. This is you reporting on you. It is
   weak, and it is better than a general impression.
4. Find one countable thing in your existing log that the practice might
   plausibly change if it did what its supporters believe. Write what you
   expect that number to do, by a date, and how sure you are.
5. Write the result that would make you say: /this did nothing I can see./
6. Seal it as you did in step 11.
7. Give the practice a small share of your time. The scored exercise keeps
   the rest.
8. Decide who you will tell, and tell them what it is and what is known.

The uncomfortable part is not the practice. It is doing something you cannot
defend to a person whose judgment you respect, and refusing to defend it
harder than the evidence allows. Say "I don't know" as often as it is true.

If one of your twenty scenes from step 3 is the scene of your skill, it can
do two jobs: it is one of the pictures, and it is the thing your check in
step 10 looks for.

* The same step in Org Reason

1. Press ~C-c r s~. At the component skill, name the practice. At the
   challenge, write the fixed routine. At the feedback question, write the
   three or four things you will report and, plainly, /self-report; no
   evidence/ if that is the case.
2. Press ~C-c r p~ and draft the prediction about the countable thing, with
   the result that would mean it did nothing, and the date.
3. Press ~C-c r f~ to freeze it, with your probability.
4. Each day, make a new practice entry and fill in the same report fields.
   Do not add new ones halfway.

- [ ] One practice entry describes a fixed routine and says what is known
  about it, and one frozen prediction names a number in your own log.

*If you keep a trusted system:* this practice is a daily commitment like any
other and wants one next action with a length. Keep it out of your project
list. It has no outcome you can check off.

*If you have worked out what limits you:* an untested practice is a poor
candidate for the one thing you put your effort behind. Give it minutes, not
the hour, until your own record says more.

* Carry forward

A fixed routine, a daily report, and a sealed line saying what would show it
did nothing. Step 14 sorts everything you own by what it would have to
prove.
