* The chapter

Eight out of forty frightened her, and she did what frightened people do.
She worked harder.

Clips first; she had kept to that since the Monday he came back. But one
hour was plainly not enough for a woman who had nine days and had scored
eight. From the Friday of the envelope she got up at half past five and did
an hour before work, and an hour after, and half an hour in the middle of
the day with a sandwich. Two and a half hours of ten-second clips. Sixty,
seventy of them a day. She filled the three columns faithfully.

For the first few days it answered. Sixty-one became sixty-six, became
seventy-one. She had never seen it move so fast.

Then it stopped.

/Tuesday 17: 71./
/Wednesday: 70./
/Thursday: 72./
/Friday: 71./
/Saturday: 71. Sunday: 70. Monday: 71./

By the Tuesday of the following week she had done more than four hundred
clips since the number last changed. She had a permanent small ache behind
her right eye. And on Monday, for the first time in eleven years, Solberg &
Nair had delivered a Kessler manual a day late, because its translator had
fallen asleep over the pneumatics chapter at nine in the evening.

Pri had not said anything about that. She had sent the invoice to the
client herself, with an apology, and put a coffee on Maren's desk.

"I need more time," Maren told Keane. She had come straight from work and
was aware of looking like something left out in the rain. "I've been doing
two and a half hours and it isn't enough. I'm thinking of taking next week
as leave. If I could do five or six hours a day until the fifth —"

"May I see the book?"

She gave him the notebook. He turned back a week and read down the third
column, the one headed /Next time/, with his finger.

"/More clips./ /More clips, earlier./ /More clips./ /Add a lunch session./
/More./" He looked up. "What was different about Thursday's attempt, from
Wednesday's?"

"There were more of them."

"Besides that."

She took the notebook back and looked. Under /Attempt/, for seven days
running, in handwriting that grew steadily worse, was the same line.
/Ten-sec clips, interviews, 3 plays, transcribe./

"Nothing," she said.

"And how many do you get right, of a hundred words?"

"Seventy-one."

"When you started it was fifty-four. Where did I say you wanted to live?"

"Half." She saw it. "It's got too easy. Not easy; I still hate it. But I'm
not missing enough."

"That's part of it." He put his fingertips together. "The other part. On
Thursday, with Frau Reinholt. Where did Matthias say you disappear?"

"In the middle. I get the first thing. Then it goes dark, and sometimes I
get the end. Never the numbers."

"Does a ten-second clip have a middle?"

Maren opened her mouth and shut it.

It did not. A ten-second clip was one sentence. She heard it three times
and wrote it down. She had become rather good at hearing one sentence three
times and writing it down. At no point in four hundred repetitions had she
been asked to hold on to a first sentence while a second and third came in
on top of it, or to keep a figure alive for a minute and a half, or to say
anything in English at all.

"It taught me to hear where the words are," she said slowly. "That was the
first thing. That's why '/haben wir/' sits. But the check isn't failing
there any more. It's failing at — keeping it. And at turning it round and
saying it. And I've never practised either. I've practised the bit I can
now nearly do, for two and a half hours a day."

"What did the forty do, when it stopped moving?"

She knew the paper better than he did by now. "They changed the exercise.
There's a table. Almost nobody added hours past the third year. They made
it longer, or faster, or took away the second hearing." She rubbed her eye.
"I read that. I underlined it. And then I got up at half past five."

"Everyone gets up at half past five. It feels like virtue." He said it
kindly. "What would you change?"

It came quickly, because the check had already told her.

"Longer. Thirty seconds, not ten, so there's a middle. One hearing, not
three; Herr Brandl won't say it three times. And I don't write down the
German. I say it, in English, out loud, what he meant. Into the telephone,
so I can hear it back."

"And how do you know how you did?"

"The text is still there. But I'm not counting words." She was thinking
aloud. "Points. Like Matthias. Before I listen I could have the assistant
read the text and list the three or four things in it that matter, and not
show me. Then afterwards I look at its list and tick what I got. I check
its list against the text the first few times, in case it's invented one."

"And the figures?"

That was the one she had no answer for. She had tried, at the baseline, to
hold a decimal in her head, and it had cost her the whole of the next
sentence.

"I don't know. I can't keep them."

"What did Frau Reinholt do for thirty-four years, when a witness said a
number?"

"She wrote it —" Maren stared at him. "She wrote it /down/. Interpreters
take notes. It's in your paper; there's a whole section. I was trying to do
it with my hands empty because I thought that was the skill."

"Some of them think that for a year or two."

"A pad. Figures only. The moment I hear one, it goes on the pad and I stop
carrying it."

She tried it that evening, at home, for one hour and not a minute more.

The first thirty-second clip was like stepping off a kerb she had not seen.
One hearing. A woman from a transport ministry, three sentences, two
figures. Maren got the first figure onto the pad and lost the sentence
around it. She pressed record and said, in English, to her kitchen, a
halting and partly invented account of what the woman from the ministry
thought about rail freight. Then she looked at the assistant's four points.

One of four.

The second: two of four. The third: one. She had forgotten what it was like
to be this bad at it. By the end of the hour she had done eighteen and her
tally stood at twenty-five points of seventy-two, which was thirty-five
percent, and she was not tired in the way she had been tired for a week.
She was tired the way she had been on the first Saturday.

In the third column she wrote: /Figures onto the pad without looking down.
Practise that alone for ten minutes first./

It was the first time in eight days that the line said anything but /more/.

She set the alarm for seven, and slept.

* What Maren has just learned

When her number stopped at seventy-one, Maren added hours, and four hundred
more repetitions changed nothing. The exercise had done its work and had
become too easy, and it had never contained what the check was failing on:
a middle to hold, a single hearing, speaking in English, and figures. She
changes the exercise so that she is failing at about half again, lets a pad
carry the numbers, and goes back to one hour.

* Your practice — Step 12: Change the exercise

/Fifteen minutes, when your number has not moved for five sessions./

1. Read down your third column for the last week. If it says the same thing
   each day, or says only "more", the exercise has stopped teaching you.
2. Check your rate. If you now get most attempts right, it is too easy,
   however much you dislike doing it.
3. Go back to the first run of your check. Write down exactly where you
   failed there.
4. Ask of each failure: is this anywhere in my exercise? Mark the ones that
   are not.
5. Change the exercise to bring one of those in. Change one or two things:
   - longer, so there is a middle;
   - once through, with no second try;
   - the real output, not an easier one;
   - real speed or real conditions.
6. Ask what the people who do this for a living allow themselves that you
   have been doing without: notes, a tool, a routine. Use it.
7. Try five. Adjust until you are getting about half again.
8. Keep your hours where they were.

It will feel like going backwards, because your number drops to where it
was on the first day. Write down the old number and the date beside the
word /done/. That exercise worked. This is the next one.
