* The chapter

The German Society on Aldine Street had a front room with a long table, a
piano nobody played, and on Thursdays at six a cake.

Maren had passed the building for years. She had the card in her coat
pocket, and nothing to write with, as instructed, and she had rehearsed on
the bus a sentence introducing herself that was grammatically beyond
reproach.

She did not get to use it. The woman at the head of the table, small,
upright, seventy or so, with white hair cut like a helmet, looked up as she
came in and said something that began with /Ah/ and contained, Maren was
almost sure, the word /willkommen/. The rest went past like a tram.

“/Entschuldigung/,” Maren said. “/Ich bin/ —”

The woman said something else, pointed to an empty chair with a cake fork,
and turned back to the man on her left, to whom she had evidently been
speaking and with whom she now went on, at the same speed.

There were eight at the table. Professor Keane was the ninth, at the far
end by the piano, with a cup of coffee and the look of a man in a waiting
room. He raised two fingers an inch off the tablecloth in greeting and did
not otherwise move.

Maren sat. Someone passed her a plate.

He had told her one thing on the telephone on Tuesday. /Don’t try to
understand it. For one minute, count the words you catch on your right hand
and the ones you don’t on your left. Roughly. Then stop counting and see
how long you can stay in./

She counted. The woman with the white hair was telling a story, about a
nephew, perhaps, and a workshop, and something that had gone wrong with a
delivery. Maren got /Neffe/. Finger. Then a long run of sound with no edges
in it at all. /Werkstatt/. Finger. Something that might have been /Dienstag/
or might have been the end of a different word running into the next.
Laughter round the table. She had no idea at what.

At the end of what she judged to be a minute her right hand had five
fingers out and her left had been round twice and more. One in five.
Perhaps.

She stopped counting and tried to stay in.

It was like trying to read a page that someone was pulling steadily
upward. She would catch a word, a good solid noun, and by the time she had
it, by the time she had laid it beside the English and felt the small click
of knowing, there were three more gone and a verb arriving from somewhere
behind her that belonged to a sentence she had lost the front of. Twice she
heard, distinctly, the word /Hammer/, and looked for a hammer in the story
and could not find where one would go.

At about four minutes the man beside her asked her something directly. She
knew it was a question because his voice went up. Everyone waited.

“/Bitte?/” she said.

He said it again. It was exactly as fast.

Her eyes stung, suddenly and ridiculously. She was thirty-nine. She had
translated eleven hundred pages for Kessler GmbH, including the hydraulics.

The white-haired woman had seen. She did not slow down, and she did not
switch to English. She pulled a paper napkin toward her, took a pen from
behind the cake stand, wrote for a few seconds in a fast slanting hand, and
slid the napkin up the table.

Maren read it.

It was two lines. She read it the way she read everything, in one pass, and
because the table was waiting she said it aloud in English without thinking
about it at all.

“’If my nephew had measured the shaft before he ordered the bearings, we
would not have had to send the whole delivery back on Tuesday, and I would
not now have twenty-three and a half kilos of the wrong bearings in my
hall.’”

Silence. A man at the end said something admiring that included the word
/Konjunktiv/.

“So,” said the white-haired woman, in English, with the vowels of
somewhere far east. “It is not the German.”

“No.”

“You have a very good German. Better than his.” The cake fork indicated the
nephew’s critic on her left, who bowed. “It lives in your eyes. In your
ears there is nobody home.” She held out a hand across the plates. “Jutta
Reinholt. Thirty-four years I wrote down what people said in the district
court in Dresden, as fast as they said it. You are the one with the
factory.”

“In March.”

“In March they will speak like this.” She had not softened anything, and it
was curiously steadying. “I will not slow down for you. I do not slow down
for anyone; it is not a kindness. Nobody in your factory will slow down.
Better you find it out from me, with cake.”

“What did you say? Before the napkin. I heard ‘hammer’.”

Frau Reinholt frowned, then gave a short bark of a laugh and said it again,
at speed, and then once more as it was written. /Haben wir./ Have we. Said
at the table it had no /b/ in it and almost no /en/: /hamma/. She had said
it twice in the story of the bearings, and Maren, who could have parsed
/haben wir/ in her sleep, had gone looking for a tool.

-----

Keane walked her to the bus stop. It had begun to sleet.

“How long did you last?”

“Four minutes. One word in five, for the minute I counted. I made a fool of
myself.”

“I didn’t see a fool. What do you know now that you didn’t know at five to
six?”

She wanted to say /that I’m worse than I thought/. It was true, and it was
not what he had asked.

“That it isn’t vocabulary. I knew every word on that napkin. And it isn’t
grammar; I had the conditional before I’d finished reading it.” She pulled
her collar up. “It’s — three things, I think. The words don’t come with
gaps between them. ‘/Haben wir/’ turns into something I’ve never seen
written. Then the numbers. Twenty-three and a half: they say the three
before the twenty, and by the time I’d turned it round she was past the
kilos. And the verb. On paper I can see the verb waiting at the end of the
line. In the air I have to hold the whole sentence open until it arrives
and I drop it.”

“That’s a great deal more than ‘processing speed’.”

“It’s still everything.”

“It’s three things. On Monday it was everything.” He stopped under the
shelter. “How many of the six hundred hours told you any of that?”

She did not answer.

“I’m not scoring a point. I’ve a drawer of my own,” he said, a little
obscurely. “I only notice it took four minutes and a napkin.”

Her telephone had been buzzing in her pocket through the last of the cake.
She looked at it while the bus came round the corner.

It was Pri, and it was three messages.

/Dunmore are THRILLED it’s you. Their plant manager says you’re the only
one who ever got the hydraulics chapter right./

/I’ve pencilled Katrin Lohse as a fallback just in case. $4,200 for the
three days. She needs a firm yes or no by Fri 13 Feb, she’s got another
offer./

/But I’ve told them it’s you. So. It’s you! xx/

Maren had said yes on the sixteenth. She remembered it exactly: the morning
after room 311, with the card in her pocket and the scene so clear in her
head that it had felt already half done. /Tell them I’ll do it,/ she had
said, before she had heard one sentence at full speed.

“Bad news?” said Keane.

“They’ve been told.” The bus doors opened. “I told them, really. A week
before I knew what I couldn’t do.”

“Mm,” said Keane. “Well. Now you know three of the things. That’s what you
start from.”

* What Maren has just learned

Four minutes at a real table told Maren what six hundred hours of reading
had not: the difficulty is not vocabulary, grammar or speed in general. It
is three particular things: words that run together, numbers said backwards,
and a verb she has to wait for. She also learns that she said yes to the
client a week before she had tried.

* Your practice — Step 4: Try it today

/The attempt itself, then ten minutes./

Take your scene from step 2 and do the nearest real thing to it that you can
arrange this week. Not a lesson. Not a practice version. The thing itself, at
full difficulty, with real people or real conditions if there are any to be
had.

1. Before you go, write one line: /I expect …/
2. Take nothing to study with. You are not there to learn. You are there to
   find out.
3. For the first minute, count roughly: how much of it can you do, and how
   much goes past?
4. Then stay in as long as you can. Notice the exact moment you fall out,
   and what was happening.
5. Afterwards ask three questions and write the answers.
   - What could I already do that I had assumed I could not?
   - If one thing had been different (slower, written down, a second
     attempt), which change would have let me through?
   - What are the two or three /particular/ things that stopped me?
6. Cross out any answer that is a faculty or a verdict on yourself. Keep the
   particular ones.

This will not be pleasant, and you will want to prepare first. Preparing is
what the box was. Go this week.

If you have promised anyone a result that depends on this skill, look at
when you made the promise, and whether it was before or after you had tried.
