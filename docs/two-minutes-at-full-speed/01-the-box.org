* The chapter

"You know every word they're going to say," Pri said. "You wrote half of
them."

It was the first Monday of January and the office of Solberg & Nair, two
desks above a bicycle shop, smelled of radiator dust. Pri had her coat on.
She had come in only to say this.

"Bernd's retired. Properly, this time; he's moved to his daughter's. Dunmore
Tool have their acceptance audit on the tenth of March. Three days. Kessler
are sending four engineers from Chemnitz to sign off the new line, and
somebody has to stand between them and Dunmore's people and turn German into
English." She spread her hands. "Eleven years you've translated Kessler's
manuals. You know what a /Rundlauftoleranz/ is. Their own sales people don't
know what a /Rundlauftoleranz/ is."

"I can read it," Maren said. "I can't hear it."

"That's the same language."

"It isn't. On paper it stays still." She had tried to explain this before.
Give her a page and she could work as fast as anyone in the trade. Put her
in a room with two Germans talking and it was a river going past: she would
catch a noun, turn it over, and look up to find them four sentences further
on. "I've never been able to. I'm not quick enough. It's a — processing
thing."

Pri looked at the shelf above Maren's desk, where a book called /The
Plastic Mind/ leaned against one called /Faster Thought/.

"You've got ten weeks," she said, "and you are the only person I know who
reads about this for fun. Dunmore is sixty thousand dollars a year. Tell me
by Friday whether I'm ringing round for a freelancer."

Maren said that she would need to prepare properly.

She meant it. That was the trouble. On Monday night she began to prepare,
which was to say that she opened a browser and typed /best method listening
comprehension adults evidence/, and at one in the morning she was reading a
discussion of whether working memory could be trained and whether the
training reached anything else. On Tuesday night she compared three schools
of thought on how interpreters should practise and found that each regarded
the other two as a waste of time. On Wednesday she watched forty minutes of
a lecture on neural plasticity, at double speed, and bookmarked a study that
said juggling thickened some part of the brain she could no longer name by
morning.

It was all promising. That was what kept her there. Every page held out the
same thing in a different coat: /people who did this for eight weeks
improved by so much/. The more of it she found, the more there seemed to be,
and the less possible it became to choose. If the working-memory people were
right she should start there, underneath everything, and the German would
follow. If they were wrong she would have spent three of her ten weeks on a
game.

And under that sat a second difficulty, which she liked even less. Suppose
she did choose. Suppose she sat down tomorrow and did something for an
hour. How would she know, at the end of the hour, whether she was any
nearer? Nobody would be there to tell her. She might do it wrongly for a
month.

On Thursday she had seventeen tabs open and had practised nothing.

One of the tabs was a paper she had printed years ago and written all over.
/How Interpreters Are Made: Training Histories of Forty Professionals/, by
A. Keane, of the Performance Laboratory at the university across the river.
It was the least exciting thing she had ever read on the subject and the
only one she had read twice. He had simply asked forty working interpreters
what they had done, hour by hour, to get good.

She wrote to him at midnight. She kept it short, for her: four paragraphs.
Who she was, what was being asked of her, the ten weeks. Then the question.
/Given the present state of the evidence, which single method would you
recommend?/

The answer was in her inbox at ten to seven on Friday.

/Dear Ms. Solberg. Thursday, 4:15, room 311. Bring everything you have
collected on the subject of improving your mind. All of it, in a box. Count
it first. A. K./

She read it three times looking for the recommendation.

*

She did the count on Saturday, on the floor of her spare room, because he
had asked and because it was easier than deciding anything.

The box was one that bananas had come in. Into it went the books from the
shelf over her desk and the longer shelf at home: thirty-one. She had read
nine to the end. She stacked the juggling balls on top, three, still in
their net, and leaned the balance board against the side.

The courses were harder to put in a box. She went through four years of
receipts in her email and wrote each on an index card. A memory course with
a palace in its logo. A speed-reading course. Two on language learning, one
of which she had finished the introduction to. /Train Your Working Memory in
20 Days./ A chess-visualization course, bought the week after she read about
a grandmaster who played forty boards blindfold. Nine cards. She added up
the prices on the back of the last one and then checked the sum, because it
could not be right, and it was. One thousand three hundred and forty
dollars.

Six applications on her telephone. Six more cards.

The articles she had saved to read later lived in a program built for that
purpose. It gave her the total without being asked: two hundred and
fourteen. She printed the list of titles, eleven pages, and laid it on top.

Then, since he had said /count/, and since by now she wanted to know, she
went looking for the other number.

The memory-game application kept a calendar of the days she had used it.
She scrolled back through three years of grey squares. Here and there a
green one. Two together. A long grey stretch. Three together, in a January.
The longest green run in the whole calendar belonged to a week in March two
years ago, and she remembered it, the feeling of having finally started.

Four days.

She checked the others. The language application: three. The juggling had
no calendar, but the balls were still in their net.

She sat back against the spare bed. Two hundred and fourteen articles.
Thirty-one books. Nine courses. Six applications, three balls and a board.
Thirteen hundred dollars and, if she was honest about the evenings, some
hundreds of hours, every one of them spent finding out how.

Four days.

It was not that she had been lazy. She would have liked it to be that; lazy
was simple. She had worked at this. She had read with a pencil. She could
have given a decent lecture on any of it. She had done everything a careful
person does before beginning, over and over, and had never once got to the
end of before.

Her telephone buzzed on the carpet. Pri.

/Dunmore just asked who's interpreting in March. I said I'd confirm next
week. No pressure. (Some pressure.)/

Maren looked at the box. On top of the printed list was the article with
the highest number of stars beside it, saved eighteen months ago, unread. It
was about a method she had not tried.

She picked it up and read the first page before she noticed what she was
doing.

* What Maren has just learned

Maren has spent years, and thirteen hundred dollars, finding out how to
improve, and the longest she has practised anything is four days. Until she
counted she would have said she was preparing. The count does not tell her
what to do. It tells her what she has been doing instead.

* Your practice — Step 1: Count what you have collected

/Fifteen minutes./

Choose one thing you have wanted to get better at for a long time: a
language, an instrument, a sport, a kind of thinking. You will work on it
through all sixteen steps.

1. Gather everything you have collected about /how/ to get better at it:
   books, courses, saved articles, videos, applications, equipment. Put what
   can be put in a box in a box. Write the rest on cards.
2. Count each kind. Add up what you paid.
3. Estimate the hours you have spent reading, watching and choosing. Be
   fair, not harsh.
4. Now find the other number: the longest unbroken run of days on which you
   actually practised. Look for a record; do not trust your memory, which
   will be generous.
5. Write the two side by side on one card: hours finding out, days doing.

Do not decide anything yet, and do not throw anything away. If you notice
yourself reading something from the box while you count it, write that on
the card too.

* The same step in Org Reason

The box is an inbox that was never emptied. You are not going to empty it
now; you are going to record that it exists.

1. Press ~C-c r c~ and capture one line: your two numbers, with today's
   date.
2. Press ~C-c r i~, put point on that capture, press ~C-c r x~ and clarify it
   as =observation=. It is kept as a note, not a task.
3. If any item in the box is something you have actually promised someone,
   capture that one separately with ~C-c r c~ and leave it in the inbox.

- [ ] One observation note holds your count. Nothing from the box has
  become a task.

*If you keep a trusted system:* look at your Someday list (~C-c r a~). Many
of the things in the box are already there under other names, as projects
you have never begun. Leave them. Step 14 sorts them.
