* The chapter

He had given her a second card with the rules on it, four lines in the same
upright hand, and she propped it against the lamp on Friday evening and
looked at it with something close to irritation.

/Things you want. For yourself./
/Each as a scene in which you already have it. The end, not the way there./
/Somewhere you can stand: what you see, hear, have in your hands./
/No order. No importance. Not a plan./

Nineteen. It did not seem many, for a woman with two hundred and fourteen
saved articles.

She wrote the first three in as many minutes.

/Be fluent in spoken German./
/Have a reliable memory for names./
/Be able to concentrate for two hours without checking anything./

Then she looked at the rules again, and at the card from Thursday with the
mezzanine room on it, and saw that she had written three faculties. She
could not stand anywhere in /a reliable memory for names/.

It took her until ten o’clock to get one that would do, and it came from
nowhere near the box.

/The kitchen at my mother’s. The blue airmail letters from Bestemor are on
the table. I am reading one aloud, in Norwegian, and my mother is laughing
at the part about the goat./

She had not known she wanted that. The letters were in a shoebox at her
mother’s house; her grandmother had been dead eleven years; Maren’s
Norwegian was a child’s, forty words and a lullaby. She looked at the card
for some time.

After that they came unevenly, some of them faster than she liked.

/A table outside a café in Lisbon. I have been here six days alone. I have
a book and there is nowhere I am supposed to be./

/My nephew’s kitchen table. He has the chessboard; I have my back to it. I
say “knight takes e5” and he says a word his mother doesn’t allow./

/The lake at my mother’s, the far side. I am standing in the shallows
getting my breath and the dock I swam from is very small./

/The red wool coat in the window on Garnet Street. I am wearing it. It is
snowing a little./

She stopped over that one. It was a coat. It cost more than she would spend
and she had walked past it since October. The rules said /things you want,
for yourself/, and had not said anything about deserving them, and she left
it in.

By Saturday afternoon she had fourteen and was stuck. She walked round the
block. She had assumed that what she wanted from life was more or less the
contents of the banana box, improved; that there was a better Maren at the
end of all that reading, and that the scenes would be photographs of her.
But the better Maren would not turn into a place. Every time she tried to
picture /sharper/ she got a woman at a desk, looking pleased, with nothing
in front of her.

The fifteenth was the office with a third desk in it and someone at the
desk. The sixteenth was a dinner at which she told a story in German and
Pri, who had no German, laughed anyway because the Germans did. The
seventeenth she was not going to show anyone.

On Sunday morning, with nineteen cards in a row on the carpet and one to
find, she caught herself doing what she always did.

She had sorted them. She had not decided to; her hands had. The ones that
would cost money were on the left. The ones that needed a skill were in the
middle, and she had put them in order of how long each skill would take.
She had a pencil and was writing /6 mo?/ on the corner of the chess card.
She was halfway to a five-year program with phases.

/No order. No importance. Not a plan./

She rubbed out the pencil. She shuffled the nineteen, properly, like a
deck, and laid them out again face up in whatever order they fell. The coat
was next to the letters. Lisbon was next to the mezzanine room at Dunmore
Tool. None of them was first.

It was oddly restful. For years the things she wanted had come attached to
the question of how, and the how had always been so large and so uncertain
that the wanting itself had started to feel like a debt. Here were twenty
things she wanted, and nobody was asking.

The twentieth, when it came, was three juggling balls going round in a park
while her nephew, aged six, shrieked.

She did look, afterwards. She could not help it, and she thought he had
probably meant her to. She went along the row with the box beside her.

Five of the twenty needed her to follow speech: the mezzanine, the dinner,
a telephone call she had written about, a film without the words along the
bottom, an argument with Jutta from the German Society, whom she had never
met and had invented on the strength of a notice on the Society’s door.
Three needed a different skill each. Three needed money and nothing else.
Seven needed nothing she had ever collected or read about. They needed a
week off, or a train ticket, or for her to ask her mother where the shoebox
was.

And /sharper/ was not on the carpet at all. Not one scene required a better
working memory. Several required that she be able to do some definite,
ordinary thing she could not do yet.

-----

She took them to room 311 on Monday morning before work, in an envelope,
meaning to leave them in his pigeonhole. The door was ajar. She knocked on
the frame and went in before she had quite been answered.

He was at the window with his back to her, waiting for the kettle. He had a
small stack of index cards in his left hand, older than hers, soft at the
corners, and he was going through them with his thumb.

He was not reading them. No one could have. They went past as a dealer
riffles a deck, the whole stack in a second or two, and again, and again,
and his head was slightly tilted and his eyes were on the car park and not
on anything in it. It was so quick and so absorbed that she thought for a
moment of a man telling beads.

The kettle clicked. He saw her in the glass, and his thumb stopped. He
squared the stack without hurry, opened the bottom drawer of the filing
cabinet, and put it in.

“Ms. Solberg. You’re early by three days.”

“I brought the nineteen.” She held out the envelope. She did not ask. It
was plainly not her business, and she was fairly sure she had seen
something private. “I didn’t want to lose my nerve about one or two of
them.”

He did not take the envelope. “They’re not for me.”

“You asked me to bring them.”

“I asked you to write them. I wanted to know whether you could.” He poured
water into a mug. “And I wanted you to see how much of what you want needs
anything in that box. Did you look?”

“Seven don’t need any of it. Five need the same one thing.” She hesitated.
“Being cleverer isn’t on any of them.”

“No. It rarely is.” He sounded neither pleased nor surprised. “Keep them.
Not in the box; somewhere of their own. And don’t let them turn into a
schedule. You’ll have had a go at that already.”

“On Sunday. In pencil.”

“Good. You rubbed it out.” He dipped the teabag twice. “They may be useful
again later, for something else. I’m not sure yet whether I’ll suggest it.”

“What sort of something?”

He seemed to consider several answers.

“Thursday,” he said. “Six o’clock, the German Society on Aldine Street.
There’s a table. Bring your first card and nothing to write with.”

* What Maren has just learned

Maren's first three wishes are faculties, and she cannot stand anywhere in
them. Twenty scenes take her a weekend. Seven need nothing she ever
collected, five need one skill, and "sharper" appears in none. When she
catches herself ranking and scheduling them, she shuffles the cards. She
does not yet know what else the list might be for.

* Your practice — Step 3: Write the wider list

/An hour, in two or three sittings./

You have one scene. Now write at least nineteen more. They need not concern
the skill you chose.

1. Each is something you want for yourself. Not what would be good for the
   world, and not what you ought to want.
2. Each is a scene in which you already have it: a place you can stand,
   with what you see, hear and hold. Write the end, never the way there.
   /Sitting on the boat/, not /saving for the boat/.
3. Mix large and small. A coat can sit beside a life's ambition.
4. If another person is in a scene, it should be one they would be glad to
   be in.
5. Keep going to twenty. The last five are the hardest and often the most
   honest.
6. When you find you have sorted them, ranked them or begun adding dates,
   stop. Shuffle them. They are not a plan and have no order.

Then, once, look along the row. Count how many need the skill you chose in
step 1, how many need some other definite skill, and how many need nothing
you have ever collected. Notice whether "being better in general" is in any
of them.

Keep the list somewhere of its own, away from your plans and your box.

* The same step in Org Reason

The list should exist as one note that is not a task, a project or a
someday item.

1. Type the twenty scenes into any buffer, select them, and press ~C-c r c~.
   The region is captured as written.
2. Press ~C-c r i~, put point on the capture, press ~C-c r x~ and clarify it
   as =reference=.
3. Do not give any scene a date, a context or a next action.

- [ ] One reference note holds all twenty, and ~C-c r a~ shows nothing new.

*If you keep a trusted system:* this is the hard part for you. Every one of
these looks like a Someday item or the outcome of a project, and you will
want to file it. Don't, for now. A project list asks "what is the next
action?" of everything on it, and for this list that question is the wrong
one. If a scene already matches a project you have, leave both where they
are.

* Carry forward

Twenty scenes on cards or in one note, in no order, kept apart. Step 4 takes
the first one out into the world.
