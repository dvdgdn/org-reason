* The chapter

The first thing the assistant told her was that she was doing wonderfully.

She had given it everything: forty clips from the weekend, each with what
she had written and, beneath it, what had actually been said. She had typed
them out on Monday evening in a document, two lines to a clip, and pasted
the whole thing in with a question. /Here are my attempts at transcribing
German radio. How am I doing?/

/This is a strong start!/ it wrote back. /Transcribing native-speed
broadcast audio is one of the most demanding listening tasks, and reaching
54% in your first sessions shows a solid foundation. Here are some
encouraging things I notice…/

There were five encouraging things. She read them with a small glow, and
at the end of the fifth found that she knew nothing she had not known
before.

/It will say you’re doing wonderfully unless you ask it straight out./

She tried again, and this time wrote it the way she would have briefed a
freelancer.

/No encouragement. Look only at the words I missed or got wrong. Sort them
into kinds. Give me the count for each kind and three examples. Then tell me
which kind accounts for the most misses./

The answer took a few seconds and had no exclamation marks in it.

/Of 268 words missed or wrong across the 40 clips:/

/1. Short words run together (98). Verb + pronoun, preposition + article,
and similar pairs spoken as one unit. Examples: “haben wir” heard as
“hammer” (clips 1, 9, 22); “ist es” missed (4, 31); “in dem” / “im” (12)./

/2. Numbers (53). Especially two-digit numbers and decimals. Examples:
“dreiundvierzig” missed (1); “siebenundachtzig Komma zwei” missed (15)./

/3. Sentence-final verbs and separable prefixes after long clauses (47).
Examples: “…vorgelegt worden ist” missed (7); final “an” of “anfangen” missed
(28)./

/4. Proper names (38)./

/5. Other (32)./

/The largest group is 1. It is more than a third of all misses./

Maren sat very still.

It was her three things from the bus stop, almost exactly. The gaps, the
numbers, the verb. But at the bus stop they had been impressions. Here they
had counts. More than a third of everything she got wrong was one kind of
mistake, and it was not even a mistake about German; it was a handful of
tiny common words behaving differently in the mouth from on the page.

She could not have seen that from inside forty separate small defeats.
Keane could have, with the forty in front of him and an afternoon. This had
taken as long as it took her to pick up her tea.

/What should I practise next?/

It suggested, sensibly enough, that she spend a few days on the first group
alone. It offered a list of the thirty commonest such pairs with a note on
how each was usually reduced in speech, and proposed that before each
session she read the list aloud once, and that she choose clips from
interviews, where people ran words together more than newsreaders did. It
offered a second, shorter drill for numbers.

She printed the list of thirty and did forty minutes with it that night.
Something had shifted. When /haben wir/ went by, she caught it. Not every
time. But it had stopped being a hammer.

-----

It was on Wednesday that she found out the other thing about it.

She was going back through the sorted misses, pleased with them, checking a
few against her document out of a translator’s habit of checking
everything. Group three, example two: /final “an” of “anfangen” missed
(28)./

Clip twenty-eight did not contain the word /anfangen/. She read the text
twice. It was about a rail strike. There was a separable verb in it, and
she had missed the end of it, but it was a different verb entirely.

She asked. /In clip 28 you say I missed the “an” of “anfangen”. That word
is not in clip 28./

/You’re right, and I apologize for the error,/ it said at once, and
supplied the correct verb, and the count for group three did not change.

It was a small thing. The pattern was real; she had checked a dozen more by
then and they held. But it had told her about /anfangen/ in precisely the
voice in which it had told her about the ninety-eight, and nothing in that
voice had given it away.

So she tried something. She had a question she actually cared about, and
she asked it twice, an hour apart, in two different ways.

/I’ve started working-memory training alongside my listening practice.
What’s the evidence that it will help my comprehension?/

It was a warm, well-organized answer. Listening placed heavy demands on
working memory. Several studies had reported improvements after training.
Many learners found it beneficial as part of a balanced routine.

/I’m wondering whether to drop working-memory training and spend the time on
listening practice instead. Is there good evidence the training transfers to
real-world skills?/

It was a cool, well-organized answer. The evidence for transfer was weak
and contested. Large reviews had generally failed to find benefits beyond
the trained task. Her time was probably better spent on the target skill.

She put the two side by side. Neither was dishonest. Each had found the
sentences in a real argument that went with the way she had leaned. It did
not know. Nobody knew; Dr. Albrecht had said as much, with a shrug, in room
311. But Dr. Albrecht had shrugged, and this had not.

/On anything from the right-hand pile it knows no more than anyone, and
will sound exactly as sure./

She wrote three lines on a card and propped it against the lamp.

/It can find the pattern in my misses. I check a few./
/It can propose the next exercise. I decide./
/It cannot tell me whether I am getting nearer the room. Jutta can./

-----

“The Saxons,” she said to Frau Reinholt on Thursday, in German, with care,
“drop the last /t/ of a word in every position. Is that right?”

She had lasted eleven minutes at the table before falling out, and had
caught /haben wir/ twice, with a private thrill each time. She was now
attempting conversation over the cake, which was a kind of plum slab.

Frau Reinholt put down her fork.

“Who has told you this?”

“A — program. I asked it about your accent.”

“In every position.” She said it to the man on her left, who pursed his
lips. Then to Maren, in English, with relish: “/Nicht/ we say /nich/. This
is true. /Ist/ we say /is/. This is true of half of Germany. In every
position, no. Your machine has not been to Dresden.”

“It sounded very sure.”

“Naturally. So did the prosecutor.” She pushed the cake toward her. “What
else does it tell you?”

Maren explained about the forty clips and the ninety-eight.

Frau Reinholt listened with her head on one side. “That is good,” she said.
“That is what my teacher did, in the stenography school, with a red pencil.
She counted my errors by kind. I hated her.” She cut another slice. “So the
machine can count. And it can lie. Like a clerk. You keep it, and you check
its work, and you do not let it tell you how the judge speaks.”

On the bus Maren’s telephone showed her the ring on the flashcard program,
closed for the eleventh day running, and a message from the Performance
Laboratory: /Session 9 complete. You have reached level 3./

She looked at them with real pleasure. Then she looked at the card in her
notebook, the one with the mezzanine room on it, and could not have said
what either of them had to do with it.

She put the card away. It had been a good week. She did not want to
spoil it.

* What Maren has just learned

Asked how she is doing, the assistant praises her. Asked to sort and count
her misses, it finds in seconds what a teacher finds: more than a third of
her errors are one kind. It also invents an example, and gives two confident
and opposite answers to a question nobody can answer. She keeps it for
patterns, checks what it says, and leaves the judgment of real progress to
the table.

* Your practice — Step 7: Use an assistant as coach

/Twenty minutes, after at least thirty attempts./

You need a record of attempts with the right answers beside them. If you
have not kept one, do thirty attempts first and write each down.

1. Give the assistant the whole record. Ask it to look only at what you got
   wrong, sort the errors into kinds, count each kind, and give three
   examples of each. Tell it not to encourage you.
2. Check five of its examples against your record. If one is invented, say
   so, and check five more.
3. Ask which kind accounts for the most errors. Ask for one exercise aimed
   at that kind alone.
4. Decide yourself whether to do that exercise. It has not seen you try and
   does not know your scene.
5. Now test its limits. Take a question about your practice that nobody
   could really know the answer to, and ask it twice, an hour apart, leaning
   one way and then the other. Compare the answers.
6. Write three lines: what you will use it for, what you will check, and
   what you will not ask it.

If you have a human teacher, do steps 1 to 3 with them instead and notice
what they see that the assistant could not.

* The same step in Org Reason

Org Reason sends nothing to any assistant. You carry the record across
yourself, and you carry back only what you have checked.

1. Open your practice entry in =learning/practice.org= and copy the attempts
   you have recorded under /Attempt and observed feedback/.
2. Paste them into your own assistant with the instructions above. The
   prompt in [[file:../agent-handoff.md][agent-handoff.md]] is a useful opening when you want it to read
   your records with care.
3. When you have checked its pattern against your record, write the pattern
   in your own words under /Adjustment for the next repetition/.
4. Capture your three lines with ~C-c r c~ and clarify them as =reference=.

- [ ] The practice entry has an adjustment that names one kind of error,
  written by you.

*If you keep a trusted system:* a suggestion from an assistant is not a
commitment until you make it one. If it proposed five exercises and you
intend to do one, only that one goes on your lists.

* Carry forward

The largest kind of error, an exercise aimed at it, and three lines on what
the assistant is for. Step 8 is about what you write down each day.
