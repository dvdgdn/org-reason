* The chapter

On Saturday morning Maren sat down to find a listening course, and had
compared four of them before she heard what she was doing.

She closed the browser. It took some effort, like putting down a drink.

Three things, she had told him at the bus stop. Words without gaps. Numbers
back to front. The verb at the end. She wrote them on a sheet of paper and
looked at them. They were so much smaller than /listening comprehension/
that it was hard to believe they were the same problem, and they did not
tell her what to do at nine o'clock on a Saturday with an hour to spend.

She rang him. He had said she could, until the end of the month, when he
was going away.

"Which of the three would you take first?" Keane said. There was a radio on
behind him and something frying.

"The gaps. If I can't find where the words are, the other two don't
arise."

"Good. What would you do about it this morning?"

"I thought I'd listen to German radio. An hour a day. Immersion."

"What happened at the table when you listened for four minutes?"

"I drowned."

"And if you listen for sixty?"

She saw it. "I drown for sixty."

"You might get something from it. People do, over years. You've got seven
weeks." A pan was moved. "Let me ask it the other way. On Thursday Frau
Reinholt wrote a sentence on a napkin, and then you knew precisely what
you'd missed. '/Haben wir/.' How long did that take?"

"A few seconds."

"Could you have that again? Today, without Frau Reinholt?"

Maren thought. The radio stations put their news bulletins online, with the
text beneath; she had used them for years to check terminology. Sound, and
the words that had been said.

"I could play a piece of the news, write down what I hear, and then look at
the text."

"How big a piece?"

"A bulletin's about two minutes."

"Try it and ring me back."

She tried it. She played two minutes of a newsreader from Hamburg, pen in
hand, and at the end of it had written eleven words, four of them wrong,
and could not have said which part of the two minutes any of them had come
from. It was the table again, without the cake.

She tried one minute. Then thirty seconds, with the same result on a
smaller scale: a few islands, no idea of the sea between.

She cut it to ten seconds mostly out of temper.

Ten seconds was one sentence, or one and a half. She played it. She played
it twice more, since no rule said she couldn't, and wrote down what she
thought had been said. Then she scrolled to the text.

/…haben wir bereits im vergangenen Jahr dreiundvierzig Prozent…/

She had written: /hammer (?) bereits … Jahr … Prozent./

There it was again, the hammer, in a newsreader's mouth this time, cleaner
than Jutta's but unmistakable. And the number gone entirely: forty-three,
said three-and-forty, had gone by as one long word. She counted. Fourteen
words in the clip. She had seven.

She played it once more with the text in front of her, and this time the
sound came apart at the places the text said it should. /Haben-wir./
/Drei-und-vierzig./ She could hear the joins, now that she knew where they
were. It was like being shown the second face in one of those drawings.

She rang him back.

"Ten seconds," she said. "Anything longer and I can't tell what I missed. I
get about half."

"How long did one take? From pressing play to knowing how you'd done."

"Two minutes. Less."

"So in your hour —"

"Twenty-five. Thirty, if I don't stop to feel sorry for myself."

"And do you know at once whether you were right?"

"The text is right there."

"Is half about what you get each time, or was that one lucky?"

She did four more while he held on. It was not comfortable, doing it with
someone listening to her breathe. Six of twelve. Nine of fifteen. Five of
thirteen; that one had a place name in it. Eight of fourteen.

"About half."

"Then I'd leave it exactly there," Keane said. "If you were getting nine in
ten I'd tell you to make it harder, and if you were getting one in ten I'd
say cut it to five seconds. Half is where you want to live. It will feel
unpleasant."

"It does feel unpleasant."

"Yes. I'm sorry. I've never found a way round that." He sounded as if he
had looked. "One thing. Keep the count. Words right, out of words said.
Every clip."

She did twenty more before lunch. It was the hardest hour she had spent on
anything since her final examinations. Each clip was a small defeat,
delivered within two minutes and exactly measured. By the twentieth she had
a headache and a column of fractions that came, when she added it up, to
fifty-four percent.

Fifty-four. She looked at it for a while. It was not a nice number. But it
was the first number in four years that was about the thing itself.

*

On Sunday, to reward herself, she did something easier.

One of the six applications in the banana box was a flashcard program, and
one of the things she had once bought for it was a deck called /The 2,000
Most Common German Words/. It seemed sensible. She could hardly follow
speech if her vocabulary had holes.

She went through a hundred cards in twelve minutes. /Der Vertrag./ The
contract. /Allerdings./ However. /Die Voraussetzung./ The precondition. The
program played a small chime for each right answer. At the end it showed
her a ring, nearly closed, and a figure: ninety-seven percent.

It was delicious. She did a hundred more.

And on Monday at five she went across the river for the first session of
Dr. Albrecht's study. A booth, a screen, headphones. A square lit up in one
of nine places while a voice said a letter, and she had to press a key
whenever the place, or the letter, matched the one two steps back. It was
maddening for three minutes and then, abruptly, it was fun, in the way that
keeping two things in the air is fun. Twenty minutes went in what felt like
five.

"Level two," Dr. Albrecht said, reading the screen. "That's where everyone
starts. You'll be at four in a fortnight; people are. I'll show you your
curve as it grows."

"There's a curve?"

"There's always a curve." She smiled, a little wryly. "That's rather the
problem with it. But yes."

Maren went home on the bus in a better temper than she had been in for a
week. She had an hour a day and now she had three things to put in it. One
of them gave her ninety-seven percent, and one would give her a curve.

The third had given her fifty-four and a headache.

* What Maren has just learned

An hour of listening at full length would have been an hour of drowning.
Cut to ten seconds, written down and compared at once with the text, the
same material becomes an exercise: she fails about half, knows within two
minutes exactly what she missed, and can do twenty-five in an hour. It is
unpleasant. Two other things she starts that weekend are not.

* Your practice — Step 5: Shrink it to one exercise

/Thirty minutes to build; then it is your daily exercise./

Take one of the particular things from step 4. The one the others depend on.

1. Find a source of the real thing: recordings, positions, problems,
   passages, whatever your skill is made of.
2. Cut it down until one attempt takes a minute or two. Keep cutting past
   the point that feels dignified.
3. Arrange to know immediately how you did: the text, the answer, the
   recording of your own attempt beside a good one.
4. Try five. Adjust the size until you get roughly half right. If you get
   nearly all, make it harder. If you get almost none, make it smaller.
5. Decide what you will count. One number per attempt.
6. Do twenty. Write down the total.

The exercise is right when four things are true: it aims at one particular
break; one attempt is short; you know the result at once; and you fail often
enough to learn from it. It will be less pleasant than anything in your box.

If you find yourself looking for a course or a better source of material
instead, stop and do five with what you have.
