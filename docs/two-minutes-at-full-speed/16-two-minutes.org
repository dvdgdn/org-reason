* The chapter

Herr Brandl did not lean back before he spoke. That was the first thing she
had got wrong about him.

He was a small, exact man of fifty in a grey pullover, who talked quickly
and quietly with his eyes on his own papers, and who had answered her
letter within the hour. /Selbstverständlich,/ he had written. Of course he
would pause. On Tuesday, on the walk round the new line, he had paused
after every second sentence and looked at her like a man waiting at a
crossing. By Wednesday afternoon, among the measuring benches, he had begun
to forget.

It was now ten past ten on Thursday the twelfth of March, in the mezzanine
room at Dunmore Tool, with the window onto the shop floor, and he had
forgotten entirely.

Four engineers from Kessler on one side of the table. On the other, Dale
Purcell, Dunmore’s plant manager, a heavy patient man, with his quality
lead and a woman from purchasing. Maren at the end, between them, with a
pad.

She had interrupted people perhaps two hundred times in two days. She had
stopped apologizing for it on Tuesday afternoon. It was, as she had told
Pri on the pavement, how the thing was done, and in pieces she was a great
deal better than twenty-three. But there was a kind of German engineer who,
once he reached the matter he had come about, could no more be stopped than
a lathe.

“/Die Spindel,/” said Herr Brandl, and turned a page.

He spoke for a little under two minutes. She knew, because the clock was on
the wall behind him and some part of her noted the second hand at the start
and at the end, the way one notes the time of an accident.

The first sentence she had whole. A figure, /0,004/, went onto the pad
without her looking. He was explaining how the runout had been measured,
at three positions, cold and then at temperature, and she was not turning
words over. She was listening to a man explain how he had measured a
spindle. It was there again, the transparency, and this time when she
noticed it she did not grab at it. She let it be there, as you let a
skittish animal stay in the room by not looking straight at it.

It went and came back. A second figure. The pad.

And then, in the middle of a long sentence, a small unstressed syllable
that might or might not have been there.

She knew this syllable. She had lost it on the fifth of March, in a back
room, in front of her partner. /Liegt innerhalb/, lies within. Or /liegt
nicht innerhalb/. He had already gone on. He was saying what Kessler
proposed.

He stopped. He looked up. Everyone looked at her.

It was exactly as she had dreaded it for eleven years: a ring of faces, and
a silence, and herself in the middle of it not knowing.

/Next time: when I am not sure of a “not”, I ask. Out loud. In the room./

“/Entschuldigung, Herr Brandl,/” Maren said. “/Eine Rückfrage. Der
Rundlauf: liegt er innerhalb der Toleranz, oder liegt er nicht innerhalb?/”

“/Nicht,/” said Herr Brandl at once, with a slight nod, as to a sensible
question. “/Vier My. Zulässig sind drei./”

She turned to Dale Purcell.

“Herr Brandl says the spindle runout was measured at three positions, cold
and at operating temperature. At temperature it’s four microns. That is
/not/ within tolerance: Kessler’s limit is three. The five microns on your
acceptance sheet is not a figure Kessler ever agreed to; he thinks it came
from the specification for the older line. He’s proposing two things.
Kessler re-seat the spindle bearings at their own cost, here, next week.
And your sheet is corrected to three before anybody signs it this
afternoon.”

She had the pad in front of her with two figures on it and had not needed
to look.

“Five was in the tender,” said the woman from purchasing.

“It was in /our/ tender,” said Dale Purcell. “Did we ever get it back from
them countersigned?” He was already pulling a folder toward him. His
quality lead was saying something about the older line. One of the Kessler
engineers had turned to Herr Brandl and was asking him, in German, low,
whether next week was really possible.

They were arguing about the spindle.

Maren sat with her pencil over the pad and waited to be needed. It was
some seconds before she understood what had just happened, and by then the
woman from purchasing was asking her to put a question to Herr Brandl about
the tender, and she put it.

Nobody was looking at her.

-----

In the afternoon a woman named Sabine Kraus sat down on her left, shook
hands all round, and for three hours did Maren’s job in front of her.

It was like watching someone walk who has never thought about walking. Frau
Kraus did not appear to be doing anything. She listened with her head a
little tilted and made marks on a narrow pad, not words, small signs with
arrows, and when a speaker stopped she gave it back in the other language
entire, in order, at once, with the figures. She took four minutes at a
stretch without visible effort. Twice she asked a speaker to repeat a
number, in exactly the tone Maren had used that morning.

Maren had expected to mind. She found she was fascinated. For years /good
at it/ had been a fog on the far side of a river. Now it was a woman in a
navy jacket, three feet away, and Maren could see how far off she was, and
roughly in which direction.

“You did the morning?” Frau Kraus said afterwards, capping her pen. “The
spindle? Mr. Purcell told me. You asked about the /nicht/.”

“I wasn’t sure of it.”

“Good. The bad ones don’t ask.”

-----

On Sunday she did what she now did on Sundays.

The notebook, the three columns, the week added up. Seven hours: four and a
half on say-it-back, an hour on cards, an hour and ten minutes on the
twenty scenes, and nothing at all on reading about methods, which she noted
with a surprise that was becoming familiar.

She took each of the three in turn and wrote one word beside it.

/Say-it-back. CHANGE. Forty-five seconds now, not thirty; I am getting
two-thirds, which is too many. And workshop talk, not the radio. Ask
Matthias if he would record his aunt./

/Cards from my own misses. CONTINUE. Fifteen new a week is enough./

/The twenty scenes./

She opened the envelope herself; there was nobody to hold this one.
/By 12 March the days a week on which I do the hard exercise will rise from
4 to 6. I am 50% sure. If nothing in my log differs from the three weeks
before, I will say it did nothing I can see./

She counted. Thirteen days of the hard exercise in the last fourteen. Six
and a half a week.

So the line was met. She looked at it without triumph. In those same two
weeks the date had been bearing down on her, and Pri had sat on a folding
chair with her handbag on her knee, and a researcher had turned up at her
house with masking tape. Any of those would have got a person to her desk.
She could not take them out and see what was left.

/Met: 6.5 a week. I cannot separate it from the date getting nearer. Still
untested. CONTINUE to the end of April, ten minutes, no more. New line:
with no date pressing, do the days hold at six? If they fall back to four I
write that it did nothing I can see./

She sealed that one too, and wrote the date across the flap.

Then the cards themselves. One of the twenty was no longer something she
wanted; it had happened, on Thursday, at about twelve minutes past ten. She
took the mezzanine room out of the deck and stood it against the lamp. In
its place she wrote a new one, and it took her a while.

She rang Keane, because she had said that she would tell him.

“Six and a half,” he said. “And you can’t tell.”

“I can’t tell.”

“No. Well. I’ve something to tell you.” He sounded like a man who has
agreed to go up in a balloon. “Ines came into my office on Monday and put
a protocol on my desk. Forty people. Half with scene cards every morning,
half with ten minutes of something dull and matched. One outcome, countable,
fixed beforehand, in a registry. She says she would like to stop being
embarrassed in either direction. She wants it run before my review, and she
wants my name second.”

“Will you?”

“I’m terrified. I’ve had nineteen years of not knowing, and it turns out
I’d grown rather fond of it.” A pause. “Yes.”

There was a message from Wim, sent at 6:04 that morning. /Day 336. Level 7.
21 min. Shop: three of four. Forgot the stamps. She says that’s a pass. I
say it isn’t./

And one from Pri, which had been sitting there since Friday night, and
which Maren had been leaving for when she had the quiet to think about it.

/Dunmore have renewed. Purcell says you saved him forty thousand dollars
with one question. ALSO: Kessler want an interpreter in Chemnitz in June,
second line, three days. They asked for you by name. Do you want it?/

She did not answer at once.

She laid the nineteen cards and the new one out on the carpet, in no order.
Five of them had needed her to follow speech. One of those was propped
against the lamp, done. She could half do the thing now, and she knew how
to get the other half: what to practise, how to tell whether it was
working, how to be wrong about it in a small way on a Thursday and not in a
large one on the day. She could do that, she thought, for almost anything
on this floor. The letters from Bestemor. The chessboard behind her back.

It had taken her four years and a banana box to find out how.

What it had not told her was which. Chemnitz, or her mother’s kitchen. The
third desk, or the lake. She had an hour a day and knew, at last, what an
hour could do, and the cards lay on the carpet in no order, exactly as the
rules said they should, not one of them more important than another.

She picked up the telephone to answer Pri, and put it down again, and went
on looking.

* What Maren has just learned

The scene on Maren's first card happens, and it happens a few seconds after
the thing she feared most: she is not sure, everyone looks at her, and she
asks. On Sunday she decides each practice separately, change, continue,
continue, and records honestly that the one she could not score met its
line for reasons she cannot separate. She knows how to become able to do a
thing. She does not yet know which thing she wants the hour for.

* Your practice — Step 16: Decide, weekly

/Twenty minutes, once a week, from now on./

1. Add up the week's hours by practice, as in step 8.
2. Take each practice in turn. Look at its numbers and at the last check.
   Write one word beside it:
   - *Continue*, if you are failing about half the time and the check is
     moving.
   - *Change*, if the number has stalled or you are getting most right. Say
     what changes.
   - *Stop*, if it met its stop line, or if it has no connection you can
     state to your scene and you need the time.
3. For any practice that cannot be scored, open its sealed line. Write the
   result. Then write, honestly, what else could have produced it. If you
   continue, seal a new line that would tell you more.
4. Look at your twenty scenes. Take out any that has happened, or that you
   no longer want. Write a new one in its place.
5. Set the date of your next check.
6. Tell whoever is relying on you what has changed.

Then one question the method cannot answer for you. You now know how to
become able to do a particular thing, and you have a limited number of
hours. Look along your scenes and ask which one the hours are for. Nothing
in these sixteen steps will tell you. It is the right question to be left
with.

* The same step in Org Reason

1. Press ~C-c r w~ to open this week's review. It keeps earlier weeks'
   answers. In its notes write each practice with its one word and what
   changes.
2. Press ~C-c r d~ and record the outcome of any prediction that is due.
   For a self-reported practice, write in the outcome what else could
   explain the result.
3. For each practice you are continuing with a new sealed line, press
   ~C-c r p~ and ~C-c r f~ again. A stopped practice goes to =someday= with
   ~C-c r C~.
4. Open your reference note of twenty scenes and edit it: remove what has
   happened, add what you now want.
5. Clarify the next check as =calendar= with its date.

- [ ] This week's review names each practice with a decision, no due
  prediction is left unrecorded, and the next check has a date.

*If you keep a trusted system:* this is one more section of your weekly
review, and it belongs there. If you have a project whose outcome was the
scene that has now happened, mark it done. Do not let it quietly become a
different project.

*If you have worked out what limits you:* the question of which scene the
hours are for is the question of what you are trying to achieve and what
most holds it back. That is a different kind of thinking from practice, and
it is where to go next.

* Carry forward

A weekly decision for each practice, a current list of scenes, and the date
of your next check. And the question this work leaves open: of everything
you could now become able to do, which do you want?
