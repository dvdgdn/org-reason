* The chapter

There were more people in the back room of the German Society on Thursday
the fifth of March than Maren had bargained for.

Frau Reinholt at the head of the table with her hands folded on a new sheet
of notes. Matthias beside her, four fresh lists face down. That was the
check. But in the corner by the coat stand sat Dr. Albrecht with an
envelope on her knee, and beside her, upright on a folding chair with her
handbag in her lap like a woman waiting for a verdict, was Pri.

"You said Thursday," Pri said. "I'm here for Thursday."

Keane was not in the room. Maren had seen him through the doorway as she
came in, by the piano in the front, with a cup of coffee and a newspaper he
was not reading. He had lifted two fingers from the tablecloth. It was not,
she understood, his to watch.

She sat down. In front of her she put a pad of paper and a pencil, and
nothing else.

"/Also,/" said Frau Reinholt, and leaned back.

It was about a bearing seat. Maren caught the first sentence, as she always
had. The second sentence came in on top of it with a figure, and her hand
wrote /0,015/ on the pad without her eyes going down, the way it had done
some hundreds of times in her kitchen, and the figure was gone from her
head and safe.

And the third sentence was simply there.

She would try afterwards to describe it to Keane and would not manage it
well. It was not that she was translating faster. For perhaps forty seconds
she was not translating at all. Frau Reinholt was explaining why the
bearing seat was out, that the fixture had been clamped before the casting
had cooled, and Maren was not catching words and turning them over; she was
hearing a woman explain something about a fixture, and understanding her,
as one understands anybody. The German was not in the way. It had gone
transparent.

Then she noticed that it had, and it stopped. The fourth sentence went by
in pieces. She got hold of the thread again near the end.

"He says the bearing seat on the second housing is out of round by fifteen
thousandths of a millimeter. The cause is that the fixture was clamped
before the casting had fully cooled. He —" There had been something here.
"He wants the fixture procedure changed, and the housings already made to
be measured again. By — I didn't get by when."

Matthias's pencil moved seven times.

She did not look at Pri.

The second passage was coolant concentration and she held most of the
middle and lost the end. The third was where it went wrong. It was about a
spindle, the same spindle as two weeks ago for all she knew, and she was
tired by then in the particular way of someone who has been listening with
every muscle. Frau Reinholt gave a figure and a tolerance band, and Maren
wrote both on the pad, and said, when the time came, with some confidence:

"The runout on the spindle is four microns, which is within the permitted
five."

Matthias's pencil did not move. He looked at his aunt.

"/Nicht/," said Frau Reinholt, in the voice of the district court. "/Liegt
nicht innerhalb./ It is /not/ within. The permitted is three. Five is what
Dunmore has written in its own sheet, and this is the whole complaint."

Maren had the four right and the five right. She had lost one syllable,
and turned the meaning inside out.

The fourth passage she did carefully and without joy.

Matthias added it up twice, as before, and turned the lists round.

"Seven. Six. Four. Six. Twenty-three."

Nobody said anything. In the corner there was the sound of an envelope
being opened.

Dr. Albrecht read it out, since it was hers to read.

"'Friday twentieth February. On Thursday fifth March, same form, scored by
Matthias, I will get at least twenty-four of forty. I am thirty percent
sure. If I get twelve or fewer, two weeks of this practice have done nothing
I can measure.'" She lowered the page. "Twenty-three. You've missed your own
guess by one."

Maren waited for it to hurt. She had been waiting for two weeks. What came
instead was something quieter and more useful, and it took her a moment to
recognize it, because she had so seldom had it: she was not surprised.

"I said thirty percent," she said.

"You did. You said you'd probably fall a little short, and you fell a
little short." Dr. Albrecht folded the page. "You knew roughly where you
were. Two weeks ago you were telling your partner you were on track, with a
graph. I'd call that the larger improvement, as it happens."

"Eight to twenty-three," said Matthias. He had been waiting to say it.
"The numbers: I gave eleven, you have nine, with the paper. Last time,
none. And you are in the middle now. You were there, I watched you. What
you lose is here" — the third list — "one /nicht/, and then you were
upset and you lost what came after."

"Twenty-three," said Frau Reinholt. "So. It is more." She stood up. "There
is cake in the front. It is cheese."

*

Pri did not want cake. She wanted to stand on the pavement on Aldine
Street, in the cold, with her car keys in her hand.

"Twenty-three out of forty," she said. "I need you to tell me what that
is. Is it a yes or a no?"

A month ago Maren would have told her what she hoped. Three weeks ago, what
the graph said.

"It's not a yes for three days on my own," she said. "A bit more than half
of what he said got across. That's with one person talking for two minutes
flat out, no pauses, about something technical. And one of the things I
lost was a 'not', and it reversed the sentence. If that had been the
contract, it would have cost Dunmore money."

Pri's jaw tightened.

"But that isn't the whole of it. In the room I don't have to take two
minutes at a time. Nobody does; it's normal to stop a speaker every few
sentences and give it back in pieces. In pieces I'm much better than
twenty-three. Tonight I had the figures nearly all, with the pad. I can do
the walk-round on Tuesday, and the measurements on Wednesday, and most of
Thursday." She made herself finish. "Thursday afternoon is the acceptance
protocol. Signatures. That's where a 'not' matters. For that half day I
want a professional sitting beside me. You said fourteen hundred dollars."

Pri looked at her for some seconds.

"That's the first time since January," she said, "that you've told me what
you can't do before I had to find out." She did the sum aloud, because she
always did. "Fourteen hundred. Against sixty-eight hundred for the three
days. Against forty-two I'd have paid Katrin if you'd said this in
February."

"I didn't know it in February. I thought I did."

"No." Pri opened the car door. "And somebody's got to tell Kessler that the
interpreter's going to keep interrupting their engineers."

"I'll write to Herr Brandl myself. In German. On paper I'm very good."

That got, at last, half a smile.

"Book her?" said Maren.

"I'm booking her."

Keane was in the doorway of the Society with his coat on when the car had
gone. He must have heard the number; everyone in the building had.

"For about forty seconds," Maren said, "in the first passage, I wasn't
translating. It just meant something. And then I noticed and it went."

"Ah," he said.

"Is that what it's like? For Frau Reinholt?"

"I've no idea. I have no German." He turned up his collar. "But that's what
the forty said, more or less. Every one of them. That it stops being
something you do."

She wanted to ask him whether ten minutes of pictures each morning had had
anything to do with it. She had the answer in her own notebook already,
and it was that she could not tell.

At home she wrote it down before she did anything else.

/Thursday 5 March. Check: 23 of 40 (7, 6, 4, 6). Guessed at least 24, 30
percent sure. Missed by one. Baseline was 8./
/Figures 9 of 11, with the pad./
/Lost: one "nicht", passage 3. Then lost my nerve and the rest of the
passage with it./
/Next time: when I am not sure of a "not", I ask. Out loud. In the room./

* What Maren has just learned

The check gives Maren twenty-three of forty, one short of a guess she had
said she would probably miss. The result does not surprise her, and that is
new. It tells her what she can do, what she cannot, and exactly where she
failed: one lost syllable that reversed a meaning. She gives her partner
that, in place of a hope, and asks for help with the part she cannot yet be
trusted with.

* Your practice — Step 15: Run the check

/The check itself, then fifteen minutes./

1. Run the check exactly as you designed it in step 10. Same form as the
   first run, same judge, same count. Do not make it kinder on the day.
2. Let the judge give the number. Do not argue it.
3. Only then have your guess opened and read aloud.
4. Before you do anything else, write down:
   - the number, and the first run's number beside it;
   - your guess, how sure you were, and whether you met it;
   - where exactly you failed, in the judge's words;
   - one line for next time.
5. Ask yourself whether the result surprised you. If it did not, your
   judgment of your own skill has improved, whatever the score. If it did,
   ask what you had been looking at instead.
6. Go to whoever is relying on you and tell them three things: what you can
   now do, what you cannot, and what you need from them for the part you
   cannot. Give them the number.

You may be tempted not to write down a result that disappoints you, or to
remember your guess as lower than it was. That is why someone else was
holding it.
