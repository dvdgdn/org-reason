* The chapter

Keane came back from Arizona with a peeling nose and a canvas bag of
notebooks, and found Maren sitting on the floor of the corridor outside
room 311 at ten to nine on Monday morning.

"Either the building's on fire," he said, "or you've been to the table."

She told him standing up, in the middle of his office, with her coat on.
The curve. Fifteen hundred cards. Wednesday, and what she had said to Pri.
The three hundred. One word in four. Katrin Lohse in Frankfurt.

She had rung Pri on Friday night, as Dr. Albrecht had told her to. It had
been the worst telephone call of her working life, chiefly because Pri had
not shouted. There had been a long quiet, and then Pri had said, /Right.
How bad?/, in the voice she used for clients who had not paid. By Saturday
she had found a colleague of Katrin's who would do the three days at short
notice for six thousand eight hundred dollars, or half a day for fourteen
hundred, and who needed to know by Friday the sixth of March. /Find out by
then whether you can do it,/ Pri had said. /Actually find out. I'm not
asking how you feel./

"So I've had five weeks," Maren finished, "and I've done everything. I
practised every single day. I did the thing your field says, I had numbers,
they all went up. And I'm no better. Everything I ever read said that if
you do the work —" She heard her voice rise and could not stop it. "I did
the work."

Keane had hung up his coat while she talked. He put the kettle on. He did
not look like a man who had been accused of anything.

"What went up?" he said.

"All of it! The game went from two to four. The cards —"

"The game went up on what?"

"On — itself. On the game."

"So you're better at the game."

"Yes."

"Is that in doubt?"

"No. I'm much better at the game."

"Good. The cards."

She saw where it was going and resented it and went there. "The first
deck: on written words I already knew. So nothing went up; it started at
the top. The three hundred: on reading a card I'd seen an hour before."

"And the clips?"

"Fifty-four to sixty-one. On ten seconds of a newsreader, in headphones,
with three plays."

"And the table?"

"One in five to one in four."

"So four things went up, each on itself." He poured water. "You're not no
better. You're better at four things. I'd only ask which of the four is a
room on a mezzanine with Herr Brandl in it."

Maren sat down, finally, in the armchair, with her coat still on.

"None of them."

"Which is nearest?"

"The table. Then the clips. The other two aren't even in the same —" She
stopped. "I knew that. I added the hours up a week ago. I knew it and I
still showed Pri the curve."

"Yes. People do." He brought her a mug she had not asked for. "I've watched
it for thirty-five years and I've done it. A number that goes up is very
hard to argue with, particularly one's own. It isn't a weakness of
character. It's that the score is there every evening and the room is in
March."

"Wim says it's what gets him to the laptop."

"Wim's right. You need something that moves, or you stop." He sat. "So the
trouble isn't that you watched a number. It's which one. The score tells
you about the exercise. What tells you about the room?"

"Going to the room."

"Which you can do once, on the twelfth of March, with the client
watching."

"Then — something as like the room as I can make it. Before."

"Go on. What's in the room?"

She shut her eyes. She had the card by heart; she had run it through her
head on the bus often enough. "Somebody speaks German for two minutes
without stopping. About a tolerance. At full speed. Then I say it in
English, and it has to be right, the figures especially."

"How would you know it was right?"

"I'd — know."

He waited.

"I wouldn't," she admitted. "I'd know how it /felt/. I thought I was doing
well on Thursday for fourteen minutes." She opened her eyes. "Somebody else
has to say. Somebody who has both languages and knows what a spindle is."

"And who speaks?"

There was only one candidate. "Frau Reinholt took down expert witnesses in
a district court for thirty-four years. She could talk about bearings for
two minutes without drawing breath. She did, the first night."

"Would she?"

"If it involved telling me I was wrong, I think she'd pay to."

For the first time that morning he smiled.

They built it on the low table in half an hour, and most of it was hers.
Four passages, each about two minutes, on the kind of thing Herr Brandl
wrote about: Maren had eleven years of his reports and could give Frau
Reinholt the substance of four of them to put in her own words. Spoken once,
at her speed, no repeats. After each, Maren would give it in English. And
the nephew of the bearings, Matthias, who was a toolmaker and had grown up
between Dresden and here, would sit with a list.

"A list of what?" Keane asked.

"What had to get across. Before we start, he and his aunt write down ten
things in each passage that matter. The figure. Which part. What's wrong
with it. What they want done. Ten points. He ticks the ones I get."

"Forty in all."

"Forty."

"Not words."

"Not words. I've had enough of words. I could get every word and lose the
tolerance."

Keane wrote /40/ on a card and looked at it.

"When?"

"Pri needs to know by Friday the sixth. So Thursday the fifth, in place of
the table." She hesitated. "That's seventeen days. If I do it then and it's
bad, there's no time to do anything about it."

"Then do it twice. Once this Thursday, to see where you are. Once on the
fifth."

"This Thursday I'll get nothing."

"Possibly. Then you'll know that nothing is where you start, and on the
fifth you'll have something to set it against. One number by itself is only
a mood."

She wrote it all down. It was strange: she had more numbers at home than
she had ever had in her life, with charts, and for the first time she was
about to get one that she was afraid of.

"Should I stop the game?" she said. "Dr. Albrecht won't tell me to. And the
cards?"

"What would your Thursday number do, if the game were helping?"

"Go up."

"Then on the fifth you'll have some idea. I wouldn't decide today on my
say-so; you've had enough of deciding things because somebody sounded
sure." He tapped the card. "But I'd notice that you have an hour, and that
you've just told me which of the four things is nearest the room."

She thought about it on the stairs, and by the time she reached the street
she had it. Clips first, before anything, the instant she sat down. The
study had five sessions left; she would finish it, since she had said she
would, after the clips and not before. The deck of two thousand she would
not open again.

She texted Pri from the bus stop.

/Check is set. Thurs 5 March, real conditions, someone else scoring. I'll
have a number for you that night and it won't be about how I feel./

The reply was one letter. /K./

It was, Maren thought, entirely fair.

* What Maren has just learned

Each of Maren's four numbers went up, and each measured only the thing it
was taken on. The score that is there every evening tells her about the
exercise. To learn about the room she needs something as like the room as
she can make it, judged by someone else, counted in what matters, on a date,
and done once now so that the later result has something to stand against.

* Your practice — Step 10: Set the real-world check

/Twenty minutes./

1. List every number you currently have about your practice. Beside each,
   write exactly what it was taken on: the task, the conditions, how many
   tries. Do not write what you hope it stands for.
2. Mark the one nearest your scene. Notice how far even that one is.
3. Now design a check that is as like the scene as you can make it.
   - Real material, at real difficulty, once through.
   - Someone else decides how you did. Choose a person who knows the real
     thing and is not inclined to be kind.
   - Count what matters in the scene, not what is easy to count.
   - Fix the date.
4. Arrange to do it twice: once now, however badly, and once on the date.
   The first tells you where you start.
5. Write on a card: /The score tells me about the exercise. The check tells
   me about the scene./

Keep whatever score gets you to sit down each day. A daily number that moves
is worth having. Just know which thing it is a number of.

If something you are doing has a score and no connection you can state to
the check, you need not stop it today. Decide what order you do things in.

*If you keep a trusted system:* if anyone is relying on you for a result,
give them the date of the check and tell them they will have a number that

*If you have worked out what limits you:* the check is also a test of your
belief that this skill is what holds you back. If you pass it and nothing
else changes, the limit was somewhere else.
