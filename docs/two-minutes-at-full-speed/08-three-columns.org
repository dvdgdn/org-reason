* The chapter

The Plasticity Club had nine hundred members and a rule that every post
must contain a number.

Maren had belonged to it for three years without posting one. It was a
forum, attached to nothing, where people who were trying to improve their
minds told each other how it was going: reading speeds, digits remembered,
streaks. She had read it the way she read everything in the box. On Sunday
the eighth of February, with fifteen days of closed rings behind her and a
message from the laboratory that said /level 4/, she finally had something
to put.

/Day 15. Dual memory game: level 2 → 4 in 10 sessions (supervised study).
Vocabulary deck: 1,300 cards reviewed, 97% correct. First time I’ve kept
anything going past four days./

She pressed the button and felt her face go hot, as if she had stood up in
a meeting.

The first reply came within a minute.

/WIM_DG: Level 4 in 10!! That’s quick, that. Took me five weeks. Well done
you. Don’t let anybody take that streak off you, the streak is what gets
you there./

She knew the name. Wim de Groot posted every morning at about six, one line,
always the same form. /Day 301. Level 6. 22 min./ He had been doing it for
most of a year. She wrote back to thank him and, because he had been kind
and it was Sunday, asked what had got him started.

His answer came as a private message and was longer than anything she had
seen him write.

/Stroke, last March. I’m 58. Came home from hospital and I couldn’t button
a shirt and I couldn’t keep hold of what the wife had said to me two minutes
before. The physio does the hands. Nobody does the head, they tell you it’ll
come back or it won’t. So I found the game. First day I couldn’t do level
1. I’m on 6 now. I don’t know what it does, I’m not daft, I’ve read the
arguments. But it’s a number and it went up today. Most mornings that’s the
reason I open the laptop at all. So I say keep your streak./

Maren read it twice. She had been about to feel superior to the ring on the
flashcard program, and now could not.

/That’s a better reason than mine,/ she wrote.

/What’s yours for?/

/German. I have to interpret at an audit in March./

/And how’s the German?/

It was a perfectly natural question. She opened her notebook to answer it.

She had kept the count, as Keane had said. There it was, a column of
fractions down the left of each page, a percentage at the bottom of each
session. Fifty-four. Fifty-one. Fifty-seven. A gap of two days. Fifty-six.
Sixty, the night with the list of thirty pairs. Fifty-eight. A gap of three
days.

The gaps bothered her. She did not remember taking days off; she had done
her hour every day, she was sure of it, it was the whole point. She went to
find out where the hours had gone.

The game was easy: the laboratory’s messages gave the minutes. The
flashcard program kept its own statistics, to the second, with a chart. The
clips she had to reconstruct from the dates in the notebook. And then there
was a fourth thing, which no program had recorded and which she arrived at
by subtraction and then by honesty: three evenings on which she had sat
down to do clips and had instead read, with great interest, about how other
people did clips.

She wrote it out for the week just ended.

/Memory game: 2 h 20./
/Flashcards: 2 h 30./
/Ten-second clips: 1 h 10./
/Reading about methods: 1 h./

Seven hours. She had done her hour every day. And of the seven, the only
one of the four that had anything to do with what happened at Jutta
Reinholt’s table had been given seventy minutes.

It was not that she had decided this. Each evening she had sat down
meaning to start with the clips, and had thought she would just close the
ring first, since it took ten minutes and was pleasant. It never took ten
minutes. Then the game, which the laboratory expected every weekday and which could not be skipped. And then it was late, and she was
tired, and the clips were the one that would make her feel stupid
twenty-five times.

There was a second thing wrong with the notebook, and it took her longer to
see because it was an absence. The column told her how she had done. It did
not tell her what she had done. /Fifty-seven/: on which clips? Missing
what? And what had she decided, at the end of that session, to do
differently in the next? Nothing; she had closed the notebook. Each day’s
practice had begun exactly where the last one began, as though the last one
had never happened. Only the night with the list of thirty had been
different, and that was the night the number had moved.

She thought of Frau Reinholt’s teacher with the red pencil, counting errors
by kind. And of something else. She went and got the paper.

At the back of /How Interpreters Are Made/ was an appendix she had never
done more than glance at: photographs of pages from the practice notebooks
of three of the forty. They were in three languages and four kinds of
handwriting and they all looked the same. Each page was ruled, by hand,
into three columns. One of them had headed hers in German. /Versuch.
Korrektur. Nächstes Mal./

Attempt. Correction. Next time.

Maren turned to a clean page and ruled it.

She did that night’s clips with the three columns open, and it was slower
and odder than she expected. Under /Attempt/ she had to write what she was
actually going to do, which meant deciding: twenty-five clips, interviews,
not news. Under /Correction/, when she had finished, the number went in as
always. Fifty-nine. But there was room beside it now, and the room wanted
filling, so she went back through the misses and tallied them as the
assistant had. The run-together words were down to a quarter. The numbers
were now the biggest group by a distance.

And then the third column, which was the one she had never had. /Next
time./ She sat with the pen over it.

/Numbers first, before anything. Fifteen minutes of nothing but sentences
with two-digit numbers in. Then clips./

It was one line. But it meant that tomorrow’s session already existed. It
had a first thing in it that followed from today.

She wrote to Wim after midnight.

/You asked how the German is. Honestly: I added it up. Of seven hours this
week I spent seventy minutes on it. The rest went on the things that give
me a number I like./

His reply was there in the morning, sent at 6:04.

/Day 302. Level 6. 21 min. — Seventy’s still seventy more than nowt, love.
But I’ll tell you what struck me. You’ve got a thing it’s FOR. Audit, March,
blokes from Germany. I haven’t. I just want my head back and I couldn’t tell
you what that’d look like if it walked in. Mind you don’t waste yours./

She read it on the bus and resolved that from now on the clips would come
first, every evening, before anything else.

After Thursday, she thought. This week was nearly over, and there was the game every weekday, and she was less than a week off a three-week streak on the cards. She would rearrange it properly after
Thursday.

That evening the ring was not quite closed, so she closed it.

* What Maren has just learned

Maren's notebook holds a column of scores and nothing about what she did or
what she would change. When she adds up her week, the one practice that
bears on her scene has had seventy minutes of seven hours. Three columns,
attempt, correction and next time, make one day's practice set up the next.
She sees all of this, and puts off acting on it until after Thursday.

* Your practice — Step 8: Record the attempt

/Five minutes a session; fifteen once a week./

Rule a page into three columns and use it every session from today.

1. *Attempt.* Before you start, write exactly what you are about to do: the
   material, the size, how many. Deciding this is part of the practice.
2. *Correction.* When you finish, write your number. Then use the space
   beside it: which kind of error was most common today?
3. *Next time.* Write one thing you will do differently tomorrow. One line.
   If you cannot think of one, write what you will do first.
4. Tomorrow, read yesterday's third column before you begin.

Once a week, add up the time.

5. List everything you did under the heading of this skill, including
   reading about it. Put the hours beside each.
6. Mark the ones that bear directly on a particular break from step 4.
7. Work out what share of the week the marked ones had.

If the share is small, look at the order in which you do things in a
session. The pleasant thing that takes "ten minutes first" is usually where
the hour goes. Do not promise yourself to fix it next week. Change
tomorrow's first line.

* The same step in Org Reason

A practice entry made with ~C-c r s~ already has the three columns as three
sections: the challenge, the attempt with its feedback, and the adjustment
for the next repetition.

1. Each session, press ~C-c r s~ and make a new entry for the same component
   skill. Type today's challenge. Before you start, read the /Adjustment/
   section of yesterday's entry.
2. When you finish, fill in /Attempt and observed feedback/ with your number
   and the commonest kind of error, and /Adjustment for the next
   repetition/ with one line.
3. If you want the time recorded, start Org's clock on the entry when you
   begin (~C-c C-x C-i~) and stop it when you end (~C-c C-x C-o~). Use it
   only if the total will change what you do.

- [ ] =learning/practice.org= has one entry per session this week, each with
  an adjustment line.

*If you keep a trusted system:* put the weekly adding-up in your weekly
review. Press ~C-c r w~ and add the question to this week's notes: /what
share of my practice time went to the thing that bears on my scene?/

* Carry forward

A three-column log from today, and this week's share. Step 9 is about when
you repeat things.
