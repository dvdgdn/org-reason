* The chapter

She had brought the paper with her. It was the old printout, soft from
handling, with her underlining on page fourteen.

"'Without exception,'" Maren read, "'the forty interpreters had worked for
a period of years under a teacher who selected their exercises, observed
their attempts and corrected them. None had reached professional standard
by practice of their own devising.'" She laid it on the low table. "You
wrote that."

"I did," said Keane.

"Then what am I doing? I cut up the radio news with a pair of headphones.
Nobody selected it. Nobody's observing. I could be drilling a mistake into
myself twenty-five times an hour and I'd never know." It came out faster
than she meant. She had been carrying it since Saturday. "Real practice
needs someone who knows. I don't have someone who knows, and you're leaving
on Saturday."

He turned the printout round and looked at his own paragraph as if it were
a photograph of himself at twenty.

"That's half of what I wrote," he said. "It's the half everyone underlines."

He got up, went to the banana box, which had lived on his filing cabinet
since the fifteenth, and began taking things out and setting them on the
table in two groups. He did it slowly. He seemed to be deciding as he went.

On the left: the book on the memory palace. The card for the speed-reading
course, after a hesitation. The juggling balls. A grammar.

"People have taught these for a long time. A memory palace has been taught
for two thousand years. There are jugglers who can watch you for a minute
and tell you your left hand throws too far forward. For interpreting there
are schools; my forty went to them. Where a field has worked out what the
next exercise is, and has people who can see what you're doing wrong, the
practice you do under them has a name in my trade." He touched the paper.
"That's what page fourteen is about."

On the right: the card that said /Train Your Working Memory in 20 Days/. A
book with a brain on the cover. The card for a course whose title Maren
could no longer explain to herself.

"Nobody knows what this is a lesson in." The working-memory card. "Ines can
tell you how to get better at the game. Nobody alive can tell you what the
game is the first step of. And this" — the mysterious course — "has no
field behind it at all. There isn't anybody to be a bad teacher of it."

He sat down again.

"So for the left-hand pile you're right, and you should find a teacher if
you can, even for an hour. For the right-hand pile there is no teacher to
find. Not at any price. That's the other half."

"And listening to German at speed?"

"Left, I'd have thought. Interpreters are taught." He picked up the
telephone that lay beside her notebook, and put it down again. "What does a
teacher do? Not what are they like. What do they do, that you can't?"

She had her list ready; it was her complaint. "Choose the exercise. Watch
me do it. Tell me what I got wrong. Know what good looks like."

"On Saturday, who chose the exercise?"

"You asked questions until I did."

"You'd have got there. Who told you what you got wrong?"

"The text under the clip."

"How soon?"

"Seconds."

"That's faster than I ever corrected anybody." He seemed struck by it.
"What's missing, then? Of your four."

Maren thought about the column of fractions. "I know I got seven of
fourteen. I don't know /why/ those seven. Whether there's a pattern.
Whether the next clip should be harder or just different. A teacher would
look at forty of my attempts and say, you always miss this kind of thing.
And then give me something for that."

"Yes," said Keane. "That's the part worth paying for."

He was quiet. Out in the corridor someone wheeled a trolley past.

"Ten years ago," he said, "I would have told you that part needs a person,
and that I was the person. I'm not certain it does any more. You have on
that telephone a thing that will read forty of your attempts beside forty
texts and tell you what the misses have in common. It will do it in the
time it takes me to find my glasses."

"The assistant? I use it for glossaries."

"I tried it last term on something I know well. I asked it to plan a month
of practice for a second-year interpreting student, and to mark a set of
her attempts." He rubbed the side of his nose. "It was better than a poor
teacher and not as good as a good one. I didn't enjoy finding that out. I
have spent a career saying page fourteen."

"So I ask it what to do."

"You ask it what your mistakes have in common. You decide what to do." He
held up a hand and counted on it, which was the nearest he came to
lecturing. "It hasn't been in the room; it knows only what you tell it. It
will say you're doing wonderfully, whether you are or not, unless you ask
it straight out what you got wrong. And on anything from the right-hand
pile it knows no more than anyone, and will sound exactly as sure."

"Then what corrects you on the right-hand pile? If there's no teacher and
the assistant's guessing?"

He did not answer at once, and when he did he was looking at the bottom
drawer of the filing cabinet.

"What happens," he said. "You say beforehand what you expect to see. Then
you look, and you don't flatter yourself. It's slow and it's lonely and
it's all there is. We'll come to it." He turned back. "For now you have
three sources of correction. The text, in seconds. That thing, in minutes,
for patterns. And Frau Reinholt on Thursdays, who is the only one of the
three that's real. Most of my forty had one."

"She's once a week."

"Then once a week you'll find out whether the other two are telling you the
truth."

She gathered the paper. She had come with a complaint and was leaving
without it, and without the reassurance she had wanted in its place.

"I should tell you the other numbers," she said at the door, because she
wanted him to hear something good before he went. "I've started a deck of
common words. Ninety-seven percent. And I'm at level two in Dr. Albrecht's
game, and she says four by the time you're back."

"What do the cards correct?"

"My vocabulary."

"Was it vocabulary, at the table?"

"No. But it can't hurt to be safe."

He nodded slowly. He looked as though he might say something, and then as
though he had decided it was not his to say.

"It's your hour," said Keane. "I'm back on the sixteenth. Arizona; I'm
watching flying instructors watch their students. Ines is here if the
building catches fire." He held out his gentle hand. "Keep the count."

* What Maren has just learned

A teacher chooses the exercise, watches, corrects, and knows what good
looks like. Maren already has correction within seconds from the text, and
an assistant can do some of what she lacked: find the pattern in her misses.
For some of what is in her box no teacher exists at all, and there the only
correction is to say what you expect and then look.

* Your practice — Step 6: Name where correction comes from

/Ten minutes./

1. Ask of your skill: is there a field that teaches this, with people who
   can watch an attempt and say what is wrong? If yes, write down how you
   could get one hour with such a person. If no, write /no teacher exists/.
   That matters at step 13.
2. List every source of correction you have for the exercise from step 5:
   - what the material itself tells you (the answer, the text, the result);
   - a comparison you can make (your attempt beside a good one);
   - an assistant that can look across many attempts;
   - a person;
   - the real conditions from step 4.
3. Beside each, write how long after an attempt it reaches you: seconds,
   minutes, a day, a week.
4. Beside each, write what it /cannot/ tell you.
5. Check two things. At least one source should reach you within seconds or
   minutes. At least one should come from real conditions, at least weekly.
   Fill whichever is missing.

Now look at anything else you have started alongside the exercise. For each,
ask what it corrects, and whether that was one of the particular things from
step 4.

2. Under /Feedback source / criterion/, list each source on its own line

4. If the real-conditions source happens on a fixed day, clarify that as

*If you keep a trusted system:* getting an hour with a real teacher is a
project with a first action, usually a message to send. Capture it now. It
is the kind of thing that stays a good intention for years.
