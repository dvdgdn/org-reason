* The chapter

She had carried the banana box home from room 311 on Friday night. It did
not seem right to leave it there.

On Sunday morning it was on the floor of the spare room again, where she
had first counted it, and she was sitting beside it with ten minutes of
pictures and forty minutes of drill already behind her, trying to decide
what to do with the rest of her life’s reading.

She began the old way without noticing. She took things out and made piles
by how promising they were. The memory palace: very promising. Juggling:
there had been that study about the brain. Within five minutes she had six
piles, a feeling of rising excitement, and the conviction that after March
she would do all of it, in sequence, properly this time.

The doorbell rang.

Dr. Albrecht was on the step in a raincoat, holding a roll of masking tape.

“Your address was on the consent form,” she said. “I shouldn’t have used
it. May I come in?”

Maren stood aside.

“I said on Friday I couldn’t be anywhere near it. I meant him and his
cards. I didn’t mean you, and I walked out as though I did.” She looked
tired. “And I lay awake wondering whether you were going to turn up on
Thursday having spent a week on index cards. So I’ve come to be rude about
your methods. It’s the only way I know of being useful.”

“I did the drill for forty minutes this morning. The cards got ten.”

“Ten.” Dr. Albrecht let out a breath. “All right. Good.” She saw the open
door of the spare room, the box, the six piles. “What’s this?”

“Everything I’ve ever collected. I’m sorting it.”

“By what?”

“By how promising it is.”

“Promising according to whom?”

Maren looked at the piles. “The people who wrote the articles.”

“Mm.” Dr. Albrecht got down on the carpet in her raincoat, tore off a long
strip of masking tape and laid it across the floor, and then a second strip
across the first. Four squares. She wrote on the tape with a pen from her
pocket.

Along the top: /A TOOL OR A COUNT CAN SCORE IT/ and /ONLY YOU CAN REPORT
IT/.

Down the side: /SHOWN TO WORK FOR THE THING PRACTISED/ and /WIDER BENEFIT
DISPUTED OR UNTESTED/.

“That’s not everything one would want to know about a method,” she said.
“It’s the two things that decide what it owes you. Pick something up.”

Maren picked up the book on the memory palace.

“Can it be scored?”

“You memorize a list and see how much you get back. Yes.”

“Does it work for the thing practised?”

“For remembering lists, apparently very well. Whether it makes your memory
better for anything else —”

“I didn’t ask that. Top left.” The book went into the first square. “Next.”

The juggling balls. Scored: you could count catches. Shown to work: for
juggling. “And the brain study?” Maren asked.

“Says something changed in the brains of people who learned to juggle. It
doesn’t say they got better at anything except juggling. Top left, if what
you want is to juggle.” The balls joined the book.

The chess-visualization course. Top left, for chess.

Flashcards. Maren hesitated. “The two thousand were useless.”

“The two thousand were words you knew. Cards made from your own misses,
spaced out?”

“Top left.”

Then the card that said /Train Your Working Memory in 20 Days/. Dr.
Albrecht held out her hand for it and placed it herself, in the lower left
square.

“Scored, beautifully. A curve, every day. Works for the thing practised.
And everything it was sold to you for is in that word.” She tapped
/DISPUTED/. “I’ve spent four years down here. So have a lot of people
cleverer than me.”

A speed-reading course followed it down. So did something about brain
waves.

“So what does it owe?” said Maren. “You said that’s what the squares
decide.”

“Top left owes you the least. Do it, and then check you can do the real
thing: recite the list, play the game of chess, follow the German.” She
moved her hand down. “Bottom left is the dangerous one, because the score
/will/ go up. It always does. So it owes you two things before you start: a
check in the real world, and your guess, written down beforehand, of what
the check will show. Otherwise the curve will tell you whatever you’d like
to hear.”

“That’s what I didn’t have in February.”

“No. I had it, in a registry, for the study. You weren’t the study. You
were a person with a client.” She did not labor it.

There were four things left. The three books Maren had put at the bottom of
the box. A pamphlet, bought secondhand, called /The Multiple Mentality
Course/, which promised to teach the reader to do several things at once
with different parts of the mind. And the card for the course whose purpose
she had not been able to remember in January and now could: a method of
describing aloud, with the eyes shut, whatever images arose, which was
supposed to raise one’s intelligence.

She put them in the lower right square without being asked. Then she took
one of her own twenty scene cards from her pocket and laid it on top.

Dr. Albrecht looked at it for a while.

“No score,” she said. “Nobody’s shown it works for anything. So it owes the
most. If you’re going to do it at all, it owes a fixed routine, so you
can’t keep changing what ‘it’ is. The same report every day. And a line,
written first, saying what would make you admit it had done nothing.”

“He said the same.”

“Did he.” It was hard to tell what she felt about that.

“I wrote the line on Friday. Before you came in.” Maren told her: the hard
exercise, four days in seven, six by the twelfth, fifty percent, and what
she would say if the log did not change.

Dr. Albrecht listened with her chin on her fist.

“It’s one person with no comparison,” she said at last. “If your number
goes to six it could be the cards, or the date getting nearer, or me
turning up at your house. You couldn’t tell.”

“I know. That’s why it’s ten minutes and not the hour.”

“That’s —” She stopped. “That’s a better answer than I’d have had from most
of my colleagues, about most things.” She looked at the fourth square, the
upper right, which was empty. “Nothing here.”

“’Shown to work, but only you can tell.’ I couldn’t think of anything.”

“If only you can tell, I don’t see how it would get shown.” She left it
empty.

They sat among the squares. It looked very different from the six piles.
Nothing had been thrown away, and nothing was any longer /promising/. Each
thing had a place and a debt.

“I have one hour,” Maren said. “And ten methods.”

“Then you can’t do them.”

“No.” She had known it since she added up the week in February; she had
needed it laid out on a floor. She took a sheet of paper. /Forty minutes:
say-it-back, thirty seconds, one hearing. Top left; aimed at where the check
fails. Ten minutes: cards from my own misses, spaced. Top left. Ten minutes:
the twenty scenes. Bottom right; routine fixed, line sealed./

Everything else she put back in the box, and wrote on the lid: /Not
rubbish. Not now./

“And my game?” said Dr. Albrecht.

“In the box. Bottom left. I’m sorry.”

“Don’t be. I’d have said so myself if I’d been allowed.”

-----

She wrote to Wim that afternoon. It took four drafts, because he had been
kind to her and it was his game too.

/I’ve stopped the memory game. Not because it’s bad. I had an hour and a
date and it was taking a third of the hour, and nobody can say what it does
for anything else. You told me you just want your head back and couldn’t
say what that would look like if it walked in. I couldn’t either, until
somebody made me name a Tuesday. What’s one Tuesday, for you?/

His answer came at 6:04 on Monday.

/Day 323. Level 7. 20 min. — I’m keeping mine, it gets me up. But I thought
on it all night. Tuesday. Kitchen. The wife says eggs, milk, the blue
cheese and stamps, and I walk to the shop with no list and come back with
all four. Haven’t managed more than two since March./

/That’s a check, Wim. Who scores it?/

/She does. Believe me./

* What Maren has just learned

Sorted by how promising the articles made them sound, Maren's methods
become six exciting piles. Sorted by two plain questions, whether it can be
scored and whether it is shown to work for the thing practised, each one
gets a place and a debt. The square where the score rises and the wider
benefit is unknown owes a real-world check and a guess in advance. The
square with no score owes a routine, a report and a line saying what would
show it had failed. She has one hour, so most of it goes back in the box.

* Your practice — Step 14: Sort by the proof owed

/Thirty minutes, with your box from step 1./

Mark out four squares on the floor or a large sheet.

| | A tool or a count can score it | Only you can report it |
|---+---+---|
| *Shown to work for the thing practised* | 1 | 2 |
| *Wider benefit disputed or untested* | 3 | 4 |

1. Take each method from your box and ask two questions. Can something
   other than my own impression tell me how I did? Has it been shown to
   improve the very thing practised? Put it in its square. Go by what you
   actually know, not by what its sellers say.
2. Write on each square what its contents owe you.
   - *Square 1:* practise, then check that you can do the real thing.
   - *Square 3:* the score will rise whatever happens. Before you start it
     owes a real-world check and a guess, in advance, of what the check will
     show.
   - *Square 4:* a fixed routine, the same report each time, and a line
     written first saying what would show it did nothing.
   - *Square 2:* if you have put something here, ask how anyone came to know
     that it works.
3. Now take your hours. Write how many you really have in a week.
4. Give most of them to one thing from square 1 that is aimed at where your
   check fails. Give a little to keeping what you have learned. If you want
   to explore, give minutes, not the hour, to one thing from square 3 or 4,
   and pay what it owes first.
5. Put everything else back in the box and write on the lid: /Not rubbish.
   Not now./

The hard part is the box. Everything in it is interesting, and you are
choosing not to do it.

* The same step in Org Reason

1. In =learning/practice.org=, put an ordinary Org tag on each practice entry
   (~C-c C-q~ on its heading): =scored= or =selfreport=, and =established=,
   =disputed= or =untested=.
2. Press ~C-c r /~ to list entries by tag. Check that anything tagged
   =disputed= or =untested= has a frozen prediction beside it. If not, make
   one with ~C-c r p~ and ~C-c r f~ before the next session.
3. For each method going back in the box, press ~C-c r C~, capture its name,
   and clarify it as =someday=.
4. Open this week's review with ~C-c r w~ and write how your hours are
   divided.

- [ ] Every practice entry is tagged, every disputed or untested one has a
  frozen prediction, and the methods you are not doing are on the Someday
  list.

*If you keep a trusted system:* a Someday list is exactly the lid of the
box. It lets you stop carrying the methods you are not doing without
pretending you have rejected them.

*If you have worked out what limits you:* you can put effort behind one
thing at a time. The method that gets your hours should be the one aimed at
what currently holds you back, which your check tells you.

* The same step in Reason Commons

Claims about what a method does are claims like any other, and a shared
record keeps them honest.

1. For each method, state the claim made for it and add the source you have
   actually read. A claim with no source you have read is recorded as
   exactly that.
2. Let others challenge the placement of a method in its square. The
   challenge stays on the record with its author.
3. Record who accepts each claim. A method one person swears by and nobody
   else accepts should show as such.

- [ ] Each method's claim shows its source, who placed it, and who accepts
  the placement.

* Carry forward

Four squares, your hours divided, and a box with a lid. Step 15 runs the
check.
