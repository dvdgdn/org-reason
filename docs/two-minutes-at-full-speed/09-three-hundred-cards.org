* The chapter

“Katrin needs a yes or a no by Friday,” Pri said on Wednesday, with her
hand over the telephone. “She’s got another offer for that week. Are we on
track?”

Maren had the numbers ready. She had, she realized afterwards, been waiting
to be asked.

“Level four on the memory training, from two. Fifteen hundred cards at
ninety-seven percent. The dictation’s gone from fifty-four to sixty-one.”
She turned the laptop so that Pri could see the curve from the laboratory,
which rose from left to right like a good year’s sales. “Everything’s going
up.”

“That’s the German? All of that?”

“It’s all part of it.”

Pri looked at the curve. She had run the business side of Solberg & Nair
for nine years on the principle that Maren did not say things she had not
checked.

“Four thousand two hundred dollars I would love not to spend,” she said.
“I’d still rather spend it than lose Dunmore. You’re sure.”

There was a moment, quite distinct, in which Maren could have said /I added
up my week and seventy minutes of it was German/. It was true and she had
written it to a stranger. But the curve was on the screen, and the ring had
been closed for seventeen days, and tomorrow was Thursday, when she would
do better at the table, and then it would be true enough.

“I’m sure.”

“Then I’ll tell her no on Friday morning.” Pri took her hand off the
telephone. “Unless I hear from you.”

-----

She meant to be ready for Thursday. That was why she did what she did on
Wednesday night.

The assistant had by now helped her build something useful: a list of the
things her ears kept missing. The run-together pairs. Numbers from
twenty-one to ninety-nine, spoken. Forty workshop words she had heard at
Frau Reinholt’s table and failed to catch. Three hundred items in all. It
occurred to her that if she could simply /have/ all three hundred by six
o’clock tomorrow, she would walk in a different person.

She made them into cards and began at eight.

The first pass was dreadful: perhaps a third. The second, an hour later,
was better than half. She made tea. On the third pass she was fast and
almost fluent, the answers arriving before she had finished reading, and on
the fourth, a little after midnight, she went through all three hundred
with eleven mistakes.

Two hundred and eighty-nine of three hundred. She had never learned so much
in an evening. She went to bed with her head ringing.

-----

“/Zweiundsiebzig/,” said the man on Frau Reinholt’s left, in the middle of
a sentence about his pension.

Maren knew that she knew it. She could see the card. She could see where on
the card the word had been, upper left, and that she had got it right three
times running at about eleven o’clock. What she could not do was hear it
and have it mean a number. By the time /seventy-two/ arrived he had finished
with the pension and was on to his knee.

It went like that for fourteen minutes. It was longer than she had ever
lasted, and that was the cruel part, because it felt like progress while it
was happening. But when she did the count on her fingers, as she had in
January, it came to one word in four. Of the three hundred, she recognized
a handful as they went by, mostly too late to use, and each time with the
same sick little jolt: /I had that last night./

/Haben wir/ she caught every time. It no longer cost her anything.

“You are tired,” Frau Reinholt said afterwards, in English, cutting the
cake. It was poppy seed.

“I was up late. I learned three hundred things.”

“In one night?”

“I thought, if I had them all for today —”

“And do you have them?”

Maren shook her head.

“No.” Frau Reinholt did not seem surprised. “But ‘/haben wir/’ you have.
I watched you. Three weeks ago it was a hammer. Since when do you practise
that one?”

“Since the first Saturday. A few minutes, most days. It’s on a list I read
before I start.”

“A few minutes, most days, three weeks. And it sits.” She tapped the table.
“Three hundred, one night, four hours. And they are gone before the cake.
My teacher in the stenography school said: what goes in in one night goes
out in one night. She was a horrible woman. She was right about
everything.”

Maren went home and did not open the flashcard program. It was the first
evening in eighteen days that the ring stayed open, and she noticed that
she minded.

On Friday at five past nine there was a voicemail.

/Hi, it’s me. I’ve told Katrin no. She was very nice about it, she’s taking
the Frankfurt job. So that’s that! Fingers crossed, partner. Not that we
need them./

-----

She went to the laboratory at five because the session was booked, and did
her twenty minutes badly, and when Dr. Albrecht came to read the screen
Maren said, “It’s not working,” and to her own horror her voice cracked.

Dr. Albrecht looked at her, then at the corridor, then pulled the booth’s
second stool across with her foot and sat down.

“The game? You’re at four point two. It’s working fine.”

“The German. I went back to the table yesterday. One word in four. It was
one in five three weeks ago. And I told my partner on Wednesday not to book
the professional, because of this.” She pointed at the curve. “And she
didn’t.”

“Ah,” said Dr. Albrecht.

She did not say /I told you/. She had told her, in room 311, in so many
words, and they both knew it.

“Show me what else you’ve been doing. All of it.”

Maren showed her. Dr. Albrecht went through the flashcard program’s
statistics the way a doctor reads a chart: quickly, stopping twice.

“This deck. Two thousand common words. Ninety-seven percent on the first
day.”

“Yes.”

“So you knew them before you started.”

“I — yes. I’m a translator.”

“Then for three weeks you’ve been testing yourself on what you already
know. That’s why it’s pleasant.” She scrolled. “And this one, from
Wednesday. Three hundred new. Four passes in one evening, then nothing.” She
looked up. “How many did you have at the table?”

“Hardly any.”

“No. You wouldn’t.” She took a marker from her lanyard and turned to the
small whiteboard on the booth wall, where someone had left a shopping list.
She drew a line that fell away steeply from left to right. “You learn
something. You start losing it at once, fast, then slower.” She drew a
second line beginning partway down the first: it rose back to the top and
fell again, less steeply. Then a third, shallower still. “If you get it
back just as it’s slipping, by making yourself recall it, not by reading it
again, it comes back and it goes more slowly the next time. Then you can
wait longer: a day, three days, a week.”

“And if I go through it four times in one night.”

“Then you’ve climbed back up this first bit four times while it had hardly
dropped. It feels marvellous. Nothing’s been fetched from anywhere.” She
capped the marker. “I’ll say this for it: it’s about the best-supported
thing I know of in the whole business of learning. It’s been found over and
over for a hundred years. It’s also no fun at all, because the moment that
does you good is the moment you nearly can’t remember.”

“That program was built to do that. The spacing.”

“It was doing it, for words you didn’t need. And for the three hundred you
did need, you went round it.” She said it without edge. “Put the three
hundred in. Let it give you fifteen a day. When it shows you one you’ll hate
it, because it’ll have waited until you’ve almost forgotten.”

Maren looked at the three curves.

“And your game?”

Dr. Albrecht was silent for a moment.

“My game isn’t doing your German any harm. I don’t think it’s doing it any
good; that’s my bet, and I’ll know more in April. What it’s doing is taking
twenty minutes of your sixty.” She stood up. “I can’t tell you to drop out
of my own study. I’d lose a participant. But I told you what I expected.
When’s Ambrose back?”

“Monday.”

“Then on Monday tell him what you told me.” She paused at the curtain. “And
ring your partner tonight. Don’t leave her till Monday.”

* What Maren has just learned

Three hundred items learned in one night are gone by the next evening, while
one small thing practised a few minutes a day for three weeks has stuck. The
deck that gave her ninety-seven percent was made of words she already knew.
She told her partner not to book the professional on the strength of numbers
that were not about the scene, and the professional is no longer free.

* Your practice — Step 9: Space the repetitions

/Fifteen minutes to set up; then a few minutes a day./

This step is for whatever you need to hold on to: the particular items,
patterns or moves that your exercise keeps turning up.

1. Write each item on its own card: what you must produce on one side, the
   prompt on the other. Take them from your own misses, not from a list
   somebody else made.
2. Throw out any you get right the first time. Being right is pleasant and
   teaches nothing.
3. Add a few new ones a day. Ten or fifteen. Not three hundred.
4. To review a card, make yourself recall the answer before you turn it
   over. Reading it again does not count.
5. When you get one right, wait longer before you see it again: a day, then
   three days, then a week, then two. When you get one wrong, start it
   again tomorrow. A flashcard program will do this arithmetic for you.
6. Expect each review to come just as you were about to forget. That
   near-miss is the part that works.

Do not cram before the real thing. If the real thing is tomorrow, what you
can do tomorrow was decided over the past weeks.

And if you have told anyone that you are on track, look at what that was
based on. If it was a score from something other than your scene, tell them
today.

* The same step in Org Reason

Org Reason does not schedule reviews. Keep your cards in whatever
spaced-repetition tool you use, in Emacs or outside it, and let that tool
decide the dates.

1. In your practice entry for today, under /Adjustment for the next
   repetition/, write how many new cards you added from today's misses.
2. If you have only a handful of things to revisit and no card tool, give
   each a bring-forward date: press ~C-c r C~, capture the item, and clarify
   it as =tickler= with tomorrow's date. When it comes back, test yourself,
   then clarify a new one for three days on.
3. If you have told someone you are on track, press ~C-c r C~ and clarify
   the correction as an =agenda= item with their name, for today.

- [ ] Today's practice entry records new cards from your own misses, and no
  review is scheduled for the night before the real thing.

*If you keep a trusted system:* daily reviews are a repeating commitment
with a context and a length. Give it one next action, "Do today's reviews",
five or ten minutes, and do it before the pleasant things.

* Carry forward

A small set of cards from your own misses, reviewed a few minutes a day at
growing intervals. Step 10 asks what any of your numbers are a measure of.
