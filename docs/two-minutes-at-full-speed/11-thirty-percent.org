* The chapter

Jutta Reinholt made an excellent Herr Brandl.

She had insisted on the back room of the German Society, with the door
shut, and on sitting at the far end of a table with her hands folded on a
sheet of notes. Her nephew Matthias, a broad, mild man of forty with a
toolmaker’s forearms, sat to one side with four lists and a pencil. Maren
had given them the substance of four of the real Herr Brandl’s reports on
Monday. She had not seen what they made of them.

“/Also,/” said Frau Reinholt, and leaned back before she began, exactly as
Maren had always imagined he would.

It was two minutes by Matthias’s watch. It was about the runout of a
spindle. Maren knew that because the first sentence had /Rundlauf/ in it
and she caught the first sentence whole, every word. She felt a leap of
hope and then the second sentence was arriving on top of the first, with a
figure in it, a decimal, and she reached for the figure and while she was
reaching the third sentence went past. After that it was the table again.
Nouns, like posts in a flood. A long passage where Frau Reinholt was
plainly explaining a cause and Maren had only its rhythm. Near the end,
/bis Ende des Monats/, by the end of the month, clear as a bell.

Silence. They were both looking at her.

“He says,” Maren began in English, “that there is a problem with the
spindle runout. On the — on the new line.” She had nothing. “And that
something must be done by the end of the month.”

Matthias made three small ticks. He did it without expression, which she
understood later was a kindness.

The second passage was about coolant. The third and fourth she could not
afterwards have named.

“Eight,” Matthias said at the end, having added it twice. “Of forty. Three,
two, two, one.” He turned the lists round so she could see. “You have the
first thing each time. Always. Then it goes dark. Sometimes you have the
last thing. The numbers, never. In the middle is where he says what is
wrong and what he wants, and in the middle you are not there.”

“I know every word you said,” Maren told his aunt. “If you gave me that
sheet I could put it into English as fast as I can type.”

“Naturally,” said Frau Reinholt. “And on the twelfth of March you will ask
the gentleman from Chemnitz to write it on a napkin.” She unfolded her
hands. “Eight. So. Now you know what eight feels like. Come back when it is
more.”

-----

The last session of the memory game was on Friday. Maren did it well, as it
happened: she finished on level five. It gave her no pleasure at all.

Dr. Albrecht had her results on a single sheet.

“Before and after. The game: two to five, which is about the average. The
reasoning test: twenty-one of thirty-six before, twenty-two after. One
point; that’s noise. The listening task, where you hold sentences while
doing sums: the same, to the decimal.” She put it down. “So you’re one more
person who got a great deal better at the game and no better at anything
else I measured. It doesn’t mean much by itself. Forty of you might.”

“I got eight out of forty at the real thing last night.”

“What real thing?”

Maren showed her. She had the four lists, with Matthias’s ticks, and the
page on which she and Keane had worked it out.

Dr. Albrecht read it standing, and then sat down and read it again. Her
whole manner changed; it was the first time Maren had seen her look at
something with appetite.

“This is good,” she said. “No, this is /good/. Points of meaning fixed in
advance by someone who knows the subject. Scored by someone other than you.
Same form both times. Who designed it?”

“I did. He asked questions.”

“He would.” It was said with exasperated affection. “In January I asked you
what the measure was, and you had ‘nobody looks at me’. This is a measure. I
could put this in a paper.” She tapped the eight. “So. The fifth of March.
What do you predict?”

“I hope I’ll pass.”

“What’s a pass?”

“I don’t know. Enough that Pri doesn’t have to hire someone.”

“That’s a hope and a consequence. I asked what you predict. Out of forty.”

Maren felt the same reluctance she had felt in room 311 when he asked for a
Tuesday. It was the reluctance of being made to stand somewhere.

“If I write a number down and then I miss it —”

“Then you’ll have learned something about your German and something about
your judgment, both at once. That’s a bargain.” Dr. Albrecht pulled a pad
toward her. “If you don’t write it down, I can tell you exactly what will
happen, because it happens to me. On the sixth of March you’ll get your
score, and within about an hour you’ll remember having expected roughly
that. Whatever it is. Everybody’s a good forecaster afterwards.”

“Thirty,” Maren said. “Thirty of forty.”

“Why thirty?”

“Because below that I couldn’t do the audit. I’d be missing a quarter of
what they said.”

“So thirty is what you need.”

“Yes.”

“I didn’t ask what you need.” She waited. “You did eight last night. You
have two weeks. You’ve changed your routine since Monday. What do you
actually think will be on Matthias’s sheet?”

It was a different question, and the number that came was a different
number, and Maren did not like it.

“Twenty-four. Perhaps.”

“How sure are you of twenty-four or better? If you did this a hundred
times.”

“Not very. A third of the time? Thirty percent.”

“So you think you’ll probably fall short of your own guess.”

“Yes.”

“Good. Most people write ninety.” She pushed the pad across. “Write it.
All of it. The date, the number, how sure. And one more line: what result
would tell you the practice isn’t working at all. Not a disappointing
result. One that means stop.”

Maren thought about eight.

/Friday 20 February. On Thursday 5 March, same form, scored by Matthias, I
will get at least 24 of 40. I am 30 percent sure./

/If I get 12 or fewer, two weeks of this practice have done nothing I can
measure. I tell Pri to hire for all three days and I change what I am
doing./

She signed it. Dr. Albrecht read it, wrote /Seen, I. Albrecht, 20 Feb/
underneath, folded it into an envelope, and put the envelope in her desk
drawer and locked the drawer.

“Why do you get to keep it?”

“Because you’d open it on the fourth and change the twenty-four.” She
dropped the key in her pocket. “Don’t be offended. I would.”

Maren was at the door before she thought to ask.

“What did you write? For your study. Before you started it.”

“That the game would transfer to nothing. Seventy percent.” Dr. Albrecht
turned back to her screen. “It’s in a registry, with a date on it, where I
can’t get at it. I’d quite like to be wrong, you know. It would be a much
more interesting career.”

* What Maren has just learned

A first run of the check gives Maren eight of forty and shows her where she
vanishes: in the middle, where the cause and the request are. Asked for a
prediction, she offers a hope, and then the number she needs. The number she
actually expects is lower, and she is only thirty percent sure of it. It
goes in a locked drawer with the result that would mean stop.

* Your practice — Step 11: Seal a guess

/Ten minutes, after your first run of the check./

1. Write down what you /hope/ the check will show on the date. Then what you
   /need/ it to show. Put both aside.
2. Now write what you /expect/. One number, on the same count as the first
   run. Use what you know: where you started, how long you have, what you
   have changed.
3. Write how sure you are that you will reach that number or better, as a
   percentage. If you write ninety, ask whether you have confused the
   expectation with the need.
4. Write the result that would mean the practice is doing nothing and you
   should stop or change it. Make it a number too.
5. Date it and sign it. Give it to someone who will not give it back until
   the day. If there is no one, seal it and write the opening date across
   the flap.

You will not want to do this, because a written guess can be missed. That is
what it is for. A miss tells you about your judgment as well as your skill,
and you need both.

* The same step in Org Reason

1. Press ~C-c r p~. Title the experiment with the check and its date. At the
   prediction, type your number, and on a second line the result that would
   mean stop. Give the date of the check as the review date.
2. With point on the new experiment, press ~C-c r f~. Say that you do not yet
   know the outcome, state the event as a yes-or-no ("I will get at least
   24 of 40"), and give your probability. Org Reason writes the original to
   a separate file that its commands will not overwrite.
3. Press ~C-c r v~ to see what was frozen.

- [ ] ~C-c r v~ shows your prediction, its probability and today's date, and
  the dashboard (~C-c r d~) will list it when the date comes.

*If you keep a trusted system:* tell the person who is relying on you the
number you expect and the number that means stop. That conversation is an
=agenda= item for them, today.

* The same step in Reason Commons

A guess held by someone else is harder to revise in memory.

1. If you practise alongside others, each person states their own expected
   result before anyone has run the check. Do not discuss the numbers until
   all are recorded.
2. Record each as that person's own statement, with the date.
3. On the day, record what was observed, and let each person say what they
   now think. Nobody's earlier statement is edited.

- [ ] Each person's guess is recorded under their name before any result
  exists.
