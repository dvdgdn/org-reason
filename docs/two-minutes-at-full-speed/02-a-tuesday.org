* The chapter

Room 311 had two armchairs facing each other across a low table, a kettle on
a filing cabinet, and no desk that Maren could see. Professor Keane took the
banana box from her at the door as if it were a guest’s coat.

He was a long, stooped man of sixty or so in a cardigan with leather at the
elbows, and he had the gentlest handshake she had ever been given. He set
the box on the table, lifted out the printed list of articles, read the
first page of titles with attention, and put it back. Then he found her
card.

/Finding out: perhaps 600 hours. Doing: 4 days./

“Thank you for counting,” he said. “Most people round it.”

“Is it bad?”

“It’s about usual, for people who write to me. The ones who never read
anything don’t write.” He sat, and waved her to the other chair. “You asked
which method has the best evidence.”

“Yes.” She had her notebook open.

“For what?”

“For — improving. Cognitively. In my case specifically for listening, but I
think the underlying problem is speed of processing. Possibly working
memory. I’ve read that —”

“What would you like to be able to do?”

She stopped. “Process spoken language faster.”

“That’s a faculty.” He said it without impatience. “I’ve spent thirty-five
years watching people get good at things and I have never once seen a
faculty. I can’t point at one. I’ve seen a woman land an aircraft in a
crosswind. I’ve seen a man hear that a second violin was a hair flat. What
would I see /you/ do?”

“Understand German when it’s spoken.”

“Where?”

“Anywhere.”

“I can’t watch anywhere. Give me a Tuesday.”

It was a strange thing to be asked, and she noticed that she resented it.
She had come for an answer and was being made to do something that felt
like a child’s exercise. But there was, as it happened, a particular day.

“It’s a Thursday. The twelfth of March. The last day of an audit.” She
described it: Dunmore Tool, the conference room on the mezzanine with the
window onto the shop floor. She had been there once to collect drawings.
Four engineers from Kessler on one side of the table and Dunmore’s plant
manager and his people on the other.

“Who speaks?”

“There’s a quality engineer, a Herr Brandl. I’ve translated his reports for
years. He writes sentences a page long.” She found she could see him,
though they had never met: someone who would lean back before he began. “He
says there’s a problem with a tolerance on the spindle. He talks for — two
minutes. Without stopping. They do.”

“And then?”

“And then everyone looks at me.”

“And what do you do?”

It was the part she had never let herself picture, because picturing it had
always ended with the looking.

“I turn to the plant manager,” she said slowly, “and I tell him what Herr
Brandl said. In English. All of it. The tolerance, the figure, what Kessler
want done about it.” She was surprised by the next thing, and it was the
truest. “And nobody looks at me. They look at each other. They start
arguing about the spindle.”

Keane had taken an index card from a tin on the cabinet. He wrote for a
moment in a small upright hand and passed it across.

/Thursday 12 March. The mezzanine room at Dunmore Tool. Herr Brandl speaks
for two minutes about a tolerance. I turn to the plant manager and say it in
English. Nobody looks at me; they argue about the spindle./

“Is that it?”

She read it. It was absurdly small. It contained nothing about speed of
processing. It was also, she realized, the only thing in four years of
reading that she actually wanted.

“That’s it.”

The door opened without a knock and a woman of about Maren’s age came in
backwards with two mugs and a laptop under her arm. Short dark hair, a
university lanyard, a conference sweatshirt.

“Ambrose, the room booking for — sorry. You’ve got someone.”

“Dr. Albrecht, who runs most of what goes on here. Ms. Solberg, who
translates machine manuals and wants to interpret.” He held up the card.
“We’ve just found out where she’s going.”

Dr. Albrecht read it over his shoulder. She read quickly and frowned at the
end.

“’Nobody looks at me.’ What’s the measure?”

“There isn’t one yet.”

“Then how does she know if she’s getting closer?” She turned to Maren, not
unkindly. “I’m not being difficult. It’s just that ‘I’ll feel more capable’
is the cheapest result there is. Everybody feels more capable after eight
weeks of anything. If you can’t say what would count as better, you can’t
train it, and I certainly can’t measure it.”

“She’ll need something countable,” Keane agreed. “Before long. At the
moment she needs to know which room she’s walking toward. Until ten minutes
ago she was walking toward ‘processing speed’.”

“Which at least has a test.”

“It has several. Has anyone ever asked you to interpret a processing
speed?”

Dr. Albrecht made a small sound that was not quite a laugh, and put one of
the mugs in front of him. Maren had the impression of an argument that had
been going on comfortably for years.

“I read that working memory is the bottleneck in listening,” Maren said.
“That if you train it —”

“That’s mine,” said Dr. Albrecht. “My study, I mean. Not the claim.” She sat
on the arm of Keane’s chair. “There’s a game. You keep track of things a few
steps back, and it gets harder as you get better. People improve at it
enormously. The question is whether anything else improves, and I test that
before and after: reasoning, a listening task, a few others. Last year’s
group got far better at the game. On everything else they were where they
started. I’m running it again with more people, because one result isn’t a
result.” She shrugged. “Twenty sessions, twenty minutes. I’m recruiting, if
you want to see your own numbers. I’d tell you not to expect it to help
your German. But I’d be glad of the data.”

Maren heard /laboratory/, and /numbers/, and /twenty sessions/. Something
with a start date and somebody in charge.

“Yes,” she said. “Please.”

Keane looked at her over his mug and said nothing for a moment.

“That would be twenty minutes of your day,” he said. “How many have you
got?”

“An hour. I work. An hour is realistic.”

“Then it’s a third.” He left it there. It was not, his manner suggested,
his hour.

She waited for him to say what she should do with the other two-thirds.
Instead he reached into the banana box, took out the three juggling balls
in their net, weighed them in his hand and put them back.

“I can’t recommend you a method,” he said. “I don’t yet know what it is you
can’t do. Neither do you; you know what you can’t do in general, which is
no use to either of us. We’ll find out next week. In the meantime I’d like
some homework, and you’ll think it’s peculiar.”

“All right.”

He tapped the card in her hand. “That’s one. Bring me nineteen more.”

* What Maren has just learned

Asked what she wants, Maren names a faculty: faster processing. Nobody can
watch a faculty improve. When she is made to give a day, a room and what
she does in it, she finds one scene she has wanted for years. It has no
measure yet, as Ines points out, and Ines is right that it will need one.

* Your practice — Step 2: Write one scene

/Ten minutes./

Take the thing you chose in step 1.

1. Write down what you have been telling yourself you want. It will probably
   be a quality: /fluent/, /sharper/, /better at/.
2. Now replace it with one occasion. Write:
   - the day and the place;
   - who else is there;
   - what happens just before your part;
   - what you do, in a sentence with a verb in it;
   - what happens next that shows it worked.
3. Test it. Could a stranger standing in the room tell whether this
   happened? If they would have to ask how you felt, rewrite it.
4. Check that it is something you want, not something that would look
   impressive. If the scene embarrasses you slightly by being small, it is
   probably the right one.
5. Copy it onto a card. One card.

You will be tempted to add a second scene, or a list of abilities the scene
"represents". Don't. One card.

* The same step in Org Reason

1. Open =gtd/horizons.org= in your workspace. Under the heading for goals,
   add your scene as one line, with its date if it has one.
2. Press ~C-c r c~, capture the scene's text again, and in the inbox clarify
   it with ~C-c r x~ as =reference=. You now have it as a note you can link
   to.

- [ ] The scene is in your horizons file and in one reference note.

*If you keep a trusted system:* the scene is a goal, not a project. It has
no next action yet and should not be given one today. If you have a project
on your list with a name like "learn German", open it and paste the scene in
as its outcome.

*If you have worked out what limits you:* set the scene beside your aim and
its conditions. Ask whether being able to do this would loosen the thing
most of your troubles share. If it would not, it may still be worth wanting;
know which it is.

* Carry forward

One card with one scene on it. Step 3 asks for the others.
