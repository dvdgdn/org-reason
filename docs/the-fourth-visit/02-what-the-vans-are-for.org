* The chapter

On Wednesday Corinne asked three people what the repair service was for, and
got three answers, each of them delivered as though the question were a
little insulting.

She had not planned to ask. She had planned to ask Gloria what the board
wanted to hear. But on Tuesday night she had sent Odile Marchetti a short
message, /Thank you for yesterday. I've escalated your father's repair/, and
the reply had come at 5:40 the next morning, from someone evidently already at
work.

/I asked you not to. But thank you for telling me. A question, if you want
one: what are the vans for?/

Gloria first, at eight.

"For?" Gloria did not look up from the board pack. "Corinne. We are a
landlord. We keep two thousand one hundred and forty homes fit to live in and
we do it on rents that haven't kept up with a single thing we buy. The city
measures us on attending within five working days. We are at ninety-one
percent and the standard is ninety. That is what I can stand up and say on
Thursday week. What I can't say is why the backlog has grown by half while
we're meeting the standard." Now she looked up. "That's your part."

/Gloria: to meet the five-day standard./ Corinne wrote it in her notebook
outside the door, and under it, because it had been said with more feeling,
/and to have something true to tell the board./

The yard on Quarry Road at ten. She had been there perhaps four times in
eleven years. Bram Kowalczyk met her at the gate in a fleece with the Harbor
Row logo and walked her straight past the vans to the thing he wanted her to
see.

The routing board filled a wall of the dispatch office: a screen as wide as a
door, twenty-four rows, each row a technician, each row a string of colored
blocks. Six blocks a row.

"Eighteen months ago," Bram said, "a consultant sat in that car park for a
week with a clipboard. You know what his report said? It said my people spent
forty percent of the day in their vans. Parked. He had photographs." He let
that sit. "I have men here who've done this for thirty years and a stranger
photographed them eating a sandwich. So. March. This." He tapped the screen.
"Every tech, six jobs planned, shortest drive between them. They average five
point two because some overrun. It was four point one. Nobody has sent a man
with a clipboard since."

"It's impressive," Corinne said, and it was.

"What's it for, you asked on the phone. It's for that. Every one of them
working, all day, and me able to prove it."

/Bram: every technician working all day, and proof./

A van pulled in while they were talking, early, which Bram noticed with a
small frown. The woman who got out was in her fifties, short, with a grey
plait down the back of her jacket. She opened the rear doors and stood
looking into the racking with her hands on her hips.

"Mags," Bram called. "You're on Pell Court till eleven."

"I was on Pell Court." She did not turn round. "Boiler's got a failed gas
valve. I know which one. I haven't got it. I used to have two." She pulled
out a plastic bin, looked into it and put it back. "So that's a visit. I'll
book it back in for when the part comes and that'll be another visit, and you
can put both of them on your wall."

"Mags Olsen," Bram said to Corinne. "Twenty-six years. Best heating tech
I've got."

"And?"

"And she thinks the board is a personal insult."

Mags had heard. She came over, wiping her hands. "I think I can do five
visits a day or I can do two repairs. Ask the tenant which one she rang up
about." She looked at Corinne's office shoes without unkindness. "You're the
one who's been given it."

"Apparently it shows."

"Come out with me one day. Bring a coat."

/Mags: to finish the repair./ Corinne underlined it without knowing why.

The call center at two, back on Tolland Avenue, on the floor below her own.
Nine desks in a horseshoe and a screen above them that changed every few
seconds. Lu Tran stood where she could see all nine.

"What's it for." Lu said it flatly, the way Gloria had. "Sit down a minute."

She pulled up a chair for Corinne and stayed standing.

"I started here in 2017 as an agent. In 2018 there were four of us. A tenant
would ring about a leak and get a recording, and leave a message, and nobody
rang back, because nobody had time to listen to the messages. People waited
three and four weeks to find out if we'd even heard them. I had a woman cry
on the phone because she thought we'd lost her name." She nodded at the
screen. "Ninety-four percent inside twenty seconds. Every single caller
leaves with an appointment inside five working days. You wrote that, didn't
you? In the handbook. 'Someone at your door within five working days.'"

"I did."

"Well. We made it true. That's what it's for."

/Lu: nobody waits to be heard./

Corinne took the notebook up to her desk and laid it open under the form with
the seven lines. Four people, four purposes. Meet the standard. Keep everyone
working. Finish the repair. Answer at once.

None of them was foolish. Gloria's kept the city off their backs. Bram's had
ended a humiliation. Lu's had ended something worse. And each of them could
be met in full, was being met, was up on a screen in green, on the same
morning that a man on Garnet Street sat in his coat beside a radiator that
three technicians had come and looked at.

She tried the question the other way round. Not what each department was
for. What would Aldo Marchetti say the vans were for?

That was not difficult at all. /To fix it./ And then, because she had read
enough complaint letters to know the second half: /so that I don't have to
ring you again./

She wrote it out in a sentence she could have put in a handbook.

/A fault a tenant reports is fixed soon and stays fixed./

Then the part she would once have skipped. How would she know? Not by
attendance; they had attended 4C three times. By the number of days between
the report and the repair. Thirty-four, on average. And by whether the tenant
had to ring again about the same thing, which Lu's screens could probably be
made to show and currently did not.

She looked at the sentence for a while. It was plainer than any of the four.
It also made no mention of five days, or of five point two, or of twenty
seconds, and on Thursday week she was going to have to say it in a room where
those three numbers were the only good news anyone had.

She texted Odile. /To fix what's reported, soon, so it stays fixed. Is that
too simple?/

The answer came that evening. /I don't know your business. Would your
superintendent agree with it? Would the woman in the call center?/

Corinne thought about it honestly. /Yes. All of them. That's what's strange./

/Why strange?/

She did not have an answer to send.

* What Corinne has just learned

Every part of Harbor Row can say what it is for, and every part is succeeding.
None of those purposes is the purpose of the whole. When Corinne writes the
aim from the tenant's side, everyone would agree with it, and nobody is
measuring it.

* Your thinking — Step 2: Say what it is for

/Five minutes./

Take the situation you chose in step 1.

1. Write down what each person or group in it would say they are there to do.
   Use their words. Do not judge the answers.
2. Now write what the whole effort is for, in one sentence, from the side of
   whoever it is meant to serve. If it is your own life, that is you in a
   year's time.
3. Under it write: /I would know by …/ and name something you could count or
   see. Not effort, not activity: the result.
4. Check it against step 1's answers. Would each of those people agree with
   your sentence? If one would not, you have two aims and should choose which
   one this work is about.

If your sentence is longer than the ones in part 1, shorten it. It should be
plain enough to be slightly embarrassing.

* The same step in Reason Commons

Alone, you decide the aim. With others the aim is a proposal like any other
statement.

1. Propose your sentence as the goal.
2. Ask each person whether they accept it as written. Acceptance is something
   a person does, by name; silence is not acceptance.
3. If someone will not accept it, ask them to propose their own wording
   beside yours instead of editing yours.

- [ ] The goal shows who proposed it and exactly who has accepted it.
