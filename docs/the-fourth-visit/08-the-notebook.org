* The chapter

“No,” said Samira Haddad, and began to close the door of 2B.

“Ms. Haddad, I only need five minutes.”

“You’ve had my neighbours’ five minutes. A hundred and thirty-eight times.”
But the door stopped, because a child behind her had started to cough, a
tight dry bark that went on too long, and she turned to it. “Karim. Puffer.
Then come and say hello.”

Corinne waited on the mat. She had learned at the yard that 118 Garnet had a
tenants’ council, that 2B chaired it, and that since October its floor
captains had been writing down every Harbor Row van that pulled up outside.
/Who would know?/ she had written in Wendell’s office. The answer was
standing in front of her in a nurse’s-aide tunic, not letting her in.

The boy appeared, seven or so, solemn, holding an inhaler like a toy
telephone. He looked at Corinne’s lanyard. “Are you the ceiling lady?”

“The ceiling?”

Samira breathed out through her nose and opened the door.

The bathroom was off the hall. Above the bath the ceiling had come through
in a patch the size of a tray, and around the patch the paint had gone the
soft speckled black that no tenant handbook has a kind word for. A bucket
stood in the tub.

“Reported October ninth. Upstairs’ waste pipe. You’ve been three times.”
Samira said it without looking at the ceiling. “First one was a decorator.
He said he couldn’t paint over it till the leak was fixed, which, thank you,
I knew. Second was a plumber who needed a part. Third knocked while I was at
work, when I’d told your girl on the phone I’m home after three. So I’ve had
a letter. My son has asthma and there’s mould over his bath and I’ve had a
letter about my attendance.”

“I wrote the paper that led to the letters,” Corinne said.

She had decided on the bus that she would say it first, before anything
else could be said. It did not feel brave. It felt like putting down
something heavy on the wrong person’s floor.

Samira looked at her properly for the first time. “Did you.”

“I told the board that a third of our visits fail because tenants aren’t
home. I believed it. I now think I was wrong, or mostly wrong, and I can’t
show it either way, because the only record I have is ours. I’m told you
have another.”

“So you want my notebook to prove you were wrong.”

“I want to find out.”

“That’s what the last one said. Man from your office, two years ago, about
the lifts. Read our log, found the three days that suited him, put them in a
report.” She went into the kitchen, and Corinne followed, uninvited. A
hardback exercise book lay on top of the refrigerator. Samira took it down
and held it against her chest. “We’re filing with the city on Friday. Two
hundred and twelve households have signed. This goes with it. I’m not
handing it to Harbor Row first so you can get your story straight.”

“Then don’t hand it to me. Keep it shut.” Corinne took out her own notebook.
She had not planned this either; it came from Wendell’s third question, asked
three times. “I’ll write down what I think it says. Before you open it. If
tenants really weren’t home, your log shows empty apartments. If I’m right
now, it shows people in, and vans that didn’t stay. I’ll write both down,
with numbers, and sign it, and you can have the page.”

“Why would you do that?”

“Because last time I looked at one of these, I saw what I’d come for.”

Karim had climbed onto a chair to watch. Samira considered her for a long
moment, then pulled out another chair with her foot.

“Write.”

Corinne wrote it at the kitchen table, in ink.

/Wednesday 26 November. Before seeing the Garnet Street log./
/Harbor Row’s system: 41 visits to 118 Garnet coded “no access,” 1 October to
date./
/If tenants were out, the log will show nobody home for most of them./
/I expect instead: someone home for more than half. And for those, the van
gone again in under ten minutes./
/If someone was home for fewer than ten of the 41, I am wrong again./

She signed it and turned it round. Samira read it twice, tore the page out
along the stitching and put it in the drawer with the takeout menus.

Then she opened the exercise book.

It was ruled in columns by hand. Date. Apartment. Van arrived. Van left. Who
was in. Initials of the floor captain. The fourth-floor entries were in a
round, careful script that turned out to belong to a retired bus driver in
4A.

They went through it together, Corinne reading out the visit from her
laptop, Samira finding the line. It took an hour and a pot of tea, and Karim
kept the tally in crayon on the back of an envelope, which Samira checked.

Forty-one visits coded /no access/.

Someone home for twenty-nine.

Of those twenty-nine, the van had stayed less than five minutes for
twenty-two.

Corinne found the line for the thirty-first of October without being asked.
/4C. Van 9:10. Left 9:14. Mr M at window. — R.D./

“He was at the window,” she said.

“He’s always at the window.” Samira’s voice had lost some of its edge, not
all. “And the other twelve were out. I’m not going to tell you they weren’t.
Mrs. Okafor in 3D has dialysis three mornings and you book her for mornings.
People work.”

“Twelve out of forty-one.”

“That’s still twelve wasted trips. Whose fault are those?”

Corinne nearly answered. /The call center books the next free slot without
asking./ It was in her diagram; it had even survived Wendell. But it had a
question mark on it.

“I don’t know yet,” she said. “I think I know. I’m trying not to say things
I think I know.”

Samira closed the book. “Friday we file. This doesn’t change that.”

“It shouldn’t.”

“And those letters.”

“I can’t withdraw them. I don’t have the authority.” It was true and it
sounded like every other thing Harbor Row had ever told this woman. “I’m
going to try.”

-----

Mags was waiting in the dispatch office at six with two hundred closed work
orders printed and stacked, because Corinne had asked her that morning and
Mags had said that if anyone was finally going to count she wanted to be in
the room.

They did the other two lines of /Go and look/ at Bram’s desk, under his wall
of colored blocks, after he had gone home.

Sixty visits coded /no access/, pulled at random from the whole estate. For
each, Mags read the order. A part outstanding, or a trade that could not
have done the job, on thirty-eight.

Then the stack. Two hundred repairs completed in October. For each one, the
number of times a van had gone to that address for that fault. Mags called
them out and Corinne made five-bar gates down a sheet of paper. One. Three.
Two. Two. Four. One. Five, for a boiler at Pell Court. It was eight o’clock
before the last sheet turned.

Four hundred and eighty visits.

“Two point four,” Corinne said.

It did not look like much, written down. She worked the rest on the same
sheet because she could not wait. Twenty-four technicians at five point two
visits a day was six hundred and twenty-four visits a week. At two point four
visits to a repair, that came to two hundred and sixty repairs.

Two hundred and ninety new reports came in every week.

“Thirty short,” said Mags, reading upside down. “Every week.”

“Is there an old stack? From before the board went in?”

There was, in a box file: two hundred orders from the spring of last year
that someone had pulled for an audit. They counted those too, faster now.
Three hundred and twenty visits. One point six. And at four point one visits
a day, four hundred and ninety-two visits a week —

“Three hundred and seven repairs,” Corinne said. “With fewer visits. We did
more repairs when everyone was doing less.”

Mags looked up at the wall, where her own row would light tomorrow with six
blocks. “Don’t tell him tonight,” she said. “Let him have his supper.”

On the bus Corinne typed three characters to a number in Halifax, /2.4/, and
looked at them, and sent them. No answer came. She had not expected one.

* What Corinne has just learned

Corinne wrote down what a record would show before she was allowed to open
it, and said what would prove her wrong. The tenants' log shows people home
for twenty-nine of forty-one "no access" visits, and out for twelve. Then she
counts the number nobody had: 2.4 visits to a repair, where it used to be
1.6.

* Your thinking — Step 8: Ask what else would be true, and look

/Ten minutes to prepare; the looking takes as long as it takes./

Take the arrows you lean on most, the ones your whole diagram would fall
without.

1. For each, complete this sentence: /If this is really how it works, then I
   should also be able to see …/ Name something you have not looked at yet,
   that somebody could go and count or read.
2. Write the opposite as well: /If I am wrong, I will see …/
3. Put a number on it where you can, and write the result that would make you
   give the arrow up.
4. Sign and date it. Give the page to someone, or put it where you cannot
   edit it.
5. Only then look. Use a record you did not make if one exists.
6. Write down what you found beside what you expected, including the part
   that did not fit.

The discomfort here is writing your expectation where another person can
hold you to it. Do it anyway. A check you only run when you are sure of the
answer is not a check.

* The same step in Org Reason

Org Reason can fix an expectation in place before the result is known.

1. Press ~C-c r p~. Give the check a title, type your prediction (what you
   expect to see, and what would show you are wrong), and give the date you
   will look.
2. Press ~C-c r f~ to freeze it, and answer its prompts honestly, including
   whether you already know the outcome. From then on Org Reason will not
   overwrite the original.
3. Go and look. Then press ~C-c r o~, choose the experiment, and record what
   you saw and what you will do next.
4. Add what you found to the commons with ~C-c r t n~, tree
   =current_reality=, role =observation=.

- [ ] ~C-c r v~ shows your original wording unchanged beside the outcome.

*If you keep a trusted system:* if the record you need belongs to someone
else, clarify the request as =delegate= with their name. A check you mean to
do yourself on a certain day is a =calendar= entry. The dashboard
(~C-c r d~) lists predictions whose date has come.

* The same step in Reason Commons

What a person saw is a different kind of statement from what a person
thinks, and a shared record keeps them apart.

1. Record each finding as an observation, with who observed it and where.
2. If the finding came from someone else's record, they add it under their
   own name. Do not enter it for them.
3. Let an observation answer a challenge only if the challenger agrees that
   it does.

- [ ] Observations are distinct from causes and each names its observer.

Your signed expectations with what you found beside them, and at least one
arrow that is now either firmer or gone. Step 9 follows the arrows down.
