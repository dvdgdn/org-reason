* The chapter

On Sunday morning Corinne wrote a list of things to do, and by noon she had
thrown it away.

It had been a perfectly good list. /Email Gloria. Draft withdrawal. Confirm
stock with Wendell. Chase cards. Brief all staff. Prepare hearing notes./
Eleven items, each beginning with a verb, the kind of list she had been
making all her working life and ticking with real pleasure.

What stopped her was the fifth line. /Brief all staff./ She had typed it
without thinking and then, with Mr. Dufresne’s lining paper photographed on
the phone beside her, asked herself which of the numbered things on that
wall it would make true. None of them. It would produce an email. She had
written it because a plan without it looked unfinished.

So she started again, and this time each line had two halves, and she did
not let herself write the first half until she could write the second.

/If Wendell releases $38,000 on Monday afternoon, then six buildings’ parts
are on the vans by Tuesday./

/If Farah and Mags finish five more cards today, then agents can take first
calls on twelve faults from Tuesday./

/If Bram moves Mags’s row to the two technicians he named, then the first-call
desk opens Tuesday at eight./

Each of those belonged to someone else, and each of them was waiting, by Mr.
Dufresne’s numbering, on things above it. She worked upward.

/If the hearing agrees to count days-to-fixed in six buildings for four
weeks, then fifty-eight percent is no longer the figure Harbor Row is judged
on there./

/If the tenants’ council asks for that count, then the hearing is being
asked by the people who complained, not by the landlord./

/If the notices are withdrawn in writing before ten tomorrow, then the
council will ask./

And there she was again, at number one, the line with no name.

She could not withdraw the notices. That was simply so; the board had voted
and only the board, or the director acting for it, could suspend what it had
decided. What could she do, herself, today, with nobody’s leave?

She looked at the question for a long time. Then she opened a blank page and
wrote, without any of her usual care for how it would land:

/To the Board of Harbor Row Housing, the Garnet Street Tenants’ Council, and
the Office of Housing Standards./

/On 13 November I presented a paper to the Board stating that the repair
backlog was growing because tenants were not at home for appointments. The
missed-appointment notices sent to 138 households were issued on the strength
of that paper./

/The paper was wrong. I did not test it. Of 41 visits to 118 Garnet Street
recorded by us as “no access,” the tenants’ own log shows someone at home
for 29. Of 60 such visits sampled across all buildings, 38 could not have
been completed because of a missing part or the wrong trade. One of the
visits I cited to the Board as an example failed because of an instruction I
gave./

/I ask that the notices be withdrawn./

She signed it with her name and her job, and printed two copies, and read
one aloud to the kitchen. It went.

Then she wrote its other half, as she had for the rest.

/If I give this to the council tonight, then number one has a name on it,
whatever the director decides./

That was the first action. It was the only one on the page that needed
nobody else. It was also the only one that could cost her the job.

-----

Gloria Ferreira was in her office at five on a Sunday with the hearing
bundle spread over the conference table and her shoes off. She read
Corinne’s one page standing up. Then she read the signed statement, and sat
down.

“You want me to walk into a hearing about our failings,” she said, “and open
by handing them an admission.”

“I’m going to hand them that one in any case. It’s mine. I’m not asking you
about it; I’m telling you before I do it.” Corinne’s mouth was dry. “The
rest I am asking.”

“That’s very clear of you.”

“I’ve been unclear for a month.”

Gloria turned the page of actions to the light. She read down the right-hand
halves, the /then/s, more slowly than the /if/s.

“Every time you’ve come into this room you’ve told me why,” she said. “This
doesn’t say why anywhere.”

“It says what I think will happen if we do each thing. That’s all I’ve got.
I gave you two reasons and you acted on both, and you were right to, because
I sounded sure.” She made herself go on. “I can’t give you something to rely
on in the way you mean. I can give you what I expect to see by a date, in
numbers, written down before it happens, and sealed, and I can tell you
tonight what would show I’m wrong again. And what it costs if I am. Six
buildings, four weeks, and thirty-eight thousand dollars of parts that we’d
fit sooner or later anyway.”

“And fifty-eight percent.”

“It’ll get worse than fifty-eight in those buildings. I’d rather say that
tomorrow, out loud, before it happens, than explain it afterwards. If the
tenants ask to be counted by days-till-it’s-fixed, it isn’t your number that
broke. It’s a number they’ve asked you to stop using.”

Gloria was silent. Outside the window the street lights had come on along
Tolland Avenue.

“A board likes a promise,” she said at last. “So does a hearing officer. So
do I. I have run this place for nine years on being able to say: this is
what we will do, and then doing it. You’re asking me to go in and say: this
is what we think will happen, and we may be wrong, and here is how you’ll
know.” She rubbed her eyes. “Do you know what it costs me to stand up and
say Harbor Row sent a fee notice to an old man it had left without heat?”

“Yes.”

“Less than a monitor. Marginally.” She pulled a pad toward her. “I can’t
withdraw a board decision. I can suspend it tonight on my own authority and
ask them to ratify on Thursday, and if they won’t, that’s my job and not
yours. Write me the letter to the households. Short. No ‘regret any
inconvenience’. I’ll sign a hundred and thirty-eight of them if I have to do
it with a rubber stamp.”

“And the six buildings?”

“Four weeks. From Tuesday. And I want the envelope you mentioned on my desk
at nine tomorrow, before we walk over, so that I’ve seen what’s in it before
the city has.” She looked up. “You understand that if the numbers in it are
wrong, I’ll have told a hearing they would be right.”

“You’ll have told them what we expected. That’s a different sentence.”

“It’s a much less comfortable one.”

“Yes,” said Corinne. “It’s been a less comfortable month.”

Gloria almost smiled, and reached for her shoes.

At ten to nine that night Corinne climbed the stairs at 118 Garnet, because
the elevator was slow, and put two sheets of paper into Samira Haddad’s
hand: the director’s suspension of the notices, signed, and her own
statement, signed.

Samira read both on the doorstep. From inside came the sound of a bath
running out, and no drip.

“Council’s at eight,” she said. “We’ll say it.”

* What Corinne has just learned

A list of tasks let Corinne write down things that produced nothing. When
each action has to be written with the effect it should cause, one line
falls away and another turns out to be hers alone. She does that one first,
at a cost. She offers the director an expectation with a date where she used
to offer a reason, and the director takes it, uncomfortably.

* Your thinking — Step 16: Write each action with its effect

/Fifteen minutes./

Take your ordered list from step 15.

1. For each thing to achieve, write the action that would bring it about.
   One action, one doer.
2. Write every action as two halves: /If [who] does [what], then [what
   becomes true]./ Do not write the first half until you can write the
   second.
3. Cross out any action whose second half you cannot write. You put it there
   because plans usually have one.
4. For each remaining line ask the old question: is that action enough on
   its own? Add what else must already be true.
5. Find the action that is yours, that nobody has to permit, and that the
   earliest item on the list depends on. That is the first action. Write it
   on a separate line.
6. Say what the first action will cost you. If it costs nothing, look again
   for the one you are avoiding.

Then do it, today if it can be done today.

* The same step in Org Reason

1. Press ~C-c r t n~ and choose the =transition= tree. Enter each action
   with the role =action= and each second half with the role
   =expected_effect=. Use =existing_condition= for what must already be true.
2. Press ~C-c r t l~, tree =transition=. Select the action and any existing
   conditions together as sources, the expected effect as the target, and
   =produces= as the relation.
3. Press ~C-c r t x~, choose your first action and a context. Org Reason
   creates one next action linked to that statement. Pressing it again later
   opens the same reminder instead of making a second.

- [ ] ~C-c r a~ shows one new NEXT action, and opening it shows the link back
  to the commons.

*If you keep a trusted system:* only the first action becomes a next action.
The others stay in the transition tree until their turn; use ~C-c r t x~ on
each as the one before it is done. Actions that belong to other people are
waiting-for entries (~C-c r x~, =delegate=) with the date you will ask.

* The same step in Reason Commons

In a shared record an action is a claim about what will follow, made by the
person who will act.

1. Each person proposes their own actions, with the effect they expect.
2. Others may challenge the expected effect without challenging the action.
3. An action in the commons is not a reminder in anyone's list. Each person
   takes their own into whatever they use to remember things; the commons
   keeps the link.

- [ ] Each action shows the person who will take it and the effect they
  expect from it.

* Carry forward

A page of actions, each with its expected effect, and a first action done or
dated. Step 17 writes down what you expect to see.
