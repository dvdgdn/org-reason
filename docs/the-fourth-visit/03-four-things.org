* The chapter

The control room was on the sixth floor of a concrete tower at the end of the
container terminal, and at six on Thursday morning it was the only lit window
on the waterfront.

/If you want to see what I do, come before the day shift,/ Odile’s message had
said. /I can’t leave the desk but you can stand behind it./

So Corinne stood behind it, holding coffee from a machine in the lobby, and
looked down at four berths. Three of them had ships alongside, lit like
stadiums, with cranes swinging boxes off their decks in a rhythm that was
almost restful. The fourth berth was empty. Its crane stood with its boom
raised, dark.

And out past the breakwater, riding lights showing, a ship was waiting.

Odile had two screens and a paper chart, and spoke for some minutes to a
radio in a language of numbers. When she put the handset down Corinne said
what anyone would have said.

“There’s a ship out there and an empty berth in here.”

“Yes.”

“Why isn’t it on the berth?”

“What would it do there?”

Corinne looked again. “Unload.”

“With what?”

The dark crane. “It’s broken?”

“A cable is being replaced. Until noon.” Odile wrote something on the chart.
“So tell me what that ship needs. Not what it wants. What it can’t be
unloaded without.”

“A berth. A working crane.”

“Go on.”

“People to work it.”

“A gang, yes. Twelve, for that ship.”

“Somewhere for the boxes to go?”

“Trucks, and room in the yard behind. Four things.” Odile glanced at her.
“The berth has been free since two this morning. If I had put the ship on
it, my berth would have looked busy. Is the ship any nearer to being
unloaded?”

“No.”

“No.” She turned back to the screens. “Three years ago this terminal paid a
bonus on crane moves per hour. The cranes had never been busier. Ships waited
longer than they had in ten years. I’ll let you work out how; I have a pilot
to call.”

She made her call. Corinne watched a box the size of a room travel through
the air and settle on a truck as lightly as a cup on a saucer, and thought
about Bram’s wall with its six colored blocks a row.

When Odile was free again she said, without turning, “You sent me a sentence
last night. Soon, and stays fixed. What can’t that happen without?”

Corinne had her notebook. She had, in fact, already made a list on the bus,
and it had eleven items on it, beginning with /adequate staffing/ and ending
with /a culture of ownership/. She read it out. It took a while.

Odile waited until she had finished. “How many of those did the ship have?”

“Four.”

“Could you run your service if I took ‘a culture of ownership’ away from
you?”

“Not well.”

“Could you fix my father’s radiator?”

Corinne thought about a seized valve and somebody with the right part
turning up to replace it. “Yes.”

“Then perhaps it isn’t one of them. I’m not saying it doesn’t matter.” She
reached for the radio again. “Try each one that way. Take it away and see if
the radiator can still be fixed.”

It was a curious twenty minutes, standing in the dark above the cranes
crossing things out. /Adequate staffing/ went, to her surprise; twenty-four
technicians had fixed more repairs a week two years ago than they did now, so
whatever was missing was not simply people. /Good communication/ went the
same way as the culture. /Modern systems/ went. What was left when she had
taken away everything the radiator could do without was short.

Somebody had to know what the job needed before going. A plumber had been
sent to 4C first, and the fault was in the heating.

The person who could do it, and the part, had to arrive at the same time. The
second visitor had known exactly what was wrong and had not had the valve.

Mr. Marchetti had to be able to let them in. She did not think that had been
the difficulty in his case, since he never went out, but it was plainly
something a repair could not happen without.

And the money had to last. She added that one last and reluctantly. It was
the only condition Gloria would have thought of first.

/We know what the repair needs before we go./
/The person and the parts that can finish it arrive together./
/The tenant can let us in at a time they agreed./
/The money lasts the year./

“Four,” she said.

“Read them to me.”

She did. Odile listened with her eyes on the anchored ship.

“If you had all four for a given repair, would it get done?”

“Yes. I think so. Yes.”

“And if you had three?”

Corinne ran them. Knowing, and the tenant home, and the money, but no part:
that was the second visit to 4C. The part and the tenant and the money, but
the wrong idea of the job: that was the first. “No. Any one missing and it’s
a wasted trip.”

“Then you have something like my four.” Odile’s desk phone lit and she let it
ring once. “I’d keep the list where the people who gave you your other
answers yesterday can see it. They each look after a piece of one of those.
I doubt anybody looks after all four.”

“That would be me, as of Friday.”

“Would it?” It was the first thing she had said that sounded like doubt, and
she softened it at once. “I mean: do you control any of them?”

Corinne considered the call center, which reported to the customer services
director; the yard, which was Bram’s; the stockroom, which for reasons of
history belonged to the controller, Wendell Price; and the money, which was
Gloria’s and the board’s.

“I can ask people for things,” she said. “That’s about the extent of it.”

“Mm.” Odile picked up the phone.

Corinne’s own phone buzzed while she waited: the dispatcher at the yard.
/4C Garnet is in for Tue 11th, first job. Dev’s got the valve now./

She showed it to Odile when the call ended, pleased to have something
finished to offer. “Tuesday. First thing. With the part.”

Odile read it and for a moment said nothing. Down on berth two a crane had
stopped with a box hanging.

“Has anyone told my father what time?”

“It’s the first job of the day. He’ll be glad to get it over with.”

“He takes the hearing aid out at night,” Odile said. “It charges on the
kitchen counter. He’s not quick in the mornings.” She handed the phone back.
“It’s kind of you. Truly. I’d only say that you have just read me four things
a repair can’t happen without, and you have arranged two of them.”

The day shift was coming in behind them, hanging up coats. Corinne made a
note, /4C: what time is “first job”?/, and on the bus back to Tolland Avenue
began drafting what she would say to the board.

* What Corinne has just learned

A ship on a berth with no crane is not being unloaded, however busy the berth
looks. Corinne's eleven requirements shrink to four when she tests each by
taking it away. A repair needs all four at once, each belongs to a different
department, and she has just arranged two of them for 4C.

* Your thinking — Step 3: Name the few conditions

/Ten minutes./

1. Under your aim from step 2, write everything you think it depends on. Let
   the list run long.
2. Take each item in turn and remove it in your mind. Ask: /with this gone,
   could the result still happen, at least once?/ If it could, cross the item
   out. It may help; it is not a condition.
3. Rewrite each survivor as a state of affairs, not an activity: /the part is
   there/, not /improve purchasing/.
4. You should have three to five. Read them aloud and ask two questions. If
   all of them held, would the result follow? If any one failed, would it be
   lost?
5. Beside each condition write who looks after it today. Notice whether
   anyone looks after all of them.

Expect to cross out things you believe in. A condition is something the
result cannot happen without, which is a much smaller set than the things
that are good.

* The same step in Org Reason

1. For each condition press ~C-c r t n~, choose the =goal= tree and the role
   =critical_success_factor=, and enter it as a state of affairs.
2. Press ~C-c r t l~ and choose the =goal= tree. Select one condition as the
   source, your aim as the target, and the relation =necessary_for=. Repeat
   for each condition, one at a time: they are each necessary, not jointly a
   cause.
3. If a condition has its own smaller requirement, add it with the role
   =necessary_condition= and link it the same way to its parent.

- [ ] ~C-c r t v~ shows your aim with three to five conditions beneath it.

*If you keep a trusted system:* you wrote who looks after each condition. If
one of them is you and nothing on your lists serves it, capture that with
~C-c r c~ now. If one is another person, add a line to that person's agenda
list so you raise it when you next speak.

* The same step in Reason Commons

Each person in a shared effort tends to see the condition they look after.

1. Ask each person to propose the conditions they think the aim cannot be
   met without.
2. Where two people have proposed the same condition in different words,
   leave both and say so. Let the group accept one wording.
3. Notice any condition that nobody proposed until someone outside the group
   asked.

- [ ] Each condition shows its proposer, and you can see which ones everyone
  has accepted as necessary.

* Carry forward

Three to five conditions and who looks after each. Step 4 sets your troubles
beside them.
