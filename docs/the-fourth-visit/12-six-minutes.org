* The chapter

“Depends who’s asking,” Mags said.

They were in the cab of her van in the yard at ten past three on Monday,
with the heater on and the napkin from St. Brendan’s flattened on the
dashboard. Mags had read the three lines and gone straight to the middle
one.

“’Nobody can tell what a repair needs until a technician is standing in the
room.’ If it’s Farah asking, no. She can’t tell. If it’s me asking, half the
time I can tell from what they say, and most of the other half I could tell
if I could see it.” She scratched her jaw. “Not all. There’s jobs you have
to open up. But I never get the chance, do I? By the time anything reaches
me it says ‘radiator cold, plumbing’ and a time.”

“Could we try one?”

“Now?”

“There’s a ceiling at 118 Garnet. Apartment 2B. Three visits and it’s still
dripping into the bath. She’s home after three.” Corinne already had the
number up. “I owe her more than a phone call, but it’s what I’ve got.”

Samira Haddad answered on the fifth ring with a television on behind her.

“Ms. Haddad, it’s Corinne Vance from Harbor Row. I’m not ringing about the
notices or the hearing. I’m sitting with a heating and plumbing technician
called Mags Olsen and I’d like her to ask you about your bathroom ceiling.
It might take ten minutes. You can say no.”

A silence. “Is she coming out?”

“Not today. She wants to ask you things.”

“Nobody’s ever wanted to ask me things.” Then, away from the phone: “Karim,
turn that down.” And back. “Go on.”

Corinne put the phone on speaker and, on an impulse she could not have
explained, started the stopwatch on her own.

Mags leaned in. “Afternoon. Where’s the wet? Middle of the ceiling or by a
wall?”

“Over the bath. Sort of the tap end.”

“Tap end. Does it drip all the time, or when somebody upstairs has a bath?”

A pause. “I never thought. Evenings, mostly. It’s worse about eight.”

“When they let the bath out. Right. Is your phone one with a camera? Can
you do a video call on this number?”

It took a minute of fumbling. Then the screen showed a swaying hall, a
doorframe, and the ceiling: the tray-sized hole, the black bloom around it.

“Get me in close to the edge of the hole. Hold it. That’s the underside of
their floor. See that grey pipe? That’s the bath waste from upstairs. Follow
it left. Stop.” Mags’s finger was on the screen. “There. That white collar.
It’s wet all round. That’s your leak. It’s not your ceiling at all, love,
it’s their trap.”

“The first man said he couldn’t paint it till it was fixed.”

“He was a decorator. He was right, that’s all he was.” Mags sat back. “Who’s
above you? 3B? Are they in?”

“Mrs. Ferrand. She’s always in.”

“If I asked very nicely, would the lad go up with the phone and ask her if
he can look under the side of her bath? There’s a panel. It pulls off.”

There was a consultation. The picture went to the hall carpet, then a
stairwell, then a door, and an elderly voice saying /what, now?/, and then
the camera, held by a seven-year-old with intense concentration, looked into
the dark space beside a bathtub.

“Bit lower, Karim. Good man. Left. There.” Mags blew out her cheeks. “Inch
and a half trap, cracked right across the nut. You can see the tide mark.
Every time she pulls the plug half of it goes down the pipe and half of it
goes on your ceiling.”

“Can you fix it?” Samira’s voice, faint, from behind the camera; she had
followed her son up.

“Forty minutes. It’s a seven-dollar part and I’ve got four of them in the
cage. But I need to be in /her/ bathroom, not yours. Has anybody been in
3B?”

“No,” said Mrs. Ferrand, off screen, with dignity. “Nobody has asked.”

Corinne stopped the watch. Six minutes and ten seconds.

Three visits. A decorator, a plumber without the part, a knock at a door
when its tenant was at work, and all three to the wrong apartment. She
looked at the work order on her laptop. /Trade: Decorating. Location: 2B
bathroom./ Farah, or someone like her, had taken it in under three minutes
and given a date before the phone went down.

“When?” said Samira.

Mags looked at Corinne, and Corinne looked at Bram’s wall through the
windshield and decided she would deal with that afterwards.

“Thursday, half past three,” Mags said. “You’re back by then and Mrs.
Ferrand’s in. I’ll bring it myself. Once it’s stopped, your ceiling wants
two weeks to dry out before anybody patches it, and I’ll put that on the
order so they don’t send you a painter on Friday.”

The picture swung up to Samira’s face, close, slightly out of focus.

“That’s the first time,” she said, “that anybody at Harbor Row has told me
what was wrong with it.”

“We’ve only just found out,” said Mags.

“You found out in five minutes. I’ve been ringing since October.” But it was
not said to Mags. She was looking, as far as a person can on a phone, at
Corinne. “Is this what you’re going to do? Instead of the letters?”

“I don’t know yet if I can. It would mean people wait longer for a van. In
return it would be the right van, and it would come once.”

“No.”

“No?”

“Don’t tell me I’m trading. That’s what you always say down there: you can
have it quick or you can have it right.” The camera steadied. “You rang at
ten past three. It’s not half past. I know what’s broken, I know who’s
coming, and I know when, and it’s a time I’m in. That /is/ quick. I’ve never
had it so quick. And if she comes Thursday and it’s done, that’s right as
well. I pay rent for both. I’ve just had both. So don’t write it down as me
giving something up.”

The call ended. The heater ticked.

Corinne took the napkin off the dashboard.

/Because a tenant only believes they have been heard when a van is at the
door./ A woman who had been sent three vans had just said she had never been
answered so fast, and no van had moved.

/Because nobody can tell what a repair needs until a technician is standing
in the room./ Mags had not left her seat.

/Because understanding a job takes days, so holding it means silence./ Six
minutes and ten seconds.

“All three,” she said. “It wasn’t one weak one. They were all the same
mistake. I thought the only two people who could be on that call were an
agent who doesn’t know and a tenant who can’t say. So either you send
someone blind or you make them wait.”

“And there’s me,” said Mags. “Sat in a van.”

Corinne turned the napkin over and wrote on the back. It took four tries to
get it short.

/The first conversation is with someone who can fix it. The tenant hears,
that day, what is wrong, who is coming, and when. The visit is booked once,
with its parts./

Mags read it.

“You know what that needs.”

“You. Off the tools.”

“Me and two more, if it’s the whole estate. On a phone all day.” She did not
sound as appalled as Corinne had expected. She sounded as if she were
pricing a job. “Bram will have a stroke. Three of his best not doing a single
visit.”

“I know.”

“And that lot upstairs with the headsets. What are they for, then, if I’m
answering?”

Corinne had not thought about that at all. She said so.

“Well,” said Mags, starting the engine, because her row had a four o’clock
on it, “somebody’d better.”

* What Corinne has just learned

All three becauses rested on one unspoken belief: that the only people who
could take a tenant's call were someone who cannot diagnose and someone who
cannot describe. With a technician on the phone and the tenant's own camera,
the job is understood in six minutes, the tenant feels answered without a
van, and nothing is held for days. Samira refuses to call it a trade.

* Your thinking — Step 12: Find the change

/Fifteen minutes./

Take your becauses from step 11.

1. For each one ask: /is this always true? Has there ever been a case where
   it was not?/ One real exception is enough to open it.
2. Ask who would know. It is often the person who does the work, or the
   person the work is done for. Ask them. Better still, try one case with
   them, today, and time it.
3. When a because gives way, write the change that makes it untrue. One or
   two sentences, in the present tense, describing how things then are. Not
   a compromise between the two actions: something that makes the choice
   between them unnecessary.
4. Test it against both needs from step 10. Read each need and ask: /is this
   met in full?/ If either side is "giving something up", you have a midpoint
   again. Keep looking.
5. Look at whether several becauses fell together. If so, write the belief
   they shared.

Be suspicious if the change costs nobody anything. Write down what it will
need that you do not yet have, and who you have not yet thought about.

* The same step in Org Reason

1. On each assumption that gave way, press ~C-c r t ?~, choose =challenge=,
   select the same relationship, and record the exception you found.
2. Press ~C-c r t n~, choose the =future_reality= tree and the role
   =injection=, and enter your change as a present-tense statement.
3. Add what you tried as an =observation= in the =conflict= tree: what you
   did, with whom, and how long it took.

- [ ] The conflict's relationships show both the assumption and the
  challenge to it, and /future reality/ holds one injection.

*If you keep a trusted system:* trying one case is a =next-action= with a
person in it. The change itself is not yet a project; do not make it one
until step 14. If you already feel the pull to start, capture that with
~C-c r c~ and leave it in the inbox.

* The same step in Reason Commons

A change proposed by one side tends to be heard as that side winning.

1. Have the person who found the exception record it as an observation
   under their own name.
2. Propose the change. Then ask each side separately: /is your need met in
   full?/ Record each answer as that person's own acceptance or objection.
3. If the person the work is for is in the commons, ask them too. They may
   state the result better than either side.

- [ ] The injection is proposed, and each side's answer to "is your need
  met in full?" is recorded under their name.

* Carry forward

One change, written in the present tense, that both needs survive in full,
and a note of what it will require. Step 13 reasons forward from it.
