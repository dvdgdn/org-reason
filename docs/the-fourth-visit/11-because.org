* The chapter

The rehabilitation wing at St. Brendan's had a day room with a view of the
car park and a row of high-backed chairs, and on Saturday afternoon Aldo
Marchetti was in one of them with a walking frame parked beside him like a
bicycle.

"They make me go up and down the corridor," he told Corinne. "Twenty meters.
Then they write it in a book. I said, in my day we were paid for piecework."

He was thinner. His voice was the same. Both hearing aids were in.

Odile had landed that morning. She brought three coffees from the machine
and sat down on her father's other side, and for a minute nobody said
anything.

"I got your message," Odile said. "Two point four. I didn't answer."

"I didn't expect you to."

"I was angry with you for about three weeks. I'm telling you so that you
know it's finished, not so that you'll apologize again." She turned her cup
a quarter turn on the arm of the chair. "What have you done with it?"

So Corinne told them: Wendell's pencil, the notebook on the refrigerator,
the count, the yellow wall and the note in capitals at the bottom of it. Mr.
Marchetti listened with his eyes half closed, as to a radio play. Then she
took out her phone and showed Odile the photograph of Lu's whiteboard, the
five boxes, and explained the arrangement she had made on Tuesday. Seven
days in place of five. Five jobs in place of six. Five-minute calls.

"And both of them agreed?"

"Both of them."

"That's not a small thing, in your building." Odile enlarged the photograph
with two fingers. "How is it going?"

Lu had sent the figures on Friday night. Corinne knew them by heart and
wished she did not. In the two buildings, over three days: attended within
the promise, seventy-eight percent, because the promise was now seven days
and tenants who had been told five were ringing to ask where the van was.
Visits per repair, on the few jobs closed so far: two point three.

"From two point four," Odile said.

"It's only three days."

"What did Ms. Tran give up?"

"Two days of her promise. And her call time."

"And the superintendent?"

"One job a day per technician."

"So each of them has a little less of what they told you they couldn't do
without." Odile handed the phone back. "And the note in capitals. The job is
sent before anyone knows what it needs. Is it still sent before anyone knows
what it needs?"

Corinne thought of Farah. /I ask two more things I won't understand the
answers to./

"Yes."

"Mm."

It was the same sound she had made in the control tower. Corinne waited for
the question that usually followed it, and for once got in first.

"You're going to ask me something and I'm not going to have the answer."

"Probably. May I have the picture again?" Odile laid the phone flat on the
arm of the chair between them, where her father could see it too. She put
her finger on the arrow between Lu's need and Lu's action. "To let every
tenant know quickly that they're being dealt with, you must book a visit at
once. Because?"

"Because that's how they know."

"That's the arrow again in different words. Because what? What would have to
be true about tenants, for a booking to be the only way?"

Corinne started to answer and found the answer was a sentence she had
written. Not the one in the handbook. The one behind it, from a meeting in
2019 that she could suddenly see: herself at a flipchart, pleased, saying
that people needed something they could /picture/.

"Because a tenant only believes they've been heard when there's a van at the
door," she said. "I said that. More or less in those words. It's why the
promise is about someone at your door and not about anything else."

"Is it true?"

"I don't know. I thought it was obvious."

Mr. Marchetti opened his eyes. "The young woman who answered me was very
nice," he said. "Very quick. She did not ask me one thing about the
radiator." He lifted a finger. "I have lived with that radiator thirty-one
years. I could have told her it was the valve. I told the first man it was
the valve and he said he was a plumber. Nobody asked."

Odile did not look at Corinne. She moved her finger to the lower arrow.

"To finish the repair at the visit, you must hold the job until it's
understood. That one seems reasonable. But the superintendent told you it
can't be done. Because?"

"Because you find out when you get there. 'That's the job.'" Corinne heard
Bram say it. "Because nobody can tell what a repair needs until a technician
is standing in the room."

"Is that true?"

Corinne looked at the old man who had known it was the valve.

"And the jagged line," Odile said. "Book at once, or hold. Why can't you do
both? Because?"

"Because understanding a job takes days. A technician has to go and look. So
if you hold it until it's understood, the tenant hears nothing for days.
Which is Lu's 2018."

She had the photograph and no pen. Odile passed her one, and a paper napkin
from under the coffee, and Corinne wrote the three of them out because she
wanted to see them outside her own head.

/…because a tenant only believes they have been heard when a van is at the
door./

/…because nobody can tell what a repair needs until a technician is standing
in the room./

/…because understanding a job takes days, so holding it means silence./

They did not look like much. They looked like the kind of thing nobody would
bother to say.

"That's why nobody says them," Odile said, when Corinne remarked on it. "How
many of the three did your arrangement on Tuesday touch?"

Corinne went down the napkin. Seven days in place of five: still a van at
the door, only later. Five jobs in place of six: still a technician finding
out in the room. Longer calls: Farah, asking questions she could not use.

"None."

"So you moved the line between the two boxes. A little toward one side." Odile
finished her coffee. "It's what I'd have done at your age. It gets everybody
out of the room."

"It took me forty minutes and I was proud of it."

"I'm sure it was well done."

Mr. Marchetti had been studying the napkin through the lower half of his
glasses. He tapped the middle line.

"At Delacroix we pulled a proof before the run. One sheet. You read it, you
mark it, /then/ you print ten thousand. Nobody said, you cannot know what is
wrong with a page until you have printed ten thousand of it." He sat back,
tired by the speech. "Somebody could look first. I am only a tenant."

A physiotherapist had appeared in the doorway with a clipboard. It was time
for the twenty meters.

Odile got up to walk beside the frame. Over her shoulder she said, "I don't
know which of those three is the weak one. I don't know your work. But I'd be
surprised if all three could stand being looked at. Who would know?"

Corinne put the napkin in her notebook. She knew a woman with a grey plait
who had fitted a valve in twenty-five minutes and said that anyone who had
ever seen a steam radiator would have known from the doorway.

/From the doorway./ Not from the room.

* What Corinne has just learned

Under every arrow of the conflict is a "because" that nobody says aloud, and
one of them is Corinne's own from six years ago. Her compromise moved the
line between the two actions and touched none of the three. Each side now
has a little less, and jobs are still sent before anyone knows what they
need.

* Your thinking — Step 11: Say the "because"

/Ten minutes./

Take your five boxes from step 10. There are five arrows: two from the needs
to the aim, two from the actions to the needs, and the jagged line.

1. Read each arrow aloud and add the word /because/: /To have [need] we must
   [action], because …/
2. Write whatever comes, even if it sounds too obvious to write. If your
   first answer only repeats the arrow, ask: /what would have to be true
   about the world for this to be the only way?/
3. Write two or three for each of the three right-hand arrows: need to
   action, need to action, and the jagged line. Those are where to look.
4. Put a mark beside each "because" that you have never actually checked.
5. Put a second mark beside any that you, personally, once said.

If you tried a midpoint after step 10, test it now: which of your becauses
did it change? If none, it moved the line and left the conflict standing.

* The same step in Org Reason

1. Press ~C-c r t v~ and find the relationships in your conflict tree.
2. For each "because", press ~C-c r t ?~, choose =assumption=, select the
   relationship it sits under, and type it.
3. Add several to the same relationship if you have them.

- [ ] Each of the three right-hand relationships carries at least one
  assumption.

*If you keep a trusted system:* an assumption you marked as unchecked is a
question. Capture it with ~C-c r c~ and clarify it as =question=. It is kept
as a note, out of your task lists, until you decide to test it.

* The same step in Reason Commons

People can accept the same conflict and hold different reasons for it. A
shared record shows where they part.

1. Ask each side to state the "because" under their own arrow, and then to
   guess the other side's.
2. Record every assumption with its author. Do not merge similar ones.
3. Ask who accepts each assumption as true. Expect some that nobody will
   accept once they are written down.

- [ ] Assumptions are attached to relationships, each with an author, and
  you can see which ones nobody has accepted.

* Carry forward

Three or more becauses, with marks on the unchecked ones and on your own.
Step 12 looks for the one that can be given up.
