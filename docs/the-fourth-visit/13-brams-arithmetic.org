* The chapter

"Three," said Bram. "You want three."

"Eventually. For the whole estate. To begin with, one."

"Three technicians at five point two a day is seventy-eight visits a week."
He had worked it out before she finished the sentence. "You're short thirty
repairs a week, by your own count, and your answer is to take seventy-eight
visits off my wall."

It was Tuesday, six days before the hearing. Corinne had come to the yard
with the napkin, now transferred to a clean page, and a speech. She had
practiced the speech. It began with Samira Haddad's ceiling and ended with
the phrase /the right van, once/, and she was two sentences into it when
Bram held up a hand.

"Don't sell it to me."

"I'm not —"

"You are. You're good at it. You sold the board on tenants and you nearly
sold Gloria on Wendell's cage, and I sat there both times and thought, that
sounds right." He said it without malice. "I don't want it to sound right. I
want to see what happens. You've got your yellow wall, where everything goes
down to one thing. Do me one that goes up. Start with Mags on a telephone and
show me where it ends."

She had not prepared that. She looked at the bare patch of wall beside her
earlier notes, and at the pad of stickies, and felt the speech go out of her
like air.

"All right. You do it with me. Stop me whenever the next one doesn't
follow."

At the bottom she put the change, on one note. /A technician takes the first
call and sees or hears the fault./

"Then the job is understood before it's booked." She wrote it and put it
above.

"Not every job," said Bram. "Some you've got to open up."

"Most."

"Mags says most. I'd say most." He nodded. "Go on."

"Then the right trade is sent, with the right part."

"/If/ the part's in the cage. If it's on a truck from the supplier you've
understood the job very nicely and you still can't do it."

That was fair. She wrote a second note and put it beside the first, joined
with a loop, as at the yard: /and the part is in the cage or is waited for
before booking./

"Then the visit finishes the repair."

"If the tenant's in."

"The technician on the phone agrees the time with them. That's part of the
call."

"Put it on, then. Don't just say it."

She put it on. The chain was growing sideways as well as up. Each time she
reached for the next effect, Bram found the thing that also had to be true
for it to follow, and each time, once it was written, the step was firmer.
She noticed that she had stopped wanting to argue with him.

/Then a repair takes fewer visits./

"How many?"

"I don't know. Mags thinks about one point four. There'll always be some
that need a second."

"One point four." Bram went to his own desk and came back with a calculator
the size of a paperback. "So. Whole estate, three off the tools. Twenty-one
technicians. And don't give me five point two, because a visit where you
actually fit the thing is longer than a visit where you look at it and go
away. Call it four a day."

"Four."

"Twenty-one fours are eighty-four, times five days, four hundred and twenty
visits a week. At one point four." He pressed the keys with one finger.
"Three hundred repairs."

"Against two hundred and ninety coming in," Corinne said, and for a second
was simply glad that it was more.

"Ten a week." He turned the calculator round so she could see it. "There are
two thousand and fifteen jobs in that queue. At ten a week that's four years.
That's your answer for the hearing. 'Give us till 2029.'"

She looked at the little grey figure. Three hundred.

It was the moment to say that it would surely be better than one point four.
That at one point two it would be three hundred and fifty. That repeat calls
would drop, that morale would lift, that — the speech was right there. She
could feel exactly how to make this number sound like a triumph.

"That's thin," she said instead. "You're right. If Mags is wrong by a
little, it's nothing."

Bram looked almost disappointed.

"I haven't got one point two," she went on. "I'd like it to be one point
two. I've got one afternoon, one ceiling, and Mags's guess. I'm not going to
tell you it's more than that."

He grunted. He stood looking at the upward chain with the calculator hanging
in his hand, and then, slowly, he went on with it himself.

"Mind you." He took the pad from her. "A third of what comes into that call
center is people ringing back about a job that's not been done. Lu said that
once, thirty-five percent. If the job's done first time they don't ring
back." He wrote /Fewer repeat calls/ and stuck it up to one side. "And if my
lads are on four jobs that they can finish, they're not on six that they
can't. Nobody's driving back here for a valve at two in the afternoon." He
wrote that too. "And I'll tell you something else that's not on anybody's
wall. Dev's asked for a transfer to grounds. Twenty-nine years old. Good
hands. He said he's sick of knocking on doors to tell people he can't help
them."

He stuck the notes up crookedly, beside her straight ones.

The chain now ran from a telephone at the bottom to four notes at the top,
and the four at the top were, she saw, her first troubles turned inside out.
/A repair takes about one visit. Open orders fall. Tenants wait days, not
weeks. Tenants do not ring twice./ She had not planned that. It was simply
where the arrows went.

"It's still ten a week," Bram said.

"Yes."

"And it's still three of my best not turning a wrench."

"Yes."

"I think it's daft." He put the calculator down. "I thought the stickers were
daft and they found two point four, so I'm not as sure of myself as I was.
Here's what I'll do. Garnet Street. The three buildings, one-sixteen to
one-twenty. From tomorrow, calls from those go to Mags, and I cover her row.
Jobs from there don't go on my wall till she says they're ready. One month.
If visits per repair in those three buildings is under two, I'll eat my
board. If it isn't, you take your stickers down and we never speak of it."

"I haven't got a month. The hearing's Monday."

"Then you'll go to the hearing with four days of it," Bram said, "same as
I'd have to."

She emailed Lu from the yard to ask that calls from the three Garnet Street
buildings be put through to a desk in dispatch from eight the next morning.
The answer was one word, /Fine/, with no name under it.

On the bus she sent Odile a photograph of the upward chain. It was not a
request for approval; she told herself that.

/Your superintendent did half of this,/ Odile wrote back. /I can see two
kinds of handwriting. Good./ Then, a minute later: /Everyone on this wall
gets something they wanted. Who isn't on it?/

Corinne looked at the photograph. The technician on the telephone. The right
trade, the right part. The tenant, who did not ring twice.

And a note she had not written, for nine people in a horseshoe on the second
floor of Tolland Avenue, who took the first call now and whose supervisor
had answered her with one word.

* What Corinne has just learned

Corinne is asked to show what follows from the change, and to stop selling.
With Bram supplying what else must be true at every step, the chain reaches
the opposite of each of her troubles, and his arithmetic gives a margin of
ten repairs a week. She says it is thin. He then adds three effects of his
own.

* Your thinking — Step 13: Reason forward

/Fifteen minutes. Better with your sternest critic./

1. Write your change from step 12 at the bottom of a clean sheet.
2. Ask: /if this is in place, what happens next?/ Write the effect above it
   with an arrow.
3. For each arrow ask the question from step 6: is the change enough on its
   own to produce this? Add whatever else must be true beside it, and join
   the two with a loop.
4. Keep going up. You are finished when you reach the opposite of each
   trouble you listed in step 4. If a trouble's opposite cannot be reached,
   say so on the sheet.
5. Put numbers on the chain wherever you can, even rough ones. Work out the
   size of the result. If it is small, write that it is small.
6. Mark every arrow that rests on a guess.

The pull in this step is to protect the idea: to round the numbers up and
talk past the thin places. Give the pencil to someone who doubts you and ask
them to add what is missing. An idea your critic has added to is worth more
than one they have merely stopped attacking.

* The same step in Org Reason

1. Press ~C-c r t n~ in the =future_reality= tree for each step of the chain.
   Use the role =predicted_effect= for the steps in between and
   =desirable_effect= for the results at the top.
2. Enter each "what else must be true" with the role =existing_condition=.
3. Press ~C-c r t l~, tree =future_reality=. Select the injection, or a
   predicted effect, together with any existing conditions as joint sources,
   the next effect as the target, and =causes= as the relation.
4. Where an arrow rests on a guess, press ~C-c r t ?~, choose =assumption=,
   and say so.

- [ ] The injection is joined by relationships to at least one
  =desirable_effect=, and the guessed steps are marked.

*If you keep a trusted system:* each "existing condition" that is not yet
true is work for someone. Do not make projects yet. List them on your sheet;
step 15 puts them in order.

* The same step in Reason Commons

The value of a second person here is the "what else must be true" that you
could not see from your side.

1. Ask the person most doubtful of the change to propose the conditions
   under each step.
2. Each condition and each predicted effect keeps its proposer. A chain with
   two people's contributions in it should show both.
3. Ask each person which results at the top they accept as following. Record
   doubt as a challenge on the specific arrow.

- [ ] Predicted effects and conditions show more than one proposer.

* Carry forward

A chain from your change to the results you want, with its guesses marked
and its size worked out. Step 14 asks who is missing from it.
