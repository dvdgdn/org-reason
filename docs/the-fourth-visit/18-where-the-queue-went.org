* The chapter

At half past two on a Monday afternoon in late January there were seven
white vans parked in the yard on Quarry Road with nobody in them.

Bram Kowalczyk stood at the dispatch office window looking at them the way a
man looks at a crack in a wall.

“Finished,” he said, as Corinne came in. “Every one of them. Four jobs, four
repairs, back here by two, restocked, and now Dev’s in the mess room doing a
crossword.” He turned round. “You know what a man with a clipboard would do
with that car park? He’d have a field day. I’d be ‘underutilized’ again by
Friday.”

“What did they finish?”

“Twenty-eight jobs between the seven of them. Twenty-seven done first
visit.” It came out grudgingly and precisely; he had the figure ready. “I’m
not saying it’s not good. I’m saying it’s half past two.”

On the wall the six trial buildings’ rows showed four blocks each, all gone
grey for /complete/. Below them the rows for the other twenty-five buildings
ran on as before, six colors to a row.

And in the corner, at the desk with two screens, Mags and the technician who
had joined her after New Year both had headsets on and did not look up. A
whiteboard beside them said /WAITING FOR FIRST CALL: 31. OLDEST: FRI
AFTERNOON./

Corinne had come to the yard for exactly that number. Eighty-one percent,
Wendell had said at the hearing, and it had not moved in ten days.

“So here’s what I want,” Bram said. “There’s seventeen hundred jobs in the
queue from the other buildings that nobody’s rung anybody about. I’ve got
seven lads and three hours. I put two of those jobs on the end of each row.
That’s fourteen more visits a day and nobody’s sat in a van.”

It was sensible. It was what she would have said yes to in October without
drawing breath.

“Which jobs?”

“Whatever’s oldest.”

“Has anyone who could fix them looked at them?”

Bram opened his mouth.

“They’re booked the old way,” Corinne said. “Trade off a list. No parts
picked. If Dev goes to one at three o’clock —”

“— he gets there and it’s the wrong part, or he’s the wrong trade, and he
comes away again.” Bram said it heavily, like a man reciting his own
sentence. “And that’s two point four. I know. I know.” He sat down on the
edge of his desk. “I’ve had four months of you. I can do it in my sleep. /If/
I put unlooked-at jobs on the row, /then/.” He waved a hand at the rest.

“I wasn’t going to say it.”

“You were going to ask me a question so I’d say it. You’ve got that off
her.” He meant Odile, whom he had met once, at the hearing, and regarded
with deep suspicion. “So what, then? They do crosswords?”

Corinne looked at the car park, and then at the whiteboard in the corner.
Thirty-one tenants waiting for a call. Seven technicians who knew what a
cracked trap looked like, with three hours.

She did not say it. She had learned something about that.

“Where’s the wait now?” she said. “Not in October. Today. If you’re a tenant
at Pell Court with no hot water, what are you waiting for?”

Bram followed her eyes to the corner. He was quiet for a while.

“Her,” he said. “You’re waiting for Mags to ring you.” Another pause. “And
she’s waiting for there to be two more of her.”

“Mm,” said Corinne, and heard herself, and nearly laughed.

“Dev’s good on the phone,” Bram said slowly. “He’s patient. He’s the one the
old ladies ask for.” He got up and went to the window again, but he was not
looking at the vans as he had before. “If the afternoon lot did first calls.
From here. Two or three of them, two till five. Then tomorrow morning’s jobs
are already looked at, and they’re on a row with the parts.” He was working
it out on the glass with a finger. “So the reason they’re back at two is the
reason they can — hang on.”

He stopped.

“I’m going to have to stand up in front of the board,” he said, “and tell
them my technicians finish at two so that the telephone works.”

“You could tell them thirteen days. It was thirty-four.”

“They’ll still want to know why there’s vans parked.”

“Yes,” said Corinne. “They will.”

It would be a real argument, and not hers to have for him. She wrote the
thirty-one on her hand, said goodbye to Mags, who lifted one finger from a
keyboard, and went for the bus.

-----

The living room of 4C was warm enough that Aldo Marchetti had taken off his
cardigan.

He did it as a demonstration when she came in, and hung it on the back of
the chair, and stood beside the radiator in his shirtsleeves with one hand
on the walking frame and the other held out, palm down, a few inches above
the iron.

“You see,” he said.

She put her own hand there. Then on the valve, new brass among sixty years
of paint.

“Twenty-five minutes,” she said. “Mags told me. It was always twenty-five
minutes.”

“And five visits to do it in. I have been telling them at the physiotherapy.
They think I exaggerate.” He lowered himself into the chair. On the table by
the window, under the block of type, there were now two letters. He tapped
the upper one. “This one I read three times. From your director. No errors.
I looked.”

Odile came out of the kitchen with soup. It was Monday. She set a bowl in
front of her father and one in front of Corinne, and sat down with her own.

“How many visits does it take now?” she said. “To fix a repair. On average.”

“One point four in the six buildings. It’s on Lu’s wallboard. She put it up
herself, next to the twenty seconds.” Corinne broke bread. “It’s two point
three everywhere else. We start the next eight buildings in March.”

“And where’s the ship waiting?”

Corinne showed her the number on the back of her hand.

“At the desk. Thirty-one people waiting for someone who knows what they’re
looking at to ring them. In November the wait was at the door. We’ve moved
it to the telephone.” She thought of Bram’s finger on the glass. “I think
he’s found what to do about that. He found it himself; I only asked where
the queue was.”

“And after the telephone?”

It was the same patient question as the first one, at the counter, and
Corinne found that she had been carrying the answer about for a week
without setting it down.

“After that it’s what people know. Lu’s agents can do twelve faults from
Farah’s cards. To take the calls for every building they’d need to do
thirty, forty. Farah can do a radiator better than I’d have believed. But
that’s one person, and she got there because Mags sat beside her for a
month.” She put her spoon down. “I can draw you why the vans were going out
blind. I can’t draw you how nine people get good at something nobody has
ever taught them. I don’t know how that works at all.”

“No,” Odile said. “Nor do I.”

She did not make it sound like a failing.

When the bowls were cleared Corinne took a repair-request form out of her
bag, because she still had a stack of them and had grown attached, and
turned it over on the tablecloth. Mr. Marchetti put on his second glasses to
watch.

She wrote the date. Under it:

/Tenants in the six buildings wait up to two days for a first call./

She looked at it. It was true and she could count it. She did not write why.

“That is a short galley,” said Mr. Marchetti.

“It’s the first line.”

“Then you will need a better pen,” he said, and gave her his.

* What Corinne has just learned

The wait has not gone; it has moved, from the tenant's door to the
first-call desk. What was the answer in December is now the place where
things queue. The old instinct to keep every technician busy would undo the
gain. Corinne asks where the wait is and lets Bram find the rest, and then
starts a new list with one line that has no "because" in it.

* Your thinking — Step 18: Begin again

/Ten minutes, and then every few weeks./

When your change has been in place long enough to have done something, look
again.

1. Ask: /where is the waiting now?/ Follow one case through from start to
   finish and note where it sits longest.
2. It will usually not be where it was. Write down the new place.
3. Look at what people now want to do with the slack that has appeared
   somewhere else. Ask what it would do to the place where things are
   waiting.
4. Take a clean sheet. Write today's date and one line: what keeps going
   wrong now. A fact, countable, with no "because".
5. Check your aim from step 2 and your conditions from step 3. They are
   probably unchanged. If they have changed, say so.
6. Put a date in your calendar to do this step again.

You may find the next limit is one you do not know how to reason about: a
skill nobody has, something no arrangement of the work will supply. Write it
down as plainly as the rest. Not knowing how is also a fact.

* The same step in Org Reason

1. Press ~C-c r !~ and record what you expected the change to leave behind
   and what you observe instead. It asks for nothing else.
2. Press ~C-c r t n~, tree =current_reality=, role =undesirable_effect=, and
   enter your new first line. It sits in the same commons as the old ones, so
   the history of the problem stays in one place.
3. On your old shared cause, press ~C-c r t ?~, choose =assumption=, and note
   the date on which it stopped being what most troubles shared.
4. Press ~C-c r w~ to open this week's review and, in its notes, write where
   the waiting is now.

- [ ] The commons shows a new trouble dated after your change, and the old
  shared cause carries a note that it has moved.

*If you keep a trusted system:* look at the project you made in step 15. If
its outcome is met, mark it done; do not let it grow a second purpose. The
new first line is an inbox item (~C-c r c~), not yet a project. And add to
your weekly review a standing question: /where is the waiting now?/

* The same step in Reason Commons

A group that fixed one thing together tends to defend the fix.

1. Ask each person where they now see things waiting. Record each as their
   own observation.
2. Do not delete the old accepted reasoning. Its history is what lets a
   newcomer see why the present arrangement exists.
3. Propose the new trouble as the first statement of the next round, and
   ask who will work on it.

- [ ] The record shows the earlier accepted view, the date it changed, and
  a new proposed trouble with its author.

* Carry forward

One new line, dated, and a day in your calendar to look again. And the
question this work leaves open: when the limit is something people cannot
yet do, how do they become able to do it?
