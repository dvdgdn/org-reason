* The chapter

Lu Tran heard her out standing up, with one eye on the wallboard, and when
Corinne had finished she said, "Come and listen to one."

She plugged a second headset into the desk of an agent called Farah, who had
been at Harbor Row six years and whose calls, Lu said, were the ones she
played to new starters. Corinne put the headset on.

/Harbor Row repairs, Farah speaking./

A man's voice, older, on a bad line. His kitchen tap was dripping. No, not
dripping, running. He had a bowl under it.

Farah was kind and she was quick. Was the water hot or cold? Was it the tap
or under the sink? He wasn't sure. She typed while he talked. A list dropped
down on her screen, /Trade/, with eleven choices, and she hovered a second
between /Plumbing/ and /General/ and clicked /Plumbing/. A calendar opened.
The first white square was Thursday morning.

/I can have someone with you Thursday, between eight and twelve. Is that all
right?/

It was all right. He thanked her twice. The timer in the corner of her
screen stopped at two minutes fifty-one, and on the wallboard above them a
number went from ninety-three to ninety-four.

Farah slid one earpiece back. "That's a good one," she said to Corinne.
"He's happy."

"Is it the tap or the valve under the sink?"

"I don't know. I'm not a plumber." She said it without offense. "He doesn't
know either. Someone'll look on Thursday."

"What if it needs a part?"

"Then they'll order it." The next call was already flashing. "I've got
forty seconds, sorry."

Lu took Corinne into the glass-walled room at the end of the floor and shut
the door.

"So you've seen what you came to see. She picks a trade off a list and takes
the first slot. And at the yard they say we send them out blind." Lu's voice
was level. "I know what they say at the yard."

"I'm not here to say it."

"You've got it on a wall. Bram rang me." She sat down at last, on the edge of
the table. "Let me tell you what you're asking for when you ask me to stop.
You want me to hold that man's job until somebody understands it. Fine. Who?
Farah can't. I can't. So it waits for a technician to ring him back, and
they're all out on six jobs, so that's tomorrow, or Friday. And meanwhile
what has he got? He rang, a nice girl said 'we'll be in touch,' and his tap's
still running."

"I know."

"You don't. You weren't on the phones." She reached behind her and took a
booklet off the windowsill, soft with handling. The tenant handbook, 2019.
She opened it to a page with the corner turned down and read aloud.
"'When you report a repair, you will not be left wondering. We will give you
an appointment before you put the phone down, and someone will be at your
door within five working days.'" She looked up. "I've got that on the wall by
the coffee machine. It's the only thing this organization ever wrote down
that I could make true with nine people. Who wrote it?"

"I did."

"You did. And it was right. So don't come down here six years later and
tell me it's the problem."

Corinne had been composing an answer and let it go. There was a whiteboard
in the room. She got up and uncapped a marker.

"Can I try something? Tell me if any of it is wrong."

She wrote at the left-hand edge, in a box: /Tenants' homes are repaired soon
and stay repaired./

"That's what it's all for. Yes?"

"Obviously."

To the right of it, two boxes, one above the other, each with an arrow back
to the first.

Top: /Every tenant who reports a fault knows quickly that it is being dealt
with./

"That's yours. Is that fair?"

Lu read it. "That's it exactly."

Bottom: /Every visit finishes the repair./

"That's Mags's. And Bram's, though he doesn't put it that way."

"All right."

"And you can't have homes repaired soon without both. If people don't know
we've heard them, it's 2018. If visits don't finish, it's thirty-four days."

Further right, one more box on each line.

Top: /Book a visit in the next free slot, at once./

Bottom: /Hold the booking until the job is understood and its parts are in
hand./

Between those two she drew a jagged line.

Lu was quiet for long enough that Corinne began to think she had made
things worse.

"That's the first time," Lu said, "that anybody from upstairs has put my
side on a board without the word 'but' after it."

"I don't think there's a 'but'. I think you're both right. That's why it's
still here. Bram can't give up his without thirty-four days getting worse and
you can't give up yours without going back to the answering machine. So
everybody shouts across that line" — she tapped the jagged one — "and
nothing moves."

"So what do we do about it?"

And here Corinne, who had thirteen days, and who had just for the first time
in a month had someone on the other side of a table nod at her, did the
thing she was best at.

"We find the middle," she said.

She got Bram on the speakerphone. It took forty minutes and it was, she
thought at the time, the best piece of work she had done since she was given
the backlog. Lu's side would give a little: the promise would stretch from
five working days to seven, so that the yard had room to get a part in
before the visit. Bram's side would give a little: he would live with five
jobs on a row instead of six in the buildings where they tried it. And calls
could run to five minutes instead of three, so that agents could ask two or
three more questions and write the answers on the order.

"My number goes down," Lu said.

"So does mine," said Bram's voice, tinnily, "and it doesn't finish any more
jobs that I can see."

"It's a week," said Corinne. "Two buildings. 118 Garnet and 9 Pell Court,
starting tomorrow. If it helps we widen it, and I have something to take to
the hearing that both of you signed up to."

There was a pause on the line. "Go on, then," Bram said.

Lu shrugged, which was a yes.

Corinne capped the marker. On the board the five boxes sat with the jagged
line still between the last two; she had not rubbed it out, only stopped
looking at it. She photographed the board for her notes and went out past
the horseshoe of desks feeling lighter than she had in weeks.

Farah caught her eye and pulled an earpiece aside. "Lu says I'm to ask more
questions."

"If you would. On Garnet and Pell Court."

"What questions?"

"About the fault. Whatever would help the technician."

Farah nodded slowly. She was too polite to say it, so it came out as a
question. "Right. So I ask two more things I won't understand the answers
to, and I write them down?"

The next call was flashing. Corinne said they would sort the details out as
they went, and took the stairs.

* What Corinne has just learned

The cause has lasted because two people are each protecting something the
aim cannot do without, and what one must do to protect hers is the opposite
of what the other must do. Drawn as five boxes, neither side has a "but".
Corinne then splits the difference, and everybody agrees.

* Your thinking — Step 10: Draw the conflict

/Ten minutes./

Take your shared cause from step 9. If it has been known for a long time and
is still there, somebody has a good reason to keep it.

1. Find that person, or imagine them fairly. Ask what they would lose if the
   cause were simply removed.
2. Draw five boxes.
   - On the left, the aim from step 2.
   - To its right, two needs, one above the other. Each is something the aim
     cannot do without. One is protected by keeping things as they are; the
     other is damaged by it.
   - To the right of each need, the action that need seems to demand.
3. Draw a jagged line between the two actions. They cannot both be done.
4. Read it aloud to each side: /To have [aim] we must have [need]. To have
   [need] we must [action]./ Change the words until each side says, "Yes,
   that is my position, and you have not made it sound stupid."

Then stop. You will want to find a midpoint between the two actions. Write
the midpoint in the margin if you must, and leave the jagged line where it
is.

* The same step in Org Reason

1. Press ~C-c r t n~ and choose the =conflict= tree. Add the aim with the
   role =objective=, each need with the role =need=, and each of the two
   opposed actions with the role =prerequisite=.
2. Press ~C-c r t l~, tree =conflict=. Link each need to the objective, and
   each action to its need, with the relation =necessary_for=.
3. Link one action to the other with the relation =conflicts_with=.

- [ ] ~C-c r t v~ shows five statements under /conflict/, four
  =necessary_for= relationships and one =conflicts_with=.

*If you keep a trusted system:* the two sides of a lasting conflict often
show up in your own lists as two projects that undo each other. Open ~C-c r
a~ and look for a pair. Do not resolve them yet; write the pair on your
sheet beside the boxes.

* The same step in Reason Commons

A conflict drawn by one side is an accusation. Drawn in a shared record,
each need is stated by the person who holds it.

1. Ask each side to propose their own need and their own action, in their
   own words.
2. Ask each side to accept the wording of the /other/ side's need as a fair
   statement of it. This is not agreeing with it.
3. Record the conflict between the two actions as a relationship both sides
   accept.

- [ ] Each need shows its holder as proposer and the other side as having
  accepted the wording.

* Carry forward

Five boxes and a jagged line, in words both sides accept. Step 11 asks what
is hidden under each arrow.
