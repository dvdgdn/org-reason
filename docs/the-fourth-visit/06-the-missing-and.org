* The chapter

Corinne went to the yard on Friday wanting a culprit, and she knew it, and
went anyway.

She had slept badly. Every time she closed her eyes she was standing in the
boardroom saying /even 4C/. By morning the feeling had hardened into
something easier to carry: a wish to find the person who had taken the valve
off the van.

Mags had told her to bring a coat. She brought one. At a quarter to eight the
yard was full of exhaust and reversing alarms, and among the white Harbor Row
vans were four grey ones she had not seen before, with /Tri-County
Mechanical/ on the doors. Her contractor. Ninety-six thousand dollars of
grey vans, on their fifth day.

Mags had her rear doors open. “Look, then.”

The racking ran down both sides: plastic bins on rails, each with a printed
label. Perhaps a third of the rails held a label and no bin.

“That one was gas valves. That one was pump heads, two sizes. Thermocouples.
Angle valves, three-quarter and one-inch.” Mags touched each empty rail as
though counting teeth. “June. They gave us a new list. A hundred and forty
lines down to eighty-five. Anything that hadn’t moved off a van in ninety
days went back to the cage, and half of what went back to the cage went back
to the supplier.”

“Whose list?”

Mags nodded across the yard.

The stockroom was a steel cage inside the main shed, and beside its counter,
in a raincoat, with a clipboard, stood Wendell Price. The stockroom had
reported to the controller for as long as anyone could remember, on the
theory that stock was money. He was doing the month’s count himself, as he
did every month. He was sixty-one and had the look of a man perpetually
adding something up.

“Ms. Vance.” He initialed a line. “I hear Mr. Marchetti is out of danger. I
was glad.”

“He was visited four times before it was fixed,” Corinne said. “On the
second, the technician knew exactly what was wrong and didn’t have the part.
A one-inch angle valve. It used to be on every heating van.”

“It did.”

“You took it off.”

“I took two hundred and twelve thousand dollars off these shelves and those
vans.” He said it without heat. “May I show you why? It’s quicker.”

He walked her down the cage. At the far end was a bay of dusty cartons.
“Burner assemblies for the boilers at Ostrander Avenue. We replaced those
boilers in 2021. Circulator pumps for a model nobody has made in nine years;
we had thirty. Every van carried three of this and four of that because
somebody, once, had been caught without one. The auditors asked me how much
of it had been touched in a year. Under half.” He turned. “That is rent,
sitting in a bin. I’m not sorry I asked the question.”

It was, she had to admit, the kind of thing she would have praised in a
newsletter.

“But the valve —”

“The valve hadn’t moved in ninety days on most vans. That was the rule. I
didn’t choose the items. The movement did.”

Mags had followed them in. “Rule doesn’t know it’s a winter part and you
counted in June.”

“No,” Wendell agreed. “It doesn’t.” He made a note on the clipboard, which
surprised Corinne. Then he looked at Mags. “Yesterday you fitted that same
valve at Garnet Street. It isn’t on your list today either. How did you come
to have it?”

“Took it from the cage before I went.”

“Why?”

“Because I knew what the job was. It was on the order from the second visit.
Seized one-inch angle.” Mags shrugged. “If I know what I’m going to, I can
carry anything. It’s when I’m sent to ‘radiator cold, plumbing’ that I need
the whole shop on my back.”

Wendell turned to Corinne with his eyebrows slightly raised and said nothing
further, which was worse than if he had.

She had written it in the notebook on the bus, ready: /If the van does not
carry the part, then the visit ends with no repair./ She had been pleased
with it. It was clean, and there was a man at the end of it.

Now she read it over and saw that it was not enough. Mags’s van had not
carried the part yesterday. The visit had ended with a hot radiator.

“It needs something else,” she said, mostly to herself.

“Say it, then,” said Mags.

“If the van doesn’t carry the part — /and/ nobody knew before setting out
which part the job would need — then the visit ends with no repair.”

“Better.”

“Is that everything?” Wendell asked. It was a real question; his pencil was
waiting.

Corinne thought of Dev on the twenty-seventh, kneeling at the radiator,
knowing what he needed, with the cage four miles away.

“He could have driven back for it.”

Mags laughed, once. “On a six-job row? He’d have lost two jobs and Bram would
have had him in the office. You don’t go back. You close it and you move.”

“And there’s no time in the day to fetch one,” Corinne said. Three things,
then, and it took all three. A shorter van list did no harm to a technician
who knew the job. A job nobody understood did no harm to a van that carried
everything. And either could be mended by an hour’s drive, if the day had an
hour in it.

She drew it the way it now had to be drawn: three boxes side by side at the
bottom, their three arrows tied together with a loop before they reached the
box above. Mags, watching, said that was how you drew a three-way valve.

“So it’s all three of us,” Mags said. “Him, and Bram, and whoever books ’em.”

“That’s what it says.”

But it was not, if Corinne was honest, what she heard. A grey Tri-County van
had backed up to the counter while they talked, and a young man in a
Tri-County jacket was signing for a carton. She read the label upside down.
Gas valves. Two.

“Are those the last two?” Mags said sharply.

The storekeeper checked his screen. “Till Wednesday.”

“I’ve got a boiler at Pell Court waiting on one of those since the fifth.”

“He’s got a docket,” the storekeeper said, helplessly. “They’re on the
contract. They draw from us.”

Mags looked at Corinne, and Corinne looked at the empty rails, and at
Wendell, who had gone back to his count. There were three boxes on her page.
Only one of them had a signature on it, and a dollar figure, and a date in
June after which, as everybody at the yard would tell you, things had gone
wrong.

“You’ve drawn three legs,” Mags said quietly as they walked back to the van.
“And you’re only looking at the one.”

“It’s the one somebody decided.”

“They were all decided.”

Corinne got into the passenger seat. The leadership meeting was on Tuesday.
She had four days, a diagram, and an empty chair on Garnet Street, and she
was going to make someone answer for it.

* What Corinne has just learned

A van without the part is no obstacle to a technician who knows the job. The
arrow Corinne wanted needs two more causes beside it before it holds: the job
is unknown, and the day leaves no time to fetch anything. She draws all three
and goes on looking at the one with a name on it.

* Your thinking — Step 6: Find the missing "and"

/Ten minutes./

Take the arrows from step 5 one at a time.

1. Cover the effect with your hand. Read only the cause. Ask: /is this, by
   itself, always enough to produce that?/
2. Look for one occasion when the cause was present and the effect did not
   happen. If you can find one, something else must also be there when it
   does happen.
3. Name the something else. Write it as its own box beside the first, and
   join their two arrows with a small loop before they reach the effect. Read
   it aloud: /If A, and B, then C./
4. Ask once more whether A and B together are enough. Some arrows need
   three.
5. Stop adding when taking away any one of the causes would stop the effect.

Then look at your joined causes and notice which one you find yourself
staring at. It is usually the one that a person decided, on a date. The
others were decided too.

* The same step in Org Reason

1. Add each extra cause with ~C-c r t n~ (=current_reality=, =cause=).
2. Press ~C-c r t l~ and choose =current_reality=. At =Source conditions=
   select all the causes that are only enough together, separated by commas.
   Select the effect as the target and =causes= as the relation. Selecting
   several sources records them as one joint cause.
3. Your earlier single arrow is still there as a proposal. Do not accept it.
   Accept the joint relationship instead when you are satisfied, with
   ~C-c r t a~.

- [ ] ~C-c r t v~ shows at least one relationship with two or more sources
  joined by AND.

*If you keep a trusted system:* each cause in a joint arrow may be looked
after by a different person. Add a line to each person's =agenda= list so
that you raise your own cause with them, and not only theirs.

* The same step in Reason Commons

A joint cause is where a group most easily divides, because each person can
see the others' boxes.

1. Ask each person to state the cause that sits in their own area, and to
   propose it themselves.
2. Propose the joint relationship as one claim about all of them together.
3. If someone accepts "A and B and C together" but not "mainly A", the record
   should show exactly that. Do not let a joint cause be summarized under
   one name.

- [ ] The joint relationship is one item with several sources, and each
  source statement has its own proposer.

* Carry forward

Arrows that each hold only with their "and", and a note of which cause you
keep looking at. Step 7 hands the whole sheet to someone who will try to
break it.
