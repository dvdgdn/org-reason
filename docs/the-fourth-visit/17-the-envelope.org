* The chapter

The Office of Housing Standards held its hearings in a room with a low
ceiling and a flag, and Ms. Petrakis, the hearing officer, ran them like a
woman with eleven more before Christmas.

Monday the fifteenth, ten o’clock. On one side of the table Samira Haddad,
Mr. Dufresne and the exercise book. On the other Gloria Ferreira, the
bundle, and Corinne. Behind them, on the public chairs, Wendell, Lu, Bram in
a tie, and a woman in a port authority jacket.

Gloria spoke first and did not read. Harbor Row had issued
missed-appointment notices to one hundred and thirty-eight households on the
basis of an analysis that had not been tested and had proved wrong. She had
suspended them last night. Each household would have a letter by Wednesday.
The author of the analysis had asked to put a statement on the record.

Corinne read her four paragraphs. It took under a minute. Nobody nodded,
which was a new experience for her in a meeting, and she found she did not
need them to.

Ms. Petrakis made a note. “Thank you. That’s unusual. Ms. Haddad?”

Samira had it written on a card and did not look at it. The council
maintained its complaint about delay. Tenants on Garnet Street had waited,
on the landlord’s own figure, thirty-four days. But the council had taken
part last week in a different way of handling repairs in three buildings and
wished it to continue, in six, for four weeks. And it asked that for those
six buildings the city set aside the count of vans attending within five
days and count instead the days from a tenant’s report to the repair being
finished.

“You’re asking me to stop measuring the landlord on the standard,” Ms.
Petrakis said.

“I’m asking you to measure what we rang up about.”

The hearing officer turned to the other side of the table. “And what does
Harbor Row commit to, in return? I’ll need a figure. Days to completion, by
a date.”

Gloria looked at Corinne. It was the look from the boardroom in November:
/tell them something./ Corinne took the envelope out of her bag.

“I can’t give you a commitment, in the sense of a thing I know we’ll
achieve. I’ve given two of those since November and I’ve just read you what
came of one. I can give you what we expect. It’s in here. There are three
copies: one for you, one for the council, one the director has already
read.” She slid them across. “I’d like them opened on the last day of the
four weeks. Friday the sixteenth of January.”

“What’s in it?”

“Five numbers for the six buildings. How many visits a repair will take.
How many days from report to fixed. How many orders will still be open. How
many tenants will hear what’s wrong, with a date, inside one working day.
And what the five-day attendance figure will be.” She breathed. “That last
one will fall. I expect about seventy percent. I’d rather tell you now than
have you find it.”

“You are telling me in advance that the number I regulate will get worse.”

“In six buildings, yes. And beside the first number I’ve written how sure I
am, which is about seventy percent, and at the bottom I’ve written what
result would mean the idea is wrong and we should stop.”

Ms. Petrakis held the envelope by its edges. She had, Corinne guessed, been
handed a great many action plans across that table, with a great many green
arrows in them.

“If I open this in January and you’ve missed all five?”

“Then you’ll know it on the same day I do, and you’ll know I didn’t change
them afterwards.”

A pause. Ms. Petrakis wrote the date across the flap, signed it, and had
Samira sign hers.

“Adjourned to the sixteenth of January, ten o’clock. No monitor is appointed
in the interim. I’ll count what Ms. Haddad has asked me to count.” She
capped her pen. “Bring the envelopes.”

-----

Four weeks.

A desk appeared in the corner of the dispatch office with two screens and a
headset, and Mags sat at it looking like a bear at a piano, and by Thursday
had stopped complaining. A second technician joined her after the new year.
Farah’s cards went through three printings; the one for radiators acquired a
line in red, /ASK THE TENANT WHAT THEY THINK IT IS/, at Mags’s insistence.
Farah did not mention her notice again and nobody asked.

Mr. Dufresne, with a telephone lent by his granddaughter, climbed to
apartments on four floors of 118 Garnet and held it up to stopcocks and fuse
boxes at the direction of a voice he came to address as /my dear/. Wendell’s
parts went onto the vans in labeled crates. Bram’s wall showed four blocks a
row for the technicians on the six buildings, and for the first week he
could hardly bear to look at it.

There was Christmas. There was a burst pipe at 40 Ostrander on the
twenty-seventh that took three visits whatever anybody did. There was a
Tuesday when the first-call desk had fourteen tenants waiting to be rung
back and Lu came down to the yard herself.

Corinne counted every Friday. She did not send the counts to anyone.

-----

On the sixteenth of January Ms. Petrakis slit the first envelope with a
letter opener and read the sheet aloud, and after each line looked to
Wendell, whom both sides had agreed should bring the figures, since he
trusted nobody’s.

“’Visits per completed repair: one point five or fewer. I am about seventy
percent sure.’”

“One point four,” said Wendell.

“’Days from report to fixed, for new reports: twelve or fewer.’”

“Thirteen.”

“’Open orders in the six buildings: below two hundred and fifty, from three
hundred and eighty-eight.’”

“Two hundred and forty-one.”

“’Tenants told what is wrong and given a date within one working day:
ninety-five percent.’”

“Eighty-one.”

“’Attended within five working days will fall to about seventy percent.’”

“Sixty-six.”

“And at the foot: ‘If visits per repair are still above two, the idea is
wrong and the trial should stop.’” Ms. Petrakis laid the sheet down. “Two of
your four targets met. Two missed.”

“Yes,” Corinne said. She had known for a week, and it was still not pleasant
to hear it in that room. “The days are one over. I thought twelve and it
was thirteen. The call back is a long way under. I said ninety-five and I
had no good reason for ninety-five; I think I wrote it because it sounded
like a promise. Tenants are waiting up to two days for the first call. I
know where that’s coming from and I don’t yet know what to do about it.”

“Ms. Haddad?”

Samira had her own page. “Thirteen days. It was thirty-four.” She let that
stand a moment. “And my ceiling’s been painted. Our log has nineteen visits
to 118 Garnet since the sixteenth of December and seventeen of them finished
the job. The council would like it to carry on.” She glanced across the
table. “We’d also like the call back to be what she said it would be.”

“So would I,” said Lu, from the public chairs, and then apologized to the
hearing officer.

Ms. Petrakis looked from one side to the other. “I don’t often get told
what went wrong by the party it went wrong for,” she said. “The complaint
stays open. No monitor. Harbor Row will report days-to-fixed monthly, for
every building as this is extended, with its expectations for the following
month filed in advance. In an envelope, if you like.” She almost smiled.
“Ms. Ferreira, is that acceptable?”

Gloria said that it was. Then she added something that had not been
rehearsed. “It’s a harder thing to bring to my board than a plan. I’ve
discovered they read it more carefully.”

On the steps outside, Odile was buttoning her jacket against the wind off
the harbor.

“Thirteen,” she said.

“And eighty-one.”

“Were you surprised?”

“By thirteen, a little. By eighty-one —” Corinne thought. “No. I wrote
ninety-five because I wanted it. I knew when I wrote it.”

“That’s worth more than the two you got right.” Odile pulled on her gloves.
“He comes home properly on Tuesday. He’s been home since before Christmas
but they’ve been coming in. Tuesday he’s on his own. He’d like you to see
the radiator.”

* What Corinne has just learned

Asked for a promise, Corinne gives what she expects, with a date, her degree
of confidence, the number that will get worse, and the result that would
prove her wrong. A month later two of four are met and two missed. Because
it was written first and sealed, the misses tell her something: one figure
was a wish, and there is a new delay she had not foreseen.

* Your thinking — Step 17: Say what you expect by when

/Ten minutes now; ten on the date./

Before your change has had time to do anything, write down what you expect.

1. Choose three to five things you could count or see. Take them from the
   top of your chain in step 13.
2. For each, write a number and a date. /By [date], [measure] will be
   [value]./
3. Write at least one thing you expect to get /worse/. Every real change
   has one. Say it before someone else finds it.
4. Beside your main prediction write how sure you are, as a percentage.
5. At the bottom write: /If [this] happens, the idea is wrong and I will
   stop./
6. Seal it, sign it, date it, and give a copy to someone who is not on your
   side.
7. On the date, open it with them. Read each line and the result. For every
   miss, say whether you were unlucky, or wrong about how things work, or
   had written down a wish.

Check your numbers before sealing. If one of them is there because it sounds
like a promise, change it to what you actually expect.

* The same step in Org Reason

1. Press ~C-c r p~ once for each prediction. Give it a title, the prediction
   in full, and the date you will look.
2. Press ~C-c r f~ on each. Where the prediction is a yes-or-no event ("visits
   per repair will be 1.5 or fewer"), give your probability when asked. Org
   Reason stores the original and will not let a command overwrite it.
3. On the date, the dashboard (~C-c r d~) lists each one under predictions
   due, with a link to record the outcome. Or press ~C-c r o~.
4. Record what you observed, whether the event happened, and what you will
   do now.

- [ ] ~C-c r v~ shows each original exactly as you froze it, and the
  dashboard counts it under prediction feedback.

*If you keep a trusted system:* the date goes in your =calendar= list, and
the person who holds your copy goes on your =agenda=. When you record an
outcome from the dashboard you can turn your decision into a next action.
Do; a result that changes nothing on your lists was not worth sealing.

* The same step in Reason Commons

A prediction shared before the result is something a group can be held to
together.

1. Each person who accepted the change states what they expect, separately.
   Do not average them.
2. Fix all of them before anyone sees a result.
3. On the date, record what was observed as an observation, and let each
   person say what they now accept. The accepted view has a history; anyone
   can see what the group believed before and after.

- [ ] Each person's expectation is recorded under their name with the date
  it was fixed.
