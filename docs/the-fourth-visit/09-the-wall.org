* The chapter

Bram Kowalczyk counted the stack himself.

He did it on Monday morning, the first of December, standing at his own desk
with his reading glasses on, and he did all two hundred. Corinne and Mags
waited. Outside, the vans were leaving in a line. When he got to the bottom
he squared the pages and laid them down.

"Four eighty."

"And the old box?" said Mags.

"I'll take your word for the old box." He took his glasses off. "So. More
visits, fewer repairs. Since my board."

"Since March," Corinne said. "I'm not saying because of the board. I said
'because' too early twice already and it has cost a great deal."

"You're not saying it. It's sat there on the paper saying it." He looked at
the wall of colored blocks, twenty-four rows, six to a row. "You know what
they called my lads, before? In the report? 'Underutilized.' I've got a man
who went out on Christmas night to a burst main and he was 'underutilized'.
I put that wall up and nobody's used the word since." His jaw worked. "What
do you want me to do, take it down? Have them sat in vans again with
somebody photographing them?"

"I don't know what I want you to do. I want to find what's underneath it
first."

"Underneath what?"

"All of it."

She had brought a pad of yellow sticky notes and she had the diagram from
Wendell's office, question marks and all, with two of the question marks now
answered. The only bare wall in the dispatch office faced the routing board.
She began putting the boxes up there, one note to a statement, effects at
the top.

/Tenants wait 34 days./ /Open orders 1,912 and rising./ /Tenants ring again./
Below those: /A repair takes 2.4 visits./ Below that, side by side, the ways
a visit ended with nothing mended: /wrong trade/, /no part/, /tenant not
told a time/. And the code that had hidden two-thirds of it: /closed as "no
access"/.

Bram watched with his arms folded. After a while he unfolded them and moved
a note. "That one goes under that one. If he's got the wrong trade he never
gets as far as finding out he's got no part."

"Thank you."

"I'm not helping. I'm correcting."

When every statement was up, Corinne did what she had not known to do in
either of her earlier attempts. She stopped looking at the top and started
at the bottom of each chain, asking the same thing of each note: /and what
is under that?/

Under /wrong trade sent/: the agent picks a trade from a list. Under that:
nobody who could fix the fault hears it described. Under /no part/: three
notes with a loop round them, the short van list, the six-job day, and
/nobody knew before setting out which part the job needed/. Under /tenant
not told a time/, for the twelve who had truly been out: the appointment is
whatever slot is free. And under that: the job is booked the moment the call
comes in.

She stood back.

There were seven troubles at the top of the wall. She traced each one down
with a finger, and Mags did it too from the other end, and they met.

Five of the seven went through the same note. She had written it small, as a
detail, and now she took it down and wrote it again on a fresh one in
capitals and put it at the bottom, by itself.

/THE JOB IS BOOKED AND SENT BEFORE ANYONE WHO COULD FIX IT KNOWS WHAT IT
NEEDS./

"Well, yes," said Bram.

Corinne turned.

"Everybody knows that. You're sent out blind. It's always been like that.
You find out when you get there." He shrugged. "That's the job."

"It's the reason a van with eighty-five lines isn't enough," Mags said. "And
the reason six in a day doesn't leave time. If I knew what I was going to,
your board'd be fine. I could do six."

Bram opened his mouth and shut it again. He was looking at the three notes
with the loop, where his own was the middle one.

"Is it ours?" Corinne asked. "Could anyone in this room change it?"

"Booking's the call center. That's Lu Tran's, and she's Customer Services,
she's not even Gloria's direct." Bram shook his head. "I can't change it and
you can't."

"But we could ask her."

"You could ask her."

So: not within her control, and within her reach. She wrote /Lu/ on the
corner of the note. Two troubles on the wall did not pass through it at all,
and she left them where they were and did not pretend otherwise.

Her phone had been buzzing in her bag for some minutes. She took it out and
it was all there at once, the way bad news arrives once you have stopped
watching for it.

From Wendell, forwarded without comment: Tri-County Mechanical's first
fortnightly return. Three hundred and ten visits. One hundred and seven
repairs completed. She did the division before she could stop herself. Two
point nine. Worse than her own technicians, at twenty-four thousand dollars
so far. Below it Wendell had added one line: /In-house completions fell to
215 a week over the same fortnight. They draw from the same shelves./

She worked it out on a sticky note. Five hundred and eighty reports in.
Four hundred and thirty repairs by Harbor Row, a hundred and seven by the
contractor. The backlog had been 1,972 when the grey vans arrived and was
2,015 now.

From the front desk at Tolland Avenue: a courier envelope from the city's
Office of Housing Standards. /Shall I open it?/ She rang, and the
receptionist read it to her. A complaint lodged by the Garnet Street
Tenants' Council on behalf of two hundred and twelve households, concerning
repair delays and the issue of missed-appointment notices. Hearing: Monday
the fifteenth of December, ten o'clock.

And a voicemail from Gloria, forty seconds long, which Corinne played with
the phone against her ear and her back to the room.

/I've seen the contractor's return. I've given them two weeks' notice, so
that is forty-eight thousand dollars by the time they're gone, for a backlog
that went up. I've had sixty-one written disputes of those notices. And I've
got a hearing in a fortnight. Corinne, I told the board it was access because
you told me. If the city puts a monitor in here, the monitor decides how we
run repairs, not me and not you. You have until the hearing to give me
something I can stand on./

She put the phone face down on Bram's desk.

"Bad?" said Mags.

"Fourteen days."

Bram was still in front of the yellow wall. He had his hands in his fleece
pockets and was reading it from the top down, the way she had first drawn
it, and then, she saw, from the bottom up.

"If everybody's always known that," Corinne said, pointing at the capitals,
"why is it still there?"

"Because you've got to send somebody."

"Why?"

"Because —" He stopped. He looked genuinely stuck, as a man does when he has
been asked why water is wet. "You'd have to ask her. She'll have her
reasons. She always does."

* What Corinne has just learned

Starting from the bottom of each chain and asking what lies under it,
Corinne finds that five of her seven troubles pass through one statement,
and that everyone at the yard already knew it. She cannot change it herself;
she can reach the person who could. Meanwhile her first explanation has cost
forty-eight thousand dollars and a city hearing.

* Your thinking — Step 9: Follow the arrows down

/Fifteen minutes. A wall and sticky notes help./

1. Put every box from your diagram on its own note. Troubles at the top.
2. Start at the bottom of each chain. Ask of the lowest note: /and what is
   under that?/ Add what you find. Stop when the answer is outside the
   situation altogether (the weather, the law, the past).
3. Now take each trouble at the top and trace down from it with your finger.
   Mark each note you pass through.
4. Find the note that the most troubles pass through. Rewrite it large. It
   need not explain all of them; say honestly which ones it leaves out.
5. Ask of that note: is this mine to change? If not, can I reach the person
   whose it is? If neither, go up one note and ask again.
6. Write beside it: /working hypothesis/, and the date.

Notice whether the note you have arrived at is something everybody already
knows. That is common, and it is not a reason to doubt it. It is the next
question: if it is known, why is it still there?

* The same step in Org Reason

1. Add any deeper causes you found with ~C-c r t n~ and join them with
   ~C-c r t l~ as before.
2. Press ~C-c r t v~ and trace each trouble down through the relationships
   shown.
3. On the shared cause, press ~C-c r t ?~, choose =assumption=, and record
   that it is a working hypothesis, the date, and which troubles it does not
   account for.
4. If the challenges on the items leading to it are answered, accept them
   with ~C-c r t a~, then press ~C-c r t g~ to see the accepted diagram.

- [ ] One cause has several troubles above it, and carries your note that it
  is a hypothesis.

*If you keep a trusted system:* this cause is, for now, the thing most worth
your effort. Capture it with ~C-c r c~ beginning /Working hypothesis:/ and
clarify it as =hypothesis=, so it is kept as a note and not a task. Then look
at your projects in ~C-c r a~ and ask which of them touch it at all.

* The same step in Reason Commons

A group's shared cause is whatever the group has actually accepted, which
may be less than one member believes.

1. Trace the troubles down together, each person tracing the ones they
   proposed.
2. Ask who accepts the shared cause as a hypothesis. Record each acceptance
   by name.
3. Record who can change it and who can only ask, as observations.

- [ ] The accepted diagram shows the shared cause only as far as people have
  accepted the items that lead to it.

* Carry forward
