#+title: The Fourth Visit — find what to change
#+startup: showall
#+options: toc:nil num:nil

/The Fourth Visit/ is a novella in which Corinne Vance is handed a housing
landlord's repair backlog. Every team is hitting its targets, tenants wait
thirty-four days, and each sensible improvement has made the wait longer. She
learns, by getting it wrong twice, the way of reasoning that Eliyahu Goldratt
called the thinking processes: how to find what to change, what to change it
to, and how to cause the change. The whole novella is here, one chapter to a
page, and each chapter is followed by an exercise. By chapter 18 you have
worked one stubborn problem of your own from a list of troubles to a tested
change.

You need nothing but this and a pen. If you have read /Three Hundred Seats/
or keep a trusted system of your own, each page says where the two meet.

* How each page works

Every page has the same six parts.

- *The chapter* :: the story itself.
- *What Corinne has just learned* :: the chapter's lesson in a few lines. It
  gives away nothing from later chapters.
- *Your thinking — Step N* :: the exercise in general terms, for pen and
  paper. It is complete in itself.
- *The same step in Org Reason* :: the same exercise with Org Reason's
  commands, on your real records. One paragraph in it, marked /If you keep a
  trusted system/, says what to do with the step's result in your lists.
  Skip it if you keep none.
- *The same step in Reason Commons* :: what changes when you do the step with
  other people, or with an assistant, in a shared record.
- *Carry forward* :: what to keep for the next step.

Each step uses what the earlier steps produced, so do them in order, on one
problem. Most take ten or fifteen minutes. Steps 7, 14 and 15 need another
person.

The story is in four parts: Busy (chapters 1–4), Two Explanations (5–9), The
Conflict (10–14) and The Test (15–18). The [[file:appendix.org][appendix]] shows how the story was
built and sums up the method with its usual names. Read it last.

* What you have at the end

One problem, reasoned through in three movements.

- *What to change* (steps 1–9): an aim and its conditions, the troubles that
  damage them, a diagram of causes that someone else has tried to break, and
  the one cause most troubles share.
- *What to change it to* (steps 10–14): the conflict that has kept that cause
  in place, the assumption under it that can be given up, a change that meets
  both sides' needs, and the same change altered by the person it would have
  hurt.
- *How to cause the change* (steps 15–18): the obstacles in order, a first
  action, an expectation written before the result, and the habit of asking
  where the difficulty has gone.

* Before you start with Org Reason

You need Org Reason 0.2.0 or later with =org-reason-mode= on (see [[file:../install.md][install.md]]).
The keys below assume the default prefix, ~C-c r~; ~C-c r ?~ shows the menu.
The tree commands under ~C-c r t~ need Python 3 with PyYAML, which ~C-c r t P~
sets up.

A statement in a commons cannot be reworded or deleted from Emacs. To change
your mind you propose the better wording and accept that one; the earlier
wording stays visible as something that was said. The steps are written with
that in mind. [[file:../reasoncommons.md][reasoncommons.md]] explains how to edit the file directly when
you must.

These exercises use your real workspace. If you would rather learn the keys
on invented data first, ~C-c r h~ opens the [[file:../user-guide.org][tutorial]] in a separate Emacs.

* Before you start with Reason Commons

[[https://reasoncommons.com/][Reason Commons]] is a method and a shared record for reasoning with other
people. A commons made with ~C-c r t c~ is an ordinary Reason Commons file:
the same record can be opened with the Reason Commons skill and an assistant,
or by a colleague, in turn. The sections on each page describe what to do
there in general terms, because the wording of its commands changes between
versions. They were written against the upstream revision pinned in
=vendor/reasoncommons/UPSTREAM.json=.

You can do every step without it. It earns its place from step 7 onward,
when the reasoning has to survive other people.

* A note on sources

The method taught here is Eliyahu M. Goldratt's, as developed by others,
notably H. William Dettmer. The story, its characters and its examples are
original. This novella was written from general knowledge of the method and
has not been checked line by line against the books; the [[file:appendix.org][appendix]] lists
them. It is independent and not affiliated with any of the authors or their
organizations.
