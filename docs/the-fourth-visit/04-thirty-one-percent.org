* The chapter

Aldo Marchetti’s living room had a bay window, a wall of books in two
languages, a television with a blanket folded on top of it, and a cast-iron
radiator under the window that was as cold as a railing.

Corinne laid her palm on it because it seemed rude not to.

“You see,” Mr. Marchetti said from his chair, with satisfaction. He was
eighty-one and narrow, wearing a cardigan over a sweater over a shirt, and
had been a typesetter for forty years at a printer’s that no longer existed.
“The bedroom one is good. The kitchen one is good. This one has a
disagreement with the building.”

Odile had invited her. /I take him supper on Mondays. If you have tagged his
radiator you should at least meet it./ She was in the kitchen now, visible
through the doorway, decanting soup. On the counter beside her a hearing aid
sat in a small lit cradle, and its twin was in her father’s left ear.

“Tomorrow,” Corinne told him, turning that way so he could see her mouth.
“Tuesday. First job of the day, with the right part this time.”

“What o’clock is first?”

“In the morning. I’m finding out exactly.” She had the note in her bag and
had emailed the dispatcher from the bus. There was no reply yet.

“I will be here,” Mr. Marchetti said, as a man might who had been asked
whether he would be attending his own birthday.

They ate at a table by the cold window, in coats. Afterwards Odile washed up
and Corinne, because the board met in three days and she could not help it,
took out the form with the seven lines and a second sheet with her four
conditions and put them side by side on the cloth.

The list had grown since the counter. She had spent Friday asking people at
the yard and in the call center what kept going wrong, and they had told her.

/The new uniform order is two months late./
/The tenant app is an ugly green and nobody uses it./
/There is nowhere to park at the yard after 7:30./

Mr. Marchetti put on a second pair of glasses and leaned over. “That is a
galley,” he said. “Ten lines. And these?” He tapped the conditions. “Four
heads. Each line goes under a head or it goes on the floor.”

Odile, drying a bowl, did not correct him.

So Corinne did it his way. She took the first of her four, /We know what the
repair needs before we go/, and asked each trouble whether it was damage to
that. /The wrong kind of technician is sent./ Obviously. /Nobody knows how
many visits a repair takes./ Yes, in its way: it was a thing not known.

/The person and the parts arrive together./ Under that went /Technicians
arrive without the part./

/The tenant can let us in at a time they agreed./ Under that went /31 percent
of visits are closed as “no access.”/

Three lines belonged under no condition because they were the aim itself
going unmet: thirty-four days, the rising count of open orders, the tenants
who rang again. She wrote them across the top.

That left the uniforms, the green app and the car park.

“Which head?” said Mr. Marchetti.

“None of them. But people are really annoyed about the parking.”

“I was annoyed for forty years about the coffee at Delacroix and Sons. We
still got the paper out.” He drew a neat line through all three with her pen
and handed it back. “Floor.”

She looked at what remained and saw it at once. It was the only place on the
sheet where a number sat squarely on top of a condition. Nearly a third of
all visits. Six hundred and twenty-four visits a week, and close to two
hundred of them ended at a closed door. If those two hundred had been
repairs —

“That’s it,” she said. “That’s the backlog. It’s access.”

Odile hung up the dish towel and came to look. She read the sheet for some
time.

“It’s a trouble sitting next to the thing it damages,” she said. “You’ve done
that well. I don’t see a cause on this page.”

“The cause is that tenants aren’t there when we come.”

“Is that written here?”

“It’s what ‘no access’ means.”

“Is it?” Odile looked, briefly, at her father, who was folding his glasses.
“He’s had three visits. Was he a ‘no access’?”

Corinne had the laptop. Visit three, the thirty-first: /No access. 09:14./

“On the thirty-first, yes.”

“Papa. Did anyone come on Halloween? The Friday.”

Mr. Marchetti considered. “Someone came on a Friday. Or it was the Thursday.
I heard the buzzer late, I think. I was in the chair.” He lifted one shoulder.
“I am not always quick.”

It was not nothing, and Corinne felt for him. But it was also exactly what
the number said: a technician, a door, a tenant who did not get to it in
time. Multiplied by two hundred a week.

“I have to tell the board something on Thursday,” she said. “This is the
biggest number on the page and it’s the only one that explains the others.”

“Does it explain them? Or is it one of them?”

“Odile, I have three days.”

“Yes.” Odile did not argue further. She put the lid on the soup container.
“You do.”

-----

On Tuesday at 8:15 the dispatcher’s answer arrived, to a question that no
longer mattered. /Dev was at 4C 7:40, first job like you asked. Knocked, no
answer. Coded NA. He’s got five more today, can’t go back./

Corinne read it standing up. She thought of the hearing aid in its cradle on
the kitchen counter, and then, to her lasting shame, she thought: /even 4C./
A red tag, the right technician, the right valve, and still nobody at the
door.

She put it in the paper.

The board met on Thursday the thirteenth and it was the best hour she had had
in that room. The paper was called /Why the Backlog Grows/ and it had one
chart. She told them about a repair she had personally expedited, with the
part in the technician’s hand, that had ended on a doormat. Heads nodded
around the table. It was a relief to everyone, including Corinne, that the
answer was so plain.

They voted on two measures. Households with two or more missed appointments
since the first of September would get a written notice, and a third would
cost thirty-five dollars. And since the backlog would not clear itself, a
contractor, Tri-County Mechanical, would be brought in for eight weeks from
Monday at a cost of ninety-six thousand dollars.

Wendell Price, the controller, voted against the second. “We are buying more
visits,” he said in his dry way, “before anyone has shown me that visits are
what we’re short of.” Nobody took it up. Gloria squeezed Corinne’s arm on the
way out and said, “That’s what I needed.”

The notices went to one hundred and thirty-eight households. Building staff
delivered them by hand on Friday so that nobody could say they had been lost.

On Saturday morning Corinne was in a supermarket queue when her phone lit.

/I’m at the gate. My father has just read me a letter over the phone. It says
he has missed two appointments and that a third will cost him $35. He has not
left the apartment since the first week of October./

A second message followed before she could think.

/I’m not asking for anything. Boarding now./

* What Corinne has just learned

Setting each trouble under the condition it damages clears away three real
annoyances and leaves seven lines that matter. But a trouble beside its
condition is still a trouble. Corinne takes the largest one for a cause, the
board agrees with her, and a letter is on Aldo's table.

* Your thinking — Step 4: Set each trouble beside its condition

/Ten minutes./

Put your troubles from step 1 next to your conditions from step 3.

1. Write each condition as a heading.
2. Take each trouble and ask which condition it is damage to. Write it under
   that heading.
3. A trouble that is the aim itself going unmet (the result is late, or
   missing, or has to be done again) goes across the top.
4. A trouble that fits under no heading and is not the aim going unmet is an
   irritation. Draw a line through it. It may be worth fixing one day. It is
   not what this work is about.
5. Keep between five and eight.

Now the uncomfortable part. Look at the sheet and notice which trouble you
want to call the reason for the rest. Write under it: /this is a trouble, not
yet a cause./ You have not shown what produces anything. The next five steps
do that.

* The same step in Org Reason

A statement in a commons cannot be reworded from Emacs, so restating is done
by proposing the better wording and accepting only what you keep.

1. For each trouble you reworded, press ~C-c r t n~ (=current_reality=,
   =undesirable_effect=) and enter the new wording.
2. Press ~C-c r t a~ and accept each trouble you are keeping. Read the exact
   wording shown before you confirm.
3. Leave the irritations and the old wordings as proposals. They stay visible
   in ~C-c r t v~ as things that were said and not taken up.
4. Keep your paper sheet with its headings. The commons holds the troubles
   and the conditions in two separate trees; the sheet is where you see which
   trouble sits under which condition.

- [ ] Five to eight troubles are accepted; the rest remain proposed.

*If you keep a trusted system:* an irritation you crossed out may still be
something you want done. Capture it with ~C-c r c~ and clarify it as
=someday= or a =next-action=. It leaves this problem and goes where small
jobs belong.

* The same step in Reason Commons

Dropping a line is easy alone and delicate in a group, because every
irritation belongs to someone.

1. Go through the troubles together and ask of each: which condition does
   this damage?
2. Where the group cannot name one, do not delete the statement. Leave it
   proposed, unaccepted, with its author.
3. Record any disagreement about whether something is an irritation as an
   open objection on that statement.

- [ ] Accepted troubles each name a condition; unaccepted ones are still
  there, with their authors.

* Carry forward

Five to eight troubles, each under a condition or across the top, and a note
on the one you are tempted to call the cause. Step 5 begins joining them.
