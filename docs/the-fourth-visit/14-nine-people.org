* The chapter

Farah had put her notice on Lu’s desk at nine o’clock on Thursday, typed, in
an envelope, and by the time Corinne came down at ten the whole horseshoe
knew.

“Two weeks,” Lu said. She was in the glass room with the door shut and she
did not ask Corinne to sit. “Six years. She trained five of the others. Do
you want to know what she said?”

“Yes.”

“She said: ‘Yesterday I took eleven calls from Garnet Street. Eleven times I
said, I’ll put you through to someone who can help you. So what was I doing
before?’” Lu’s voice was quite steady. “I didn’t have an answer for her. Do
you?”

Corinne had come down with an answer. It was a good one, about how nobody’s
job was at risk and how the agents’ role would /evolve/. She had got as far
as the stairs with it. She thought of Bram holding up his hand.

“No,” she said. “I should have been down here on Monday, before I asked you
to route a single call. I started it in three buildings and told you by
email. I’m sorry. That’s not an answer either.”

Lu looked at her for a moment and then away, through the glass, at her nine.

“Mags was in the yard yesterday on a telephone,” she said. “I hear it went
well. I hear a woman on Garnet Street had her leak found in six minutes. I’m
not stupid, Corinne. I can see it’s better for the tenant. I can also see
what it does in here, and you can’t, because it isn’t on your wall.”

“Then put it on.”

“What?”

The whiteboard still had Tuesday’s five boxes on it, a little smeared. The
marker was on the ledge. Corinne picked it up and held it out, cap first.

“I’ve got a chain that runs from Mags on a phone up to everything I want.
Bram built half of it. There’s nothing on it that goes wrong. That can’t be
right. You know what goes wrong. I’d like it in your handwriting, not mine.”

For a second she thought Lu would refuse. Then Lu took the marker, wiped a
space with the side of her fist, and wrote fast, in block capitals, from the
bottom.

/TECHNICIAN TAKES THE FIRST CALL./

/AGENTS STOP DIAGNOSING AND BOOKING. THEY ANSWER AND TRANSFER./

/THE JOB IS A SWITCHBOARD. THE GOOD ONES LEAVE./

“That’s Farah. That’s today. Keep going?”

“Keep going.”

/NEW STARTERS, NO ONE TO TRAIN THEM. CALLS NOT ANSWERED IN 20 SECONDS./

/TENANTS HOLD. TENANTS GIVE UP./

And at the top, underlined twice: /2018./

She stepped back. “There. Every arrow. You can check them like you checked
Wendell’s.”

Corinne read it from the bottom, aloud, with /if/ and /then/, because she
had promised herself she would always do that now. Each one went. It was the
same chain as her own, the same first note, and it climbed just as steadily
to the place Lu had spent six years climbing out of.

“It’s right,” she said.

“I know it’s right.”

“I don’t know what to do about it.”

“No.” Something in Lu’s shoulders came down a little. She capped the marker
and uncapped it again. “That’s the first time you’ve said that in this
room.”

She stood looking at her own second line. /AGENTS STOP DIAGNOSING./ Then
she said, not to Corinne exactly:

“They don’t diagnose now. That’s the thing. Farah asks if it’s hot or cold
and picks off a list. She’s good at people. Nobody ever gave her anything to
be good at about a tap.” She tapped the board. “What did Mags ask that woman?
Exactly.”

Corinne had it in her notebook. “Where’s the wet. Does it drip all the time
or when someone upstairs lets a bath out. Can you show me.”

“Three questions.”

“And then she knew where to point the camera.”

“Farah could ask those three questions.” Lu was speaking faster. “She
couldn’t have made them up. But if someone gave them to her. How many
different faults are there, really? Not the strange ones. The ones we take
all day.”

“Bram would know.”

They rang him. He put Mags on. Mags thought about it for some time and said
a dozen. Dripping tap, blocked sink, blocked toilet, no hot water, radiator
cold, radiator leaking, bath or shower leak, extractor fan, door lock,
window won’t shut, lights out in one room, damp patch. Between them, about
six calls in ten. For most of those she could say on one side of a card what
to ask, what to look at, and which answers meant which part.

“And the other four in ten come to you,” Lu said into the speaker.

“The other four come to me.”

Lu rubbed out her second line with two fingers and wrote under it.

/AGENTS TAKE THE FIRST CALL FOR THE TWELVE COMMON FAULTS, FROM MAGS’S CARDS.
THE REST GO TO THE TECHNICIAN’S DESK./

She considered it. Then, lower down, squeezed in:

/AGENTS OWN THE CALL BACK. “YOU WILL HEAR FROM US WITHIN ONE WORKING DAY WITH
WHAT IS WRONG AND A DATE.”/

“That’s a promise,” she said. “It’s a different one from yours. It’s one I
can keep with nine people, and it’s about something. And nobody in here is a
switchboard.” She drew a line from it that went up past /THE GOOD ONES LEAVE/
and did not touch it. “If you’d come to me with that on Monday I’d have said
yes.”

“I didn’t have it on Monday. I couldn’t have had it. It’s yours.”

Lu opened the door and called Farah in, and showed her the board, and Farah
read it standing with her arms crossed.

“Who writes the cards?”

“You and Mags,” said Lu.

“I’ve handed my notice in.”

“I know you have.”

Farah looked at the twelve faults, which Corinne had scribbled down the
margin. “The fan one’s wrong. Nobody says ‘extractor fan’. They say the
bathroom’s steamy.” She uncrossed her arms. “I’ll write the cards. I’m not
saying I’m staying.”

-----

At half past three Mags fitted a seven-dollar trap under a bath in 3B, and
sent Corinne a photograph of Karim Haddad holding the old one.

At ten the next morning, Friday, Gloria asked her to come up.

The director had a single sheet on her desk: the weekly return that went to
the city. One line was highlighted. /Attended within five working days —
116, 118, 120 Garnet Street — 58%./

“Three days,” Gloria said. “It was ninety-one.”

“Twenty-six jobs are being held until the parts are in. They’ll each be done
in one visit. The measure counts them as late because nobody’s knocked on
the door.”

“I understand what it counts.” Gloria turned the sheet to face her. “On
Monday I sit in front of a hearing officer on a complaint about delays. I
have one number that says Harbor Row does not leave people waiting. One.
You’ve put a hole in it, in the three buildings that filed.”

“Because it was measuring the wrong —”

“Corinne.” It was said gently. “I think you may be right this time. I
thought so the last two times. I can’t take ‘I think she’s right’ into that
room with a figure of fifty-eight percent on the table and a monitor waiting
to be appointed.” She laid her hand flat on the sheet. “Stop it today. Garnet
Street goes back to normal booking this afternoon. After the hearing, we’ll
see.”

“If it goes back, those twenty-six get a van on Monday with no parts.”

“Then they get a van on Monday.”

Corinne went down one floor. Through the glass she could see Lu and Farah at
the whiteboard with a stack of index cards, arguing happily about a toilet.

She stood in the corridor and did not go in.

* What Corinne has just learned

The person a change will hurt can draw the harm better than anyone, and is
the one most likely to see how to alter the change so that it stops. Lu's
chain is as sound as Corinne's own. Lu rewrites the change, and it becomes
hers. Corinne asked three days late, which cost Farah's notice. Then the
change breaks the one number the director is relying on.

* Your thinking — Step 14: Ask who it hurts

/Fifteen minutes, with the person concerned./

Look at your chain from step 13 and ask who is not on it.

1. Name the person or group who would lose something if your change went
   ahead exactly as written: work they are proud of, a number they are
   judged by, a promise they have kept.
2. Go to them before you start anything. Say: /I think this hurts you and I
   can't see how. Would you draw it?/
3. Give them the pen. Have them write the chain from your change up to the
   harm, in their own words. Read it back aloud with "if" and "then". Do not
   reassure. If the arrows hold, say so.
4. Ask: /what would have to be different about the change for that not to
   happen?/ Let them answer. Their alteration is usually better than yours.
5. Rewrite the change with the alteration in it. Check both needs from step
   10 again.
6. Ask once more who is not on the page. There is often a second person.

If you have already started, as most people have by this step, stop and go
now. It costs more every day you leave it.

* The same step in Org Reason

1. Press ~C-c r t n~, tree =future_reality=, role =undesirable_effect=, for
   each step of the harm. Join them from the injection upward with
   ~C-c r t l~ and the relation =causes=.
2. Enter the altered change as a second =injection=.
3. Link the altered injection to the first harmful effect it prevents with
   the relation =overcomes=.
4. Leave the original injection unaccepted. Accept the altered one only when
   the person it affected has agreed the wording.

- [ ] /Future reality/ shows a harmful branch, a second injection, and an
  =overcomes= relationship between them.

*If you keep a trusted system:* the conversation is the action. Put it on
that person's =agenda= list today, ahead of anything that starts the change.
If you find you have next actions that begin the change before that
conversation, move them under it as later steps.

* The same step in Reason Commons

Here the shared record matters most: the harm belongs to the person who
would suffer it.

1. Invite them into the commons and ask them to add the harmful branch
   themselves, under their own name.
2. Their alteration to the change is their proposal, not an edit to yours.
3. Your original stays in the record, unaccepted. Anyone reading later can
   see what was first proposed, who objected, and what replaced it.

- [ ] The harmful effects and the altered injection carry the affected
  person's name as proposer.

* Carry forward

A change rewritten by the person it would have hurt, and the names of anyone
else it reaches. Step 15 lists what stands in the way.
