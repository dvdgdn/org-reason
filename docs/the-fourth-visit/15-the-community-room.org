* The chapter

“I’m not doing this by telephone,” Samira Haddad had said on Friday night,
when Corinne rang to tell her the trial was stopped and that twenty-six
Garnet Street jobs would go back to ordinary booking on Monday. “Tomorrow.
Two o’clock. The community room. Bring whoever you’ve got.”

Corinne had not expected to have anyone. She sent four messages that said
only where and when, and that it was a Saturday and nobody was obliged.

The community room at 118 Garnet was on the ground floor behind the
mailboxes: stacking chairs, a tea urn, a noticeboard, a radiator that worked.
By two there were nine people in it.

Samira, with the exercise book. A heavy, courteous man in a cardigan whom
she introduced as Mr. Dufresne from 4A, the floor captain whose round
handwriting Corinne already knew. Mags in a jacket that was not Harbor Row’s.
Lu and Farah together, with a shoebox of index cards. Bram, last through the
door, looking like a man visiting a relative in hospital. And Wendell Price
in his raincoat with a folder, who nodded to her and sat near the urn.

At the back, by the window, Odile had taken a chair without being asked and
without explaining. She had a newspaper.

Corinne had been up until one making a plan. It was on a single page and it
was in order: first the director, then the hearing, then the stock, then the
cards, then the start date. At the bottom, after a gap, /review of
missed-appointment notices/. She stood up with it.

“Thank you all for coming. I’ve put together a sequence of —”

“Before your list,” said Samira. She had not raised her voice. “You’ve been
stopped. So something’s in the way. I’d like to hear what, from everybody.
One each. Then we’ll see about your list.”

Corinne sat down.

Mr. Dufresne, it turned out, had brought a roll of lining paper and a tin of
markers from the children’s cupboard. He taped a length of it to the wall.

“The director’s stopped it,” Corinne said. “That’s mine.”

He wrote it up. /Director has stopped the trial./

“City counts a van at the door inside five days,” said Lu. “Doesn’t matter
what the van does. While that’s the count, we look worse the better we do
it.”

/City counts “attended within 5 days.”/

“Vans haven’t got the parts,” said Mags. “I can know exactly what a job
wants and it’s still on a truck.”

“My row,” Bram said, when it came to him. He cleared his throat. “Her row.
Mags’s. If she’s on a telephone, six jobs a day don’t get done unless I move
them, and I can move them for three buildings. I can’t for the whole
estate.”

“Cards aren’t finished,” said Farah. “We’ve done seven. Mags hasn’t seen
four of them.”

Mr. Dufresne wrote each one. Then he put the marker down and spoke for
himself.

“I have a telephone,” he said. “It has a cord. Half this building is my age.
You want us to show you the pipe on a camera. With what?”

/Many tenants have no camera phone./

That left Samira. She did not hurry.

“There are a hundred and thirty-eight households holding a letter from
Harbor Row that says they don’t open their doors. Forty-odd of them are in
these three buildings. I’ve got a notebook that says most of them were
home.” She laid her hand on it. “I am not going up those stairs to ask my
neighbours to point their telephones at their ceilings for the people who
sent that letter. Not while it stands. Write that down, Mr. Dufresne.”

He wrote it. /138 households hold a notice that calls them absent./

Seven lines. Corinne looked from the lining paper to her own page. Six of
the seven were on it. The seventh was at the bottom, after a gap.

“That one I can’t do before Monday,” she said. “I don’t have the authority
to withdraw a board decision. After the hearing —”

“Then we’re finished here,” Samira said, pleasantly, and began to gather
her things.

“Hold on.” It was Lu. “Hold on. Nobody’s said what any of these would look
like if it wasn’t in the way.” She got up, took a marker of a different
color, and went to the paper. “This is what we did on Thursday with mine.
You don’t say ‘it’s a problem’. You say what’d be true instead.” She pointed
at Mr. Dufresne’s own line. “No camera phone. So what would have to be true?”

“Someone comes up with one,” he said slowly. “A floor captain. I’d need
lending one.”

“Or the caretaker,” said Mags. “He’s got a phone and a master key.”

Lu wrote it beside the obstacle, in green. /A floor captain or the caretaker
goes up with a phone./

They went down the wall like that, and it was not Corinne who did it. /Vans
lack parts/ became /Six buildings’ worth of parts are on the vans that serve
them/, and at that Wendell opened his folder. The contractor had been given
notice. Of the ninety-six thousand dollars, forty-eight thousand would never
be spent. He had priced the stock for six buildings on Friday evening:
thirty-eight thousand. “I can release it the morning the director authorizes
a trial,” he said. “I can’t release it against a trial she has stopped. I
would be the first to object.”

“Six buildings?” said Bram. “I said three.”

“We’ve got floor captains with phones in Pell Court, nine and eleven,” said
Samira, “and at 40 Ostrander. If this happens, it happens where we can make
it work. That’s six.”

Bram looked at the ceiling, and nodded.

/City counts attended within five days/ became /The hearing agrees to count
something else in six buildings for a month./

“And who asks the hearing for that?” Odile said from the back. It was the
only thing she said all afternoon, and she did not lower her newspaper.

There was a short silence.

“It’s our complaint,” said Samira. “If the landlord asks to be measured
differently, that’s a landlord wriggling. If the tenants’ council stands up
and says, we would rather be counted by days-till-it’s-fixed, in our own
buildings, for a month — that’s ours to say.” She looked at Corinne. “And I
will not say it while that letter stands.”

So it came round again. But now it was different, and Corinne saw how. On
her page the letters had been a courtesy, to be seen to when the real work
was done. On the wall they were underneath everything. No withdrawal, no
council at the hearing. No council, no new count. No new count, nothing for
Gloria to stand on. No Gloria, no money from Wendell. She had had the order
upside down.

Mr. Dufresne was numbering them as people called it out, arguing only once,
over whether the cards came before the parts. They did not depend on each
other, Farah pointed out, so it did not matter.

/1. The notices are withdrawn, in writing, to each household./
/2. The council tells the hearing it will take part./
/3. The director allows six buildings for four weeks./
/4. The hearing agrees to a different count for those buildings./
/5. Parts for six buildings are on the vans. — Mr. Price/
/5. Twelve cards finished and checked. — Farah, Mags/
/5. Floor captains and caretakers have phones. — Mrs. Haddad/
/6. Mags’s row is covered. — Mr. Kowalczyk/
/7. First calls start, Tuesday the sixteenth./

“Number one’s got no name on it,” said Mags.

Everyone looked at Corinne.

“The board voted for those notices,” she said. “I can’t unvote them.”

“Who asked the board to?” said Samira.

The urn ticked. At the back of the room a page of newspaper turned.

* What Corinne has just learned

Corinne brings a plan in order and is made to hear the obstacles first, one
from each person. Each obstacle is turned into a state of affairs that would
be true if it were gone, by the people it belongs to. When they ask which
must come before which, the item at the bottom of Corinne's page turns out
to be the one everything else stands on, and it is the only one with no name
beside it.

* Your thinking — Step 15: Put the obstacles in order

/Twenty minutes, with the people who will have to act./

1. Do not bring a plan. Bring your altered change from step 14 and a large
   sheet of paper.
2. Ask each person: /what is in the way?/ One each, then round again until
   there are no more. Write them in their words. Include the one that is
   about you.
3. Beside each obstacle write what would be true if it were not in the way.
   A state of affairs, not a task: /the parts are on the van/, not /order
   parts/. Let the person who named the obstacle say it.
4. Now ask of each pair: /does this one have to be true before that one can
   be?/ Number them. Things that do not depend on each other share a number.
5. Put a name beside each.
6. Read from the top: /once this is true, then this can be./ Change the order
   wherever it does not read.

Compare the order on the wall with the order you would have written alone.
The item you had put last, or left off, is worth a second look. So is any
line with no name on it.

* The same step in Org Reason

1. Press ~C-c r t n~ and choose the =prerequisite= tree. Enter your altered
   change with the role =objective=.
2. Add each obstacle with the role =obstacle=, and each "what would be true
   instead" with the role =intermediate_objective=.
3. Press ~C-c r t l~, tree =prerequisite=. Link each intermediate objective to
   its obstacle with =overcomes=.
4. Link each intermediate objective to the one that must follow it with
   =precedes=, and the last ones to the objective with =necessary_for=.

- [ ] ~C-c r t v~ shows every obstacle with something that overcomes it, and
  a chain of =precedes= ending at the objective.

*If you keep a trusted system:* the altered change is now a project. Press
~C-c r C~, capture its outcome in one line, and clarify it as =project= with
the first thing-to-achieve that is yours as its first action. Things that
other people own go in as =delegate= entries with their names, so they
appear under waiting and not among your own actions.

* The same step in Reason Commons

An order worked out by the people who must act is one they will keep to.

1. Each person proposes the obstacles they can see and the state that would
   remove each.
2. Ordering claims ("this before that") are relationships like any other.
   Whoever sees the dependency proposes it.
3. Ask each person to accept the items with their own name beside them.
   Nobody accepts on another's behalf.

- [ ] Every intermediate objective shows who proposed it and who has
  accepted responsibility for it.

* Carry forward

A numbered list of things to achieve, each with a name, in an order that
reads aloud. Step 16 turns the first of them into something you do.
