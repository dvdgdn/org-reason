* The chapter

On the night of Tuesday the eighteenth the temperature in the city went down
to fourteen degrees.

Aldo Marchetti slept, as he had for two years, in the chair in his living
room, because of his hip. On Wednesday morning a woman named Ruth, who ran
deliveries for the Maple Street Pantry, came up to the fourth floor at 118
Garnet with a winter box. She was not someone who left boxes on mats. When
nobody answered she went down for the caretaker and his keys. The caretaker’s
thermometer read fifty-four in the living room. Mr. Marchetti knew his own
name but not the day, and he could not get his fingers to close around the
cup she tried to give him.

Corinne heard at eleven, from the caretaker, and then spent the day learning
what she could: St. Brendan’s, admitted, mild hypothermia, a chest they
wanted to watch. She sent Odile two messages and had no answer to either.

On Thursday she went to the hospital with nothing to say.

Odile was in the corridor outside the ward in the port authority jacket,
with a cabin bag at her feet; she had flown in overnight. She looked at
Corinne without surprise and without any expression Corinne could use.

“He’s asleep. They say he’ll do. He’ll be here some days and then in the
rehabilitation wing.” She might have been reading from a chart. “I go back
Sunday. There’s a penalty clause, and there’s nothing I can do in that chair
that the nurses can’t.”

“Odile, I am so —”

“Please don’t. I can’t hear it yet.” She picked up the bag and put it down
again. “You’ll want to know whose fault it was. I’d rather you didn’t come to
me with a name. Go and read his work order, out loud, to someone who does
the work. Every time one thing leads to another, say ‘if’ and ‘then’. And
listen for where it doesn’t follow.”

She went back into the ward.

-----

Mags Olsen was already in 4C when Corinne got there, on her knees at the
radiator with the old valve out and lying on a rag. The caretaker had let her
in. The chair by the window had a blanket folded over one arm.

“Twenty-five minutes,” Mags said without looking up. “That’s what this job
is. One valve, seized. Anybody who’d ever seen a steam radiator would’ve
known from the doorway.”

Corinne sat down on the edge of the sofa with the laptop and found she could
not sit in the chair.

“I’ve been told to read this out loud.”

“Who by?”

“His daughter.”

Mags threaded the new valve and reached for a wrench. “Go on, then.”

“October fifteenth. Tenant reports radiator cold. Booked: plumbing. First
available, Monday the twentieth.”

“Why plumbing?”

“It’s — I don’t know. The agent picks from a list.”

“Radiator’s got water in it, hasn’t it. Pipes. Sounds like plumbing if you’ve
never held one.” Mags tightened. “Say it your way. If, then.”

Corinne felt foolish and did it. “If the agent has to choose a trade from a
list, and has never seen the fault, then she can choose the wrong one.”

“Carry on.”

“If the wrong trade is booked, then a plumber goes to a heating job. And then
the visit ends with no repair.” She scrolled. “Monday the twenty-seventh.
Heating technician, Dev. Seized valve. Part not on van. Ordered.”

“Used to be on the van. One-inch angle. I carried three.”

“If the technician doesn’t have the part, then the visit ends with no
repair.” It was coming more easily, and it was not pleasant. “Friday the
thirty-first. Arrived 9:10. No access. Left 9:14.”

Mags stopped working.

“Read me that as an if-then.”

“If the tenant isn’t home —” Corinne began, and stopped, because she was in
the tenant’s home and it had a hospital-cornered blanket in it and a man who
had not been further than the mailboxes since early October.

“Doesn’t go, does it,” Mags said. “Had the valve come in by the
thirty-first?”

Corinne checked the order. “It was received at the yard on November fourth.”

“So some lad’s sent here on the thirty-first for a part that’s still on a
truck somewhere. He knows by the time he’s parked that there’s nothing he can
do. He’s got five more jobs on his row and four minutes to close this one,
and the screen wants a code.” She sat back on her heels. “What code would you
like him to use? There’s ‘completed.’ There’s ‘part required,’ which he can’t
use because the part’s already on order and the system won’t take it twice.
There’s ‘no access.’ There isn’t one for ‘I was sent for nothing.’”

Corinne said it slowly. “If a visit can’t be finished, and it has to be
closed with a code, and the only code that asks no questions is ‘no
access’...”

“Then that’s what you get. I’m not saying he should have. I’m saying I’ve
done it.”

The laptop screen had dimmed. Corinne woke it. There was one visit left and
she did not want to read it.

“Tuesday the eleventh. 7:40. Dev. No access.”

“Seven-forty? He doesn’t start his row till eight.”

“It was red-tagged. First available.” Her voice had gone thin. “By me. I
asked the dispatcher to squeeze it in.”

Mags waited.

“If a job is squeezed in ahead of the day’s row,” Corinne said, “then nobody
books a time with the tenant, because it isn’t a booking. If nobody tells him
a time, and he’s eighty-one, and his hearing aid is charging in the kitchen,
then one knock at twenty to eight —” She stopped. “He had the valve in his
hand. Dev. He had the right part and he was the right person and I’d told Mr.
Marchetti myself it would be Tuesday.”

“Three out of your four,” Mags said, and Corinne looked up sharply. “Bram’s
got them on the wall in dispatch. He thinks they’re daft. I don’t.” She
turned back to the valve. “Two codes, then. Thirty-first and the eleventh.”

Two codes since the first of September. The notice on thirty-five dollars had
been her policy, argued from her chart, illustrated in the boardroom with
this very visit. /Even 4C./ She had stood up and used a failure she had
caused as evidence that tenants were not at home.

On the table by the window, under a paperweight shaped like a block of type,
lay the letter. She had not meant to read it. Someone had gone through it
with a soft pencil. In the second paragraph a word was circled,
/appointement/, with a small proofreader’s mark in the margin and the extra
letter struck out.

Mags opened the supply. The pipe knocked twice, deep in the wall, and then
the radiator began to tick as the iron took the heat.

“There,” she said. “Fifth visit.”

Corinne put her hand on it and kept it there until it was too hot to hold.
Then she turned the work order over and wrote on the back, with Mags reading
over her shoulder and twice making her change a word.

/If the agent must pick a trade without anyone who could fix the fault
hearing it described, then the wrong trade can be sent./

/If the wrong trade is sent, then the visit ends with no repair./

/If a visit ends with no repair and must be closed with a code, then it is
closed as “no access.”/

She read all three aloud. Each of them went. The one she had taken to the
board she tried once more, under her breath, in that room, and could not
finish.

* What Corinne has just learned

Said aloud with "if" and "then", three of the four failed visits to 4C read
as plain consequences of how the work is booked and closed. The sentence
Corinne gave the board will not read at all in the room it was about. And the
fourth visit failed because of her.

* Your thinking — Step 5: Join two troubles

/Ten minutes./

Until now your troubles have been a list. This step puts the first arrows
between them.

1. Pick one real case: a single occasion when the result failed. Write down
   what happened in order, as plainly as a log.
2. Wherever one thing led to the next, write it as a sentence: /If …, then
   …/. Use whole statements on both sides.
3. Read each sentence aloud. To another person if you can; to the wall if
   not. You are listening for the ones you cannot say with a straight face.
4. When a sentence does not go, do not repair it by adding force ("obviously",
   "always"). Ask what actually came between the two things.
5. Take the sentence you believed before you started, the one from the end
   of step 4, and read that aloud for this one case.

Draw what survives as boxes with arrows, cause below and effect above. Three
arrows are enough for today.

Expect one of your own actions to turn up in the chain. Write it in like any
other box.

* The same step in Org Reason

1. For each new "if" that is not already in your commons, press ~C-c r t n~,
   choose =current_reality= and the role =cause=, and enter it as a full
   statement.
2. Press ~C-c r t l~ and choose =current_reality=. Select the cause as the
   source and the effect as the target. Choose the relation =causes=.
3. Press ~C-c r t v~. Each relationship is listed as its own item. Read each
   aloud as "if source, then target".

- [ ] At least three relationships are shown, each proposed, none yet
  accepted.

*If you keep a trusted system:* if one of the boxes is something you did,
there may be a thing to put right with a person. That is a commitment, not
reasoning. Capture it with ~C-c r c~ and clarify it as a =next-action= or an
=agenda= item for them.

* The same step in Reason Commons

In a shared record an arrow is a claim in its own right, separate from the
two statements it joins.

1. Read each relationship aloud to the others as "if … then …".
2. Someone may accept both statements and still not accept the arrow. Record
   that as their position on the relationship, not on the statements.
3. Ask the person who does the work to reword any arrow that sounds wrong to
   them, as a new proposal under their own name.

- [ ] Relationships carry their own proposer and their own acceptance,
  separate from the statements they connect.

* Carry forward

Three or more arrows from one real case, and the sentence you could not say
aloud. Step 6 asks whether each arrow is enough on its own.
