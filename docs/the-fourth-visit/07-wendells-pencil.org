* The chapter

For the leadership meeting Corinne redrew the diagram, and in redrawing it
she tidied it.

The version from the yard had three boxes along the bottom tied together
with a loop, and it looked, on a slide, like a mistake. So the slide had one
box at the bottom. /June: van stock cut by $212,000./ An arrow up to
/Technicians arrive without the part/. An arrow up to /Repeat visits/. An
arrow up to /Backlog: 1,912 and rising/. It was clean. Each arrow, read aloud
in her kitchen the night before, had gone.

Tuesday the twenty-fifth, nine o'clock, the small conference room. Gloria at
the head. Bram with his arms folded. Lu with a tablet. Wendell Price with a
legal pad and a sharpened pencil laid parallel to its edge.

Corinne told them about 4C. She told it well; she could hear the room
listening. She told them about the empty rails in Mags's van and the valve
that used to be there, and then she put up the slide and walked them from the
bottom to the top.

"So when we ask why the backlog is growing," she finished, "I think we have
to look at a decision that was taken in June."

Nobody spoke. Bram had unfolded his arms. It was not his board on the slide,
and he looked like a man who has been let off.

Wendell picked up the pencil. "May I?"

"Of course."

"Thank you. When did open orders begin to rise?"

She had the year-on-year figure by heart. "They were 1,240 last November."

"Month by month."

"I don't have that with me."

"I do. I get it for the accruals." He turned the legal pad a quarter turn; he
had written the months down the margin. "Flat through February. Up sixty in
March. Up again in April and in May. The stock came off the vans in the
third week of June." He looked up. "Your arrow has the cause arriving three
months after the effect."

Corinne felt her face change.

"Second. After we spoke on Friday I had the storekeeper pull a hundred and
fifty repeat visits from the last four weeks. Jobs where a technician went
back to the same fault. I wanted to know how many had waited on a part that
would have been on the van before June." He turned a page. "Thirty-three.
Twenty-two percent. That's real; I'm not pretending it away. I've already
asked for the ninety-day rule to be run again on a full year, because Ms.
Olsen is right that it can't see winter. But it leaves a hundred and
seventeen that waited on something else."

"That's one sample."

"It is. It's one more than is on the slide."

Gloria had not moved.

"Third. On Friday you stood in my cage and said, yourself, that a van
without the part is no trouble to a technician who knows the job. You added
two more causes beside mine. I wrote them down, because I thought it was a
good piece of thinking." He tapped the single box at the bottom of her slide
with the pencil's eraser end. "Where did they go?"

"I simplified it for the room."

"Yes."

There was a fourth, and he offered it almost apologetically. Had she
considered that it might run the other way? His rule had flagged any part
that had not left a van in ninety days. If, since March, technicians had been
finishing fewer jobs, then fewer parts would have left the vans, and more
lines would have looked idle in June than truly were. "In which case the cut
is partly a result of your problem. I don't know that. But I can't rule it
out, and neither can that arrow."

Corinne had nothing. She stood beside a slide she had been proud of at eight
o'clock.

Gloria spoke at last, and quietly, which was how she did it when she meant
it most. "Two weeks ago it was the tenants. I took that to the board. I sent
a hundred and thirty-eight letters and I have a contractor in the yard at
twelve thousand dollars a week, on the tenants. One of those letters went to
a man who is now in St. Brendan's." She closed her folder. "This morning it's
Wendell. Corinne, I can't go back in there with a third one. Come to me when
somebody other than you has tried to knock it down and couldn't."

That was the meeting.

*

She sat for some time on the back stairs between the second and third
floors, where nobody went.

The worst of it was that she had seen each of his four points before he made
them. Not clearly. But the three-legged drawing had been in her notebook the
whole time, in her own hand, and she had left it out because it did not say
what she wanted said. She had used the method the way she used a chart: to
carry a conclusion into a room.

At eleven she knocked on the controller's door with the notebook open at the
drawing from the yard.

Wendell looked at it, and then at her. "People don't usually come back."

"You had four objections and I'd have had none. I want the rest. All of it,
every arrow. Including the ones that aren't about your cage."

"I should say that I dislike this diagram a great deal. It has my signature
at the bottom of it."

"I know."

"I mention it because disliking it is not one of my objections, and I'd want
you to be able to tell the two apart. If I'm only disliking, say so." He
cleared a space on the desk. "Sit down. What does this box mean?"

It was the box that read /Technicians rush jobs./

"That they hurry. They don't finish properly."

"I don't know what I'd count. Six jobs planned per day is a thing. 'Rush' is
a view." He waited while she rewrote it. /Each technician's day is planned
with six jobs./ "Now. This one. 'The wrong trade is sent.' Is that so? How do
you know?"

"It happened at 4C."

"Once. How often?"

She wrote a question mark beside it.

They went up the page like that. He did not have a list; he had the habits of
thirty years of people bringing him numbers they wanted signed. Does this
really lead to that, or do they merely happen together? Is it enough by
itself? What else could produce the same thing? /A visit is closed as "no
access"/, he pointed out, could also come about because the tenant was in
fact out, and some certainly were; she could not draw it as though there were
only one road to that box. Which came first? And, three times, at the three
arrows she leaned on hardest, the same question in almost the same words.

"If this is true, what else would have to be true? Something you could go
and look at."

By half past twelve the drawing was a mess. Two boxes were reworded. Four
arrows had question marks. One arrow he had struck through, and when she
objected he heard her out and then put it back with a note. Down the margin,
in her writing, were three lines headed /Go and look/.

/Were tenants home when visits were coded "no access"? Who would know?/
/When a van leaves a "no access", how long was it there?/
/How many visits does a repair take? (Count closed orders.)/

"It's worse than it was this morning," Corinne said.

"It's uglier." Wendell capped his pencil. "This morning you had something
nobody had tried to break. I'd sooner sign this one, and I don't like it any
better."

At the door she stopped. "Why did you vote against the contractor?"

"Because nobody could tell me what we were short of." He had already turned
back to his screen. "They still can't. But you've now got three things to go
and look at, which is three more than the board had."

* What Corinne has just learned

Corinne drew a diagram to prove what she already believed, and removed the
parts that spoiled it. Wendell broke it in four questions. When she goes back
and asks him to try every arrow, the drawing gets uglier and stronger, and
he tells her plainly when he is objecting and when he merely dislikes it.

* Your thinking — Step 7: Invite a challenge

/Fifteen minutes, with another person./

Find someone who has a reason to disagree with your diagram. The person whose
decision sits in one of the boxes is the best choice and the hardest to ask.

1. Give them the sheet and say: /I want you to break this. Tell me when you
   are objecting and when you just don't like it./
2. Ask them to go arrow by arrow. For each box and arrow, these are fair
   objections:
   - /I don't understand what this says./
   - /Is that a fact? How do you know?/
   - /Does this really produce that, or do they only happen together?/
   - /Is it enough on its own?/
   - /What else could produce the same result?/
   - /Could it be the other way round? Which came first?/
   - /If this were true, what else would we see?/
3. Do not defend. Write a question mark on anything you cannot answer.
   Reword what was unclear. Add a second road into any box that has one.
4. Collect every "what else would we see?" into a list headed /Go and look/.

Before you ask them, look at whether the sheet you are about to hand over is
the one you drew, or a cleaner one you made to be persuasive. Hand over the
ugly one.

* The same step in Org Reason

1. With your challenger beside you, press ~C-c r t v~ and go through the
   relationships one at a time.
2. For each objection press ~C-c r t ?~, choose =challenge=, select the
   statement or relationship, and type the objection in their words.
3. Where you reworded a box, add the new wording with ~C-c r t n~ and leave
   the old one unaccepted.
4. Do not press ~C-c r t a~ on anything that carries an unanswered challenge.
   Org Reason will refuse one-key acceptance of a disputed item in any case.

- [ ] The view shows challenges against at least two items, and those items
  are not accepted.

*If you keep a trusted system:* asking the person is an action with a
person's name on it. Before the conversation, put it on their =agenda= list.
Afterwards, each line of /Go and look/ is a =next-action= or, if you are
waiting for someone's records, a =delegate= entry with the date you will
check.

* The same step in Reason Commons

This is the step a shared record exists for.

1. Ask each person to challenge at least one item that is not in their own
   area, and one that is.
2. A challenge is recorded with its author and stays attached to the item
   until it is answered. Answering it is also a recorded act.
3. Accept items one person at a time. An item three people have accepted and
   one has challenged is exactly that, and the record should say so.

- [ ] Each challenge names its author, and no challenged item is shown as
  agreed by everyone.
