* The chapter

The woman at the front counter had not raised her voice, and that was why the
receptionist had called upstairs.

“She has a folder,” the receptionist said. “She asked for whoever is
responsible for repairs. I didn’t know who to say.”

Until Friday nobody would have known. On Friday afternoon Gloria Ferreira had
closed her office door, told Corinne Vance that the board met on Thursday the
thirteenth, and said, “I need someone to tell them why. You’re good at telling
people things.” Corinne had been resident services manager at Harbor Row
Housing for four years and in its communications office for seven before
that. She had never held a wrench. As of Friday she held the repair backlog.

She went down.

The woman was about fifty, in a port authority jacket, with reading glasses
pushed up into grey hair. The folder was on the counter, squared to its edge.

“Odile Marchetti. My father is Aldo Marchetti, apartment 4C at 118 Garnet
Street. On October fifteenth he reported that the radiator in his living room
was cold. Your people have been to him three times. It is still cold.”

Corinne had brought her laptop for exactly this. She found 4C. Three visits:
the twentieth, the twenty-seventh, the thirty-first. Beside each one the
system showed a small green tick.

“I can see we’ve attended each time within our five-day standard,” she said,
and heard how it sounded.

“Yes. I’m not disputing that you came.” Ms. Marchetti did not open the folder.
“May I ask you something? When a tenant reports a fault, how many times do
you usually go to the apartment before it is fixed?”

“It depends on the repair.”

“On average. Roughly.”

Corinne looked at the screen as if the number might be on it. There was a
number for nearly everything. Open work orders: 1,912. A year ago: 1,240. Days
from report to completion: 34. Attended within five working days: 91 percent.
She scrolled. Calls answered within twenty seconds: 94 percent.

“I’d have to ask.”

“Please.”

She rang Bram Kowalczyk at the yard. Bram ran the twenty-four technicians and
answered on the second ring, as he always did.

“Visits per repair?” he said. “I can give you visits per tech per day. Five
point two. It was four point one before the board.” He meant his routing
board, not Gloria’s. “Why?”

“Someone’s asking how many visits it takes to finish a job.”

“As many as it takes. Tell them five point two.”

She rang Lu Tran in the call center. Lu had the answer rate, the average call
at three minutes ten seconds, and the share of reports given an appointment
inside five days. She did not have that one either.

Corinne put the phone down. “We don’t measure it.”

Ms. Marchetti nodded slowly, as though a gauge had read what she expected.
“At the port I plan berths. Which ship goes to which crane, and when. I have
twenty minutes before I’m due back.” She took a pen from her jacket and laid
it on the counter beside the folder. “You’ve been given this, I think. You
came down with the look of someone who has been given something. Would you
write down for me what keeps going wrong? Not what you’d tell a board. What
keeps going wrong.”

It was not a reasonable request from a stranger and Corinne did it anyway,
partly because nobody else in the building had asked. She turned a
repair-request form over and wrote fast. She had been composing it in her
head all weekend.

/Tenants are never home for appointments./
/Technicians rush jobs and cut corners because of the routing board./
/Stores never has anything in stock since the cuts./
/The call center books whatever slot is free without thinking./
/Backlog is out of control./
/People wait too long./
/Nobody talks to each other./

She turned it round. Ms. Marchetti read it without touching it.

“Which of these have you seen?”

“All of them. Everyone knows these.”

“Have you seen a technician cut a corner?”

“I’ve been told —”

“I see a word here twice. ‘Because.’ And here it’s hiding: ‘since the cuts.’
Could you cross those parts out and leave what’s left?”

Corinne crossed them out. /Technicians rush jobs/ looked thinner without its
reason.

“Is that one something that happened, or something somebody is?”

“I don’t follow.”

“’Tenants are never home.’ Never? My father has not left that apartment since
the first week of October. He has a bad hip and one working ear.” She said it
evenly. “What is the thing you could count?”

Corinne looked at the laptop. “Thirty-one percent of visits are closed as ‘no
access’.”

“Write that. It’s longer, but you could be wrong about it, and then you’d
find out.”

It took most of the twenty minutes. /Nobody talks to each other/ would not
turn into anything she could count and Ms. Marchetti said, mildly, that
perhaps it was a feeling, and that feelings were allowed but should go on a
different sheet. /Stores never has anything/ became, after a second call to
Bram, /Technicians arrive without the part the job needs/. Bram said it
happened “all the time,” and when asked how often said he’d never had a reason
to count. So that went on the form as well, the thing she had learned at the
counter: /Nobody knows how many visits a repair takes./

Seven lines. She read them back and felt oddly robbed. Her first list had
sounded like someone who understood the problem. This one sounded like a
meter reading.

/Tenants wait 34 days for a repair./
/Open work orders have gone from 1,240 to 1,912 in a year./
/31 percent of visits are closed as “no access.”/
/Technicians arrive without the part the job needs./
/The wrong kind of technician is sent./
/Tenants ring again about the same fault./
/Nobody knows how many visits a repair takes./

“There’s nobody to blame in it,” Corinne said.

“No.” Ms. Marchetti looked at her watch and picked up the folder, which she
had never opened. “Is that a loss?”

“The board will want to know why.”

“I expect they will. So do I.” She put her pen away. “I fly to Halifax on the
fifteenth, for three weeks. A berth study; there’s a penalty if I’m not there.
I mention it so that you know I am not going to stand at this counter every
morning.” She paused. “And I am not asking you to move my father up your
list. I’d like that to be clear. If you do that, the next person’s father
moves down, and I schedule for a living. I’m asking how many visits.”

She left a card. Corinne stood at the counter with the form.

Then she went upstairs and did the thing she had just been asked not to do.
It took one email to the dispatcher at the yard: /4C, 118 Garnet — red tag,
first available, please. Daughter came in. Elderly, no heat in living room./
The reply came inside a minute. /Will squeeze it in./

It was the first useful thing she had done since Friday, and it felt like it.
She pinned the form with its seven lines to the wall above her desk, where
she would see it while she wrote something better for the board.

* What Corinne has just learned

Corinne's first list explains everything and can't be checked. Each line names
a culprit or carries a "because". When she strips those out she has seven
things that could each turn out to be false, and one of them is a number
nobody has. She would rather have the first list.

* Your thinking — Step 1: List what keeps going wrong

/Ten minutes./

Choose one situation that has resisted effort: at work, at home, in a group
you belong to. Something that people have already tried to fix. You will work
on this one situation through all eighteen steps, so pick one you care about
and know from the inside.

1. On one sheet, write what keeps going wrong. Ten lines or fewer.
2. Go down the list and cross out every "because", "since" and "due to",
   together with what follows it.
3. Cross out every line that says what somebody /is/ (lazy, careless, never
   there). Ask what happened that you could count or point to, and write that
   in its place.
4. For each line that is left, ask: could I be wrong about this, and would I
   find out? If not, move it to a second sheet headed /Feelings/. They are
   real, and they are not for this sheet.

The list should now feel flatter and less clever than the one you started
with. That is the uncomfortable part, and it is the point: you have set down
what you know before saying why.

* The same step in Org Reason

The list becomes the start of a commons, which is Org Reason's record of one
problem. The tree commands need Python 3 with PyYAML; ~C-c r t P~ sets that up
if a command says it is missing.

1. Press ~C-c r t c~. Give a short directory name such as =repairs=, then a
   plain name for the situation at =Goal / system= (you will state the aim
   properly in step 2), then your participant ID and display name.
2. For each line on your sheet press ~C-c r t n~. At =Tree question= choose
   =current_reality=. At =Role in this tree= choose =undesirable_effect=. Type
   the line as the statement.
3. Press ~C-c r t v~ to see them all.

- [ ] The view lists your troubles under /current reality/, each marked as
  proposed.

*If you keep a trusted system:* some lines on your first sheet were really
things you have promised to do about the situation. Those are not troubles.
Capture each with ~C-c r c~ and clarify it later, so it stops posing as part
of the problem.

* The same step in Reason Commons

A commons is a shared record. What changes when other people are in it is
that each line has an author.

1. Open the same commons with the Reason Commons skill, or sit down with one
   person who lives with the situation too.
2. Have each person add what keeps going wrong in their own words. Do not
   merge two people's lines into one tidy sentence.
3. Look at whose lines are whose. A trouble only one person reports is still
   a trouble; it tells you where they stand.

- [ ] Every statement shows who proposed it, and none is yet marked as
  agreed.

* Carry forward

Your sheet of plain troubles, and the second sheet of feelings. Step 2 asks
what the whole effort is for.
