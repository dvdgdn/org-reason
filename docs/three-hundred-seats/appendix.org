* How the Story Was Built

** The seed

After losing a sold-out benefit’s venue, a producer has ten days to make good
on three hundred tickets.

** The five sentence story

Maya Torres sells three hundred tickets to a supper that will fund the Maple
Street Pantry’s winter deliveries. She misses a confirmation she made on the
phone and loses the hall ten days before the event. She announces a
replacement and learns the next morning that it seats eighty fewer people than
she promised. She finds room by splitting the dinner into two sittings, then
learns that serving twice would leave the pantry short of its delivery money.
Reviewing her promises together gives her time to change what she offers
guests, return their money if they want it, and give her deputy a decision he
can make while she keeps a promise to her daughter.

** The characters under pressure

Maya treats a quick answer as proof that she cares. Losing the hall teaches
her that an empty inbox can hide a promise; losing the crew’s confidence
teaches her that a promise made privately can take a decision away from
someone else. Her final test is whether she can say when she will know, and
then actually be there when she said she would.

Eli wants to lead a crew whose time is treated as real. He can draw a usable
room and calculate a reset. He can’t do his job if a message from Maya can
undo a shift he’s already staffed. His turning point comes when she gives him
a real decision and backs it.

Ruth needs a number she can translate into deliveries. She buys Maya time
after the first loss and insists on hearing what a second failure would cost.
Her authority covers the pantry’s spending and its contingency, never the
guests’ choice.

Denise has a printer deadline, a bank board and a fixed sponsorship. She
offers a more expensive way to wait and later puts people on phones. Jo
protects a date she can sell and a room people can move through. Both show
what happens downstream when Maya says yes.

Nina wants to know whose name to put on a chair. She arranges backup rides
because she can. She can’t arrange for her mother to hear the first note after
the rest.

** The scene map

Part One follows a spoken promise that never reached Maya’s system. Chapter 1:
the vanished reservation gives Ruth a Friday deadline. Chapter 2: a kitchen
table catches commitments her email missed. Chapter 3: a usable outcome
narrows the search, and a timed follow-up saves a van. Chapter 4: Ruth puts a
price on getting the next decision wrong.

Part Two tests the difference between a fact and a guess. Chapter 5: Maya
sends a drawing and gets a hold, then lets a print deadline turn the hold into
an announcement. Chapter 6: one chair in an aisle shows why a bigger number on
a brochure won’t seat more people. Chapter 7: signing the hall secures the
date while the cost of a second service stays open. Chapter 8: a private favor
unravels a public staffing decision. Chapter 9: the catering quote breaks the
budget.

Part Three makes two promises visible at the same time. Chapter 10: the bank’s
offer puts callers within reach and extra cash out of reach. Chapter 11: the
review finds the earlier of two deadlines and surfaces the clash with Nina’s
recital. Chapter 12: Ruth and Maya choose what the pantry can offer, even if
the guests decline. Chapter 13: each guest gets the choice; a table host asks
what Maya knew when she pressed send.

Part Four asks whether other people can act on Maya’s word. Chapter 14: Eli
decides what fits, and Nina plays to the chair she saved. Chapter 15: a table
host’s reply turns six ambiguous tickets into two arrivals and four refunds.
Chapter 16: the reset tests the crew’s authority under pressure. Chapter 17:
two returned calls and a pencil mark show the new shape of a promise.

* Getting Things Done in the Story

The missed date at Merritt and the saved reservation at the rental desk are
versions of the same problem. Someone else is free to act after a certain
time. Maya has to remember what she owes them or what she needs from them
while there’s still a choice. The difference is where the promise goes after
the call.

** Capture what has your attention

Maya empties her email inbox and still loses the hall. The promise was made on
the phone. On the back of the envelope, she gathers things from her memory,
her pockets and her messages. At first she writes /van/. That’s enough to
catch the thought. She’ll decide what it means when she comes back to it.

** Clarify the outcome and the next action

/Venue/ is the name of a problem. A room for three hundred people at dinner
tables, with a way in from the street that doesn’t require steps, describes a
result Maya can check. Sending Jo the drawing is something she can do in the
minutes she has. An event project may take dozens of steps. The next action is
the one she can take with what she has now.

** Organize reminders where you will use them

After Maya sends the drawing, she’s waiting for Jo to answer. The drawing
belongs with her event materials, Jo’s reply belongs on the list she checks
for things other people owe her, and the noon expiration belongs on the
calendar. The morning follow-up leaves her time to act before noon. Putting an
item on a list doesn’t change the state of the world: Weller still has to say
what fits.

** Reflect while there is time to change course

On Tuesday Maya checks her notes, calendar, projects and outstanding replies
together. The deadline for committing the crew is two days earlier than the
one for the headcount. Seeing it now gives her an option she won’t have
Friday. A regular review keeps a system worth trusting. It lets her remove
stale reminders, check promises, and catch collisions while she can do
something about them.

** Engage with the time and authority you have

At the start of the second reception, Maya can help move the photograph or
open check-in. Eli has the information and authority to move the photograph.
At the door, Maya can match a guest to the right booking and get another
volunteer set up. She chooses the work that needs her at that moment. The
lists help her see the choice; she still has to make it.

** Try a different promise

You offered to collect a key for a family visit. The landlord needs the name
of the person collecting it before she’ll confirm pickup. Your train leaves at
six. The draft message with your name is open. What can you do now? What
answer are you waiting for, and what is the last time you could check and
still make another plan? What can you safely tell your family before the
landlord replies?
