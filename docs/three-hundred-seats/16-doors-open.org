* The chapter

On Saturday, Maya and Eli taped a paper arrow to the door of Merritt Hall.

Jo had sent someone to put signs out at Weller, then called Maya to ask if
anyone had thought about the other building. Gene had said they could put a
sign up if the arrow pointed away from his doors. Maya printed the new address
as big as the paper would take and put the phone number below it. Eli pulled
the tape tight against the glass. Behind the sign, men were setting out
bottles for the retirement dinner.

Through the glass Maya could see where she’d thought Ruth would stand. A man
inside raised a folding screen. The place disappeared.

When they got to Weller, Ben had unloaded the van. Luis was putting the keys
in his pocket. Maya asked him to show her where he was going to park. He
pointed at the far side of the lot, where the van could leave without backing
across the ramp. She put the address of the rental return in his hand. He said
Eli had given him one and showed her the paper. It had the address, the time
and the name of the clerk. She folded hers up and put it away.

By five the front door was letting in cold air faster than the heaters could
warm it. The volunteers at check-in had coats over their aprons. Maya stood
behind them for a few minutes, listening. People gave their names and were
told which way to go for soup. A man came in holding a paper ticket with
Merritt Hall printed on it and asked if he was in the right place. One of
Denise’s callers looked up and said, “Mr. Fields. I spoke to your wife.”

“Yes,” he said, as though that settled the question of which building it was.

At a quarter past, Doris came through the side door. She was walking with one
hand on a cane and a gloved hand on the rail. Ben had come out far enough to
see that Lenny’s truck was beside the painted line. He didn’t hurry Doris. At
the threshold she stopped to move the cane to her other hand. Maya remembered
the cart catching on the leg of the extra chair. There was no chair there now.
Doris went all the way to the nearest table without changing direction.

“The soup cups have handles,” Maya told her.

“Then I can get my own.”

Maya went with her as far as the soup and took a cup for herself. She’d missed
lunch. It was thick enough to leave a yellow line inside the cup as she drank.
At the keyboard a girl in dress shoes too new to bend was pressing the pedal
to see how it felt. Her father was standing with his back to the room, holding
her jacket. She began to play before he knew she was ready.

Ruth spoke at six. She stood on the floor beside the keyboard. When she
unfolded her notes, the microphone caught the sound of the paper and several
people turned toward her before she said anything. She told them about the
bags with numbers on them. The number six was not a route. It was the sixth
door on a route, and on Wednesdays they went there first because the woman who
lived behind it had a ride at ten.

“I tell you that because on a budget it just says fuel,” Ruth said. “If we can
make the runs through March, I can give her the dates on Monday. She’ll put
them on her refrigerator. She keeps the paper there with a magnet that says
/I’d rather be fishing/.”

There was some laughter. Ruth told them what else the pantry needed for the
run. No one asked for a second microphone. At the back of the room a server
had stopped with a tray of empty cups and was listening.

After the first guests began to leave, the bank gathered its board for the
photograph. Eli had agreed to one by the front wall at six twenty. One of the
board members had gone to bring a guest around from the ramp. They waited.
Then the photographer asked for Lewis, who had stopped to help somebody with a
coat. By the time he got to the front wall it was six thirty-two. He called
Ruth back to the sign and asked the photographer to take another one without
the check.

Maya saw a server come out of the kitchen with a gray tub. There were two
empty tubs stacked on a cart behind him and no place to take the cart through.
The bank group had spread out to get everybody into the picture. Beyond them a
woman was setting a fresh cloth on a table that still had cups on it.

“Give us another minute,” Denise said when she saw Maya.

Maya stepped toward the photographer. She’d spent enough time at events to
know what words to use. /I’m going to steal this group for just a second./ She
could get them to move two feet and make them feel they were being taken
somewhere they wanted to go.

Eli came out of the kitchen with a tub of his own. He set it on the table and
looked across the room. Maya could see him find the cart, then the sign, then
her. He held up a hand, flat, which could have meant wait or stop.

“Can you open the second list?” he called. “They’re starting to line up.”

Maya looked at the door. There were people on the pavement. Two volunteers
were outside with them, giving directions. The clock above the bar said
twenty-five to seven. If Maya moved the photo, she’d still have to tell the
people outside when they could come in. If she went to the door, Eli could
move the photo while she did it.

She went to the door.

A woman in a red coat said her sister had given her a ticket but hadn’t said
whose name it was under. Maya asked her sister’s name and found them both on
the list. “You’re in the right place,” she said. “I can check you in here.
We’ll let people through at seven.”

“Can I get out of the wind?” the woman asked.

Maya opened the outer door and pointed out the bench in the entry. Then she
set one of the volunteers there with a copy of the list, so the woman didn’t
have to give her name again when she went through. When she looked back into
the room, the photographer was picking up his case. Eli and Denise had put the
sign on the other side of the bar, where the bank could finish without
standing in the path of the cart. The cart made it to the kitchen with all
three tubs.

At three to seven Eli came to the door and told her the room was ready. She
asked if he wanted her to wait until seven. He looked at the woman on the
bench.

“If she wants a seat, let her have one.”

Maren came in just after seven with a big yellow envelope under her arm. Maya
walked her as far as her table. Six chairs. Maya had checked them herself that
morning. Maren laid the envelope flat. On the back she had written the name of
the woman who was leaving the clinic. There were five lines of handwriting
down the side, one in purple ink.

“You want a pen?” Maya asked.

Maren pulled three out of her pocket. “I work at a clinic,” she said. “You
want one?”

Maya took a black one. She used it to mark off the last arrival. Later, when
all six women were at the table, Maren asked her to take a picture.

Ruth gave the same account to the second room. When she got to the fishing
magnet, a man said, “I know that woman,” in a voice loud enough for most of
the room to hear. Ruth paused and looked toward him. “Then you know she goes
whether the fish are biting or not,” she said. This time, when she folded the
paper, the man started clapping before anyone else did.

At nine twenty-six Jo walked the room with her keys in her hand. The tables
were stacked. Ben and Leila carried the last chair between them because a leg
had caught in a chair below it and they were both already holding on. Maya
picked up a paper cup the cart had left against the wall. On the clean floor
she could see the square where the machine had stood. They’d had a piano on
one side of it and sixteen tables on the other. It looked too small to have
held anything.

Jo turned off the lights nearest the kitchen. Maya went to thank Eli, who was
outside seeing Luis into the van. She waited until he’d finished telling him
where to leave the keys.

“Did everybody get what they needed?” she asked.

“Leila wants to know if she can take the rest of the apple cake.”

“Did she ask you?”

“I told her yes,” Eli said.

Maya followed him out through the side door. At the bottom of the ramp Leila
was waiting for them with the cake in a white bakery box.

* What Maya has just learned

A sponsor's photograph is blocking the cart, and guests for the second
reception are lining up outside in the wind. Maya knows exactly what to say to
move the photograph. Eli calls across the room to ask whether she can open the
second list. Moving the photograph is his job, and he can do it. Only she is
free to work the door. She goes to the door. Earlier she had tried to hand a
driver an address that Eli had already given him; she folded her copy and put
it away.

* Your trusted system — Step 16: Engage

/Five minutes now, then all day./

The lists show you your options. You still have to choose among them, and
choosing well is a skill of its own.

When you have a gap, read your Next Actions and narrow them with four
questions, in this order:

1. *Where am I, and what do I have with me?* Drop what is impossible here.
2. *How long do I have?* Drop what won't fit.
3. *How much energy do I have?* Drop what you would do badly.
4. *Of what is left, which matters most, and which can only I do?*

Then do that one thing and no other.

Two checks from the story:

- When something goes wrong in front of you, ask whose it is before you step
  in. If you handed it over in step 14, leave it with them.
- When you catch yourself repeating an instruction someone already has, stop.
  Fold the paper and put it away.

Try it once today. Write down what you chose and what you left alone.
