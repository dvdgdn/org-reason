* The chapter

At six forty, Maya was on her hands and knees in the pantry office, looking
under the borrowed desk for the name of a woman who rented a gym. She found a
red pen and an unopened packet of crackers. The name was in a text from a
florist, six messages above a photograph of someone’s dog.

She called. A man answered and said his wife would be back in the morning.
Behind him, a game show audience applauded. Maya thanked him and turned off
the light.

By the time she got to her apartment, Nina had made toast. She stood in socks
at the counter, scraping the black edge into the sink with a butter knife. Her
cello was on the floor in its case. Maya stepped over it.

“Dad picked me up,” Nina said.

“From school?”

“Rehearsal. I told you he was going to.”

Maya’s phone showed the message, sent at lunch. She remembered seeing it while
she waited for the parks office to answer and thinking that she should ask if
Cal could take Thursday too. She had neither asked him nor put Thursday in the
calendar.

“Thanks for sorting that out,” she said.

Nina offered her the toast with the most butter. “Are you coming next week?”

“Where?”

“The recital.”

The answer reached Maya’s mouth before she understood the question. She took a
bite of toast. On the refrigerator, under the magnet shaped like an ear of
corn, was the recital sheet. Thursday at six. Nina’s group would play first.
Students were to come at five thirty with music and a stand if they had one.

“Give me a minute to look,” Maya said.

She opened the calendar. There was a fitting at four for a job the following
month. A reminder to order coffee for the supper sat at six; she couldn’t
remember why she’d put it there. She took the coffee off the calendar and
wrote it on the back of an envelope. Then she put in the recital and asked
Nina if she wanted a ride or if she’d go straight from her father’s.

“Dad’s taking me for the warm-up,” Nina said. “They let us save one chair.”

“Save it for me.”

Nina turned the butter knife in her hand. “I have to put a name on the tape.”

“Put mine.”

Maya went into the living room with the envelope. She made it as far as the
lamp before she remembered the van. The rental desk had emailed on Monday to
say they needed a driver’s license number. She’d asked Eli to send his. Had
he? Then she remembered the switch on the pantry freezer that Ruth wanted her
to tell the building manager about, which was different from the noise it
always made. The switch had to be jiggled to shut off the light. There was a
dress Nina had outgrown that she’d offered to take to Cal’s sister. It was in
a bag under Maya’s bed. Twice she had driven to Cal’s with the bag still
there.

She sat on the floor and emptied her coat pockets. A receipt. A piece of paper
on which she had written /KLINE?/ in the car. A button. The manager at Merritt
had told her Tuesday, and she had done nothing as small as putting a mark on a
scrap of paper. She tried to remember how many times she’d thought about it
since the call. She remembered thinking of the ovens.

Nina came in with her stand and extended it one section at a time. Maya put
down eleven things, including /room/, /van/, /coffee/, /call Cal/, and
/freezer/. Then she sat for a while and put down a twelfth: /Ruth thinks I’m
careless/. There was room for it on the envelope. It didn’t mean she had to
send it to anyone.

“Can you count for me?” Nina said.

Maya put the envelope on the lamp table. “From where?”

“The top. You say one, two, three, four. Twice. Then I come in.”

Maya counted the first four. At five Nina dropped her bow onto her lap.

“Start at one again.”

“You want eight.”

“I want two bars. If you say five I think I’m late.”

Maya began again. In the second bar she heard the apartment upstairs run water
into a tub. Then Nina’s first note, thin enough at the start that it seemed to
come through the wall with the water. She played four more and stopped.

“Was that where I came in before?” she asked.

Maya had been thinking about the van. She could hear the five notes in her
head and couldn’t say when the first one had happened.

“Do it again?” she said.

This time she turned the envelope over before she counted.

* What Maya has just learned

On the back of an envelope Maya writes eleven things from her memory, her
pockets and her messages: =room=, =van=, =coffee=. The twelfth is a worry, not
a task. Writing it down doesn't mean she has to send it to anyone. Only then
can she hear her daughter's first note.

* Your trusted system — Step 2: Capture everything

/Fifteen minutes. This is one of the two long steps./

Take the list of entrances from step 1 and go through them one at a time:
messages, pockets, bag, desk, voicemail, then your head. Write down everything
that has your attention. Tasks, promises, unanswered messages, things to fix,
decisions, and worries with no action attached.

Three rules:

- One line per thing. A single word is enough if you will know what it means
  tomorrow.
- Don't sort, rank or solve. =van= is a complete entry today.
- Don't stop at the work things. The recital goes on the same list as the venue.

You are finished when you sit for a full minute and nothing new comes. Expect
thirty to a hundred lines. That number is normal.

* The same step in Org Reason

1. Press ~C-c r c~, type one thing, press ~RET~. Repeat. The prompt takes one
   line and files it at once, so the rhythm is fast.
2. For something already on screen in Emacs, select the text and press
   ~C-c r c~: the selection is captured without retyping.
3. For paper, a photograph or a voice memo, press ~C-c r m~ and choose the
   file. Org Reason keeps a copy of the original with an inbox entry pointing
   to it.
4. Capture the worries too. Later you may file one as a =feeling= or a
   =question=, which is not a task and never shows up as one.

Do not press ~C-c r x~ yet, however obvious an item looks.

- [ ] ~C-c r d~ opens the dashboard, and its first line counts your
  unclarified captures. Note the number.
