* The chapter

Ruth was at the packing table with a roll of blue tape around her wrist. She
put a strip across the top of a cardboard box, tore it off with her teeth, and
wrote /BELL ST/ on it. A boy in an apron took the box off the table and
replaced it with an empty one.

“Have you got a room?” she asked Maya.

“I have one hall with a free night and one place that’s trying to move
something.”

The boy set an opened case of beans on the table. Ruth sent him for the cans
with the ring pulls. The cans he’d brought were the kind you needed an opener
for. The pantry had learned that some people would take them and give them
away unopened.

“Did you tell Alan what happened?” Ruth asked.

“He wanted to know whether they should hold the mailing.”

“What’d you say?”

“That there’d be an address by the time it went.”

Ruth took the tape off her wrist. “What time does it go?”

“I’m finding out.”

“Find out before you tell him there’ll be an address.”

The boy came back with the beans and Ruth showed him where to put them. She
wiped her fingers on her apron and led Maya to the office. From the top drawer
she took a sheet of paper with a strip of adding machine tape clipped to it.
The tape curled in on itself when she set it on the desk. She held it down
with the stapler.

The tickets brought in twenty-two thousand five hundred dollars. The bank had
committed another five thousand. The hall, the food, and everything it took to
serve that food came to twelve thousand in the original plan. Ruth moved the
stapler down the sheet. What stayed with the pantry was fifteen thousand five
hundred.

“I know the budget,” Maya said.

“Which of these numbers did you tell the halls?”

“I told them the rental allowance.”

“Good. If a room eats the whole allowance, we can still pay the servers. If it
costs more, I need to know how much more.”

Ruth took the delivery schedule from the wall. It was a grid of weeks. Each
week had three lines, one for each run. Along the right side she’d penciled
what the pantry had to pay for drivers, fuel, cold storage and bags. At the
bottom Maya saw fourteen thousand. She could do the subtraction without
looking back.

“Fifteen hundred to play with,” she said.

“Fifteen hundred above what the runs cost,” said Ruth. “I’d like to replace
the freezer latch. There’s a man who fixes these who won’t sell you a new
freezer. But the latch can wait.”

Maya tried to picture giving Ruth a new latch. At the end of the last supper
there’d been a moment when Ruth lifted the hem of her apron to wipe her
glasses. She’d just been told what the night had made. Maya had been at a
table sorting vouchers and heard a woman who sounded like Ruth laugh from
somewhere on the other side of the room. She hadn’t known she’d be able to
recognize that sound again until she thought of it now.

Her phone rang. Denise wanted to talk about an address. The bank was about to
send its November mailing. It had agreed to tuck a card into the envelopes for
ticket holders and pay for the ticket email through the same vendor. The
envelopes were already addressed. The vendor would prepare both from the same
file.

“Hold it for me,” Maya said.

“Till three twenty-five. Then I have to tell them what’s going in.”

“What if you send the bank mailing without our cards and do those later?”

“We can. You’d be paying for a separate run and postage. I’ll get a number.”

After the call Maya checked the calendar. The appointment at three thirty was
still called /Pickup/, in the pale green she used for home. She could ask Cal
to go. She pictured Nina at the glass doors with the cello between her knees,
taking out her phone to see which one of them was coming. Maya left the
calendar alone.

She called the place with the dance lesson. The instructor could move it, the
woman said. But the room couldn’t seat more than two hundred and fifty. Maya
asked why she’d said three hundred that morning.

“The lesson’s three hundred,” the woman said. “The parents sit on the floor.”

Maya took the name off the list.

At the pantry window, a volunteer in a yellow vest was trying to stack the
last of the Bell Street boxes in the back of a car. The top box would only fit
if she took out the parcel shelf. Ruth went outside, showed her how to lift it
off its pins, and carried it back into the building. She set it upright by the
office door. It would have to go back in the car when the woman returned. Ruth
wrote the driver’s name on a piece of tape and stuck it on.

Maya clicked on the drawing. She had an email from Jo, sent while she’d been
asking about the dance lesson. Before she opened it a proof from the bank’s
printer appeared at the top of her inbox.

* What Maya has just learned

Ruth holds the adding-machine tape flat with a stapler. The supper has to leave
fourteen thousand dollars for the winter delivery runs, and there is fifteen
hundred to spare. Now Maya knows what the room is for and what a wrong
decision costs. Ruth adds one more thing: find out before you tell someone
there will be an address.

* Your trusted system — Step 4: Projects

/Ten minutes./

Go back to the lines you marked in step 3 as needing several steps. Each is a
project: any result that takes more than one action to reach.

Start a list titled *Projects*. For each one write three things.

1. *The outcome*, as a finished state: "Passport renewed", not "passport".
2. *What it is for, and who is counting on it.* One line. Ruth's version is
   "winter runs paid for: $14,000".
3. *The next action*, which goes on your Next Actions list.

The Projects list is short entries only. It reminds you what you have agreed
to finish. Plans, drawings and budgets are kept elsewhere.

Most people find between ten and forty projects. If one of yours has a real
deadline set by someone else, write it beside the outcome. Don't invent
deadlines for the others.

give the date.

* Carry forward

A Projects list where every entry has a reason and a next action. What is left
in the inbox now is mostly waiting on other people, or tied to a day.
