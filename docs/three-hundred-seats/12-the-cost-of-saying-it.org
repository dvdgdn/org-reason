* The chapter

At eight fifty-eight on Wednesday, Sergio sent a photograph of the new menu
beside his quote. There were little sandwiches with the bread turned so you
could see what was in them, cups of squash soup and apple cake cut into
squares. You could eat everything with one spoon and a napkin. The photograph
came from a job he’d done at a library. On the edge of it, a woman had put her
book down open to take a sandwich.

Maya brought the photograph and both quotes to Ruth. She found her by the back
door, seeing the Wednesday driver off. The van they owned had to be started
with the driver’s door open so you could hear if the starter caught. They
waited till it did.

“How long do you have?” Maya asked.

“I have until that van comes back or somebody calls about it.”

They spread the papers on the packing table. Maya set down the figure for two
full dinners first. Ruth read it, then moved it aside to see the photograph.

“What would you call that?” Ruth asked.

“A reception.”

“Would you call it supper?”

“No.”

“What are you telling a woman who paid for supper?”

“That the room we booked won’t serve all three hundred at dinner tables. That
we can give her a place at one of these two receptions. And if that isn’t what
she wants, she gets the seventy-five dollars back.”

Ruth looked at the photograph again. “Have you counted the people who’ll want
it back?”

“I can tell you how many we can afford.”

Maya put down the original budget. The reception menu saved thirty-seven
hundred dollars against the original dinner. The second crew and reset added
twenty-five hundred. Sergio had written the two numbers on separate lines.
Ruth used the edge of the other quote to run a pencil straight down the page
between them.

“So twelve hundred better than the night we sold,” she said.

“If everybody keeps a ticket. Sixteen thousand seven hundred to the pantry.”

Ruth took a scrap of tape and wrote $14,000 on it. She stuck it on the table
beside the quote.

“Twenty-seven hundred between us and that,” she said.

“Thirty-six tickets.”

“And the thirty-seventh?”

“Thirteen thousand nine hundred twenty-five. We’d be short seventy-five
dollars. We’d still give the refund.”

Ruth looked over at the delivery schedule. “What happens if there are sixty?”

Maya had figured thirty, forty and fifty. She did sixty on the back of the
quote. Four thousand five hundred returned. Twelve thousand two hundred left.
Ruth walked to the wall and took the schedule down. She didn’t look at the
total at the bottom. She looked down the weeks, one by one.

“If I need to save eighteen hundred, I can combine some of the Friday runs
after New Year’s. That puts people on a different day.” She pulled the
schedule closer. “I have to ask the drivers while they can still say yes. And
I have to tell the building coordinators before they make their own
schedules.”

“We could wait and see how the calls go,” Maya said.

“Is your man going to hold the crew while we do that?”

“Till noon.”

Ruth tapped the paper. “The dinner leaves us short even if nobody asks for
their money. This gives us a chance to keep the runs. If people say no, we
give the money back and I call the board with the number we’ve got.”

Maya had expected to feel relief when Ruth chose. She felt it for about as
long as it took Ruth to fold the delivery schedule in half.

“The hosts,” Ruth said. “Call the ones who bought tables. Don’t have Denise’s
people read them a note from us.”

“I’ll start with Maren.”

“She’s bringing the night shift from the clinic. They’ve been trading hours to
come.”

Maya looked at the photograph. There was no angle she could hold it at that
would make it look like a dinner. She picked up the phone and called Sergio.

He could hold the food price up to a hundred and sixty guests per reception.
By Friday at noon he wanted the guaranteed maximum and the dietary orders. The
pantry would pay the quoted price even if fewer people came; at check-in they
could still keep an accurate count without buying or canceling another plate.
Maya gave him five and seven for doors. He asked if he should tell the bakery
to cut the cake small or big.

“Big,” she said.

When she hung up, Ruth was putting the route schedule back on the wall. She
used the old holes so she didn’t make two new ones.

“Now you can tell people what you have,” Ruth said.

* What Maya has just learned

Ruth writes =$14,000= on a scrap of tape and sticks it beside the quote. The
dinner as sold would leave the pantry short even if every guest came.
Receptions with refunds on offer might not. Ruth asks what happens at thirty
refunds, at thirty-seven, at sixty, and what she would do in each case. They
choose before the caterer's noon deadline. Maya had expected to feel relief.
It lasts about as long as it takes Ruth to fold a schedule.

* Your trusted system — Step 12: Purpose

/Ten minutes./

A project is a way of getting something that matters more than the project
itself. When the two come apart, keep the purpose and change the project.

1. On a new page titled *What this is for*, list the few things your projects
   serve: areas you are responsible for, and goals for this year. Five to ten
   lines.
2. Beside each project on your Projects list, write which line it serves. A
   project that serves none is a candidate for Someday / Maybe, or for
   stopping.
3. Pick the project in most trouble. Write its purpose on a slip of paper and
   put it where you can see it. Ask: is the plan as it stands still the best
   way to reach this? What else would?
4. Write the number at which the answer changes: "This works if no more than
   __." Then write what you will do above that number.

Decide while the people who depend on you can still adjust.

* The same step in Org Reason

1. Open =gtd/horizons.org= in your Org Reason directory (~C-x C-f~). Add a
   heading for each area of responsibility or goal: ~M-RET~, type it, repeat.
2. Open each project from ~C-c r a~ and extend the =For:= line from step 4 so
   it names one of those headings.
3. A project that serves nothing: ~C-c C-t~ on its heading and choose SOMEDAY to
   set it aside, or CANCELLED to stop it. Either one asks nothing more of you
   and keeps the record.
4. For the threshold in item 4, write it under the project heading:
   =Works if refunds ≤ 36. Above that: combine the Friday runs.=

*Optional: keep the forecast honest.* A threshold like that is a prediction, and
Org Reason can hold you to it. ~C-c r p~ drafts an experiment: give it a title,
the prediction ("No more than 36 of 300 will ask for a refund") and a review
date. With point on its top heading, ~C-c r f~ freezes the wording so it cannot
be quietly improved afterwards. Step 15 compares it with what happened. Lesson
7 of the [[file:../user-guide.org][user guide]] covers this in full.

* Carry forward

A purpose behind every project, and one plan you have decided to change. Other
people were promised the old plan, and they have not been told yet.
