# Volume 2: the thinking processes

A novella that teaches the Theory of Constraints thinking processes: how to
find what to change, what to change it to, and how to cause the change.

Read [principles.md](principles.md) and [snowflake.md](snowflake.md) first.

## What the reader has at the end

On paper, with nothing else:

- a goal and the few conditions that must hold to reach it;
- a list of what keeps going wrong, stated as facts;
- a diagram of causes that someone else has challenged and that survived;
- one cause that most of the troubles trace back to, held as a hypothesis;
- the conflict that has kept that cause in place, with the assumption that can
  be given up;
- a proposed change, what it should produce, and the harm it could do, with
  that harm dealt with;
- the obstacles in order and a first action;
- a statement of what they expect to see by a date, and the habit of looking.

With Org Reason as well: all of that kept as records that link to their
commitments, with the expectation frozen before the outcome. With Reason
Commons as well: all of that built with other people, each claim carrying who
proposed it, who accepts it and who objects.

## The central tensions

These are the tensions the reader must feel. Each is written as a conflict
between two fair needs. Step 3 of the Snowflake method assigns each side to a
character.

| # | Shared aim | One need | The other need | Felt as |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| T1 | Fix what is wrong | Act now on an explanation everyone accepts | Be right about the cause before spending on it | A convincing story against a correct one |
| T2 | Make the whole place work | Show that every part is working hard | Put effort where the whole is actually held up | Busy everywhere against progress somewhere |
| T3 | End a standing conflict | Keep the peace today with a compromise | Meet both sides' needs in full | Splitting the difference against dissolving the conflict |
| T4 | Bring in a good change | Protect a fragile idea from being talked to death | Find what the idea will break before it breaks it | The champion against the sceptic |
| T5 | Get the change to happen | Work it out alone, quickly and cleanly | Have the people who must change own the reasoning | Being right against being joined |
| T6 | Carry the plan through | Commit so that people can rely on it | Treat it as a prediction that may be wrong | Keeping your word against learning |

T1 is the spine of the book. The main character must lose something real by
acting on an explanation that everyone found convincing. T5 is where shared
reasoning matters, and it is the reason the Reason Commons section exists.

## The lesson ladder

Provisional. Seventeen or eighteen rungs, one chapter each. Step 4 of the
Snowflake method may merge or split rungs with the owner's agreement, but may
not reorder them.

| # | The reader learns to | Tree | Tension |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | List what keeps going wrong as plain facts, without causes or blame | | T1 |
| 2 | Say what the whole effort is for and how they would know | goal | T2 |
| 3 | Name the few conditions that must hold for that aim | goal | T2 |
| 4 | Restate each trouble against a condition it damages, and drop the ones that are only irritations | current reality | T2 |
| 5 | Join two troubles with "if … then …" and read it aloud | current reality | T1 |
| 6 | Find the missing "and": the second cause without which the first is not enough | current reality | T1 |
| 7 | Invite a challenge, and tell a fair objection from a dislike | current reality | T1, T5 |
| 8 | Ask "if this cause were real, what else would I see?" and go and look | current reality | T1 |
| 9 | Follow the arrows down to the cause most troubles share, and ask whether it is within reach | current reality | T2 |
| 10 | Ask why a known cause is still there, and draw the conflict behind it | conflict | T3 |
| 11 | Say the "because" under each arrow of the conflict | conflict | T3 |
| 12 | Find the assumption that can be given up, and the change that gives it up | conflict | T3 |
| 13 | Reason forward from the change to the results wanted | future reality | T4 |
| 14 | Ask the person the change would hurt, and alter the change until it no longer does | future reality | T4, T5 |
| 15 | List what stands in the way, turn each obstacle into something to achieve, and put them in order | prerequisite | T5 |
| 16 | Write each action with what it should cause, and choose the first | transition | T6 |
| 17 | Say what should be visible by when, look on that date, and revise | | T6 |
| 18 | Notice that the limit has moved, and begin again | | T2 |

Rung 7 must teach the standard tests of a causal claim in plain words: is the
statement clear, is it a fact, does the cause really produce the effect, is
the cause enough on its own, is there another cause, are cause and effect the
wrong way round, and would the predicted effect be visible. They are taught by
a character raising each objection, not as a list.

## Constraints on the story

The Snowflake method produces the story. These are the limits it works within.

- **Setting.** An organisation with an aim that can be measured, several
  groups who each do their own work well, and a history of sensible
  improvements that left things worse. Candidates named so far: a struggling
  school, a hospital department. Step 1 offers three candidates.
- **Not a factory and not an event.** The first is Goldratt's own ground in
  *The Goal*; the second is volume 1.
- **The main character** has responsibility for the result and no authority
  over most of the people who produce it. They are capable and have already
  tried working harder.
- **The mentor** asks questions, has limited time, and wants something of
  their own. They are not called Jonah, which is Goldratt's character. They are
  absent for at least one part, during which the main character misuses the
  method (principles, rule 4).
- **The first fix** is a real improvement to one part of the organisation. It
  succeeds on its own measure and harms the whole. This is T2 made visible.
- **The false cause.** Around the end of the first part the group settles on
  an explanation that is persuasive and wrong. Acting on it produces the
  second disaster. The reader should have believed it too.
- **The cost of the answer.** The real change removes something a sympathetic
  character depends on. Rung 14 is that character's scene.
- **Shared world.** If the owner chooses it, one character from volume 1 may
  appear. The scene must pass the independence test.

## The page format for this volume

Six parts, in this order, with these headings.

1. `* The chapter`
2. `* What <name> has just learned`
3. `* Your thinking — Step N: <title>`
4. `* The same step in Org Reason`
5. `* The same step in Reason Commons`
6. `* Carry forward`

Part 3 uses plain words. A diagram is "boxes and arrows on a sheet of paper".
The formal names of the trees are given once each, in the appendix and in a
closing line of the step where the tree is finished.

## Synergy with volume 1

Every row must be covered by the **If you keep a trusted system** paragraph of
at least one page.

| Result of a step | What a reader with a trusted system does with it |
|---|---|
| A fact to check (rung 8) | A next action or a waiting-for entry, with the date they will look |
| A person to ask for a challenge (rung 7, 14) | An agenda entry for that person |
| The change they intend (rung 12) | A project with an outcome that can be checked |
| Each thing to achieve, in order (rung 15) | Later steps under that project; only the first is a next action |
| The first action (rung 16) | One next action, linked back to the action in the tree |
| What they expect by when (rung 17) | A prediction frozen before the date, reviewed from the dashboard |
| The cause they are working on (rung 9) | The current constraint, set by hand, shown at the top of the hub |
| A weekly look at whether the limit has moved (rung 18) | One line added to the weekly review |

Existing commands, which the writer must confirm against the tagged release:

| Need | Command |
|---|---|
| Start a commons for the problem | `C-c r t c` |
| Propose a statement or a relationship, including joint causes | `C-c r t n`, `C-c r t l` |
| Record a challenge or an assumption | `C-c r t ?` |
| Accept an item | `C-c r t a` |
| See the trees and the accepted diagrams | `C-c r t v`, `C-c r t g` |
| Turn a transition action into a next action | `C-c r t x` |
| Draft, freeze and resolve a prediction | `C-c r p`, `C-c r f`, `C-c r o` |
| Note an expectation that was not met | `C-c r !` |

## What the Reason Commons section says

It answers one question per page: what changes when this step is done with
other people, or with an assistant, instead of alone?

| Rungs | The section clarifies |
|---|---|
| 1–4 | A commons is a shared record of one problem. Statements are proposed by a named contributor and are not yet anyone's agreed view |
| 5–6 | A relationship is its own claim. A joint cause is one claim about several statements together |
| 7 | An objection is recorded with its author and stays visible until it is answered. Acceptance is an act by a person, not a default |
| 8 | An observation is a different kind of statement from a hypothesis, and says who saw it |
| 9–12 | People can accept the conflict's two needs while disagreeing about the assumption. The record shows exactly where they part |
| 13–14 | The person who would be hurt adds the harmful branch themselves, under their own name |
| 15–16 | An action in the shared record and a reminder in one person's lists are two things, linked |
| 17–18 | What was expected is fixed before the result, and the accepted view has a history that shows how it changed |

The section follows the same pattern as the Org Reason section: one line on
the purpose, the concrete actions, a way to confirm the result. Before writing
it, read `docs/reasoncommons.md` and the upstream material listed in
`docs/sources.md`, and name the version the page was checked against.

## The appendix

- How the story was built: the sentence, the paragraph, the characters under
  pressure, the scene map.
- The method summed up, with the six trees named and drawn from the story.
- The story's finished reasoning, exported from the story commons.
- Sources and acknowledgements.

Sources to read before step 0, and to add to `docs/sources.md`:

- Eliyahu Goldratt, *It's Not Luck* (1994), the novel that introduces the
  thinking processes; and *The Goal* (1984).
- H. William Dettmer, *The Logical Thinking Process* (2007), for the goal tree
  and the tests of a causal claim.
- Lisa Scheinkopf, *Thinking for a Change* (1999).

Check how each author's terms may be used and acknowledge them as volume 1
does for GTD. Do not use Goldratt's characters or examples.
