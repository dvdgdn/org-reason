# Volume 3: deliberate practice

A novella about someone who wants to become more capable, has read everything
about how, and cannot begin. It teaches how to choose a skill, practise it
with feedback, and find out whether the practice changed anything outside the
practice itself.

Read [principles.md](principles.md) and [snowflake.md](snowflake.md) first.

## What the reader has at the end

On paper, with nothing else:

- one real-world scene they want to be true, in place of a vague wish to be
  better;
- the part of that scene that fails today, found by trying it;
- a small repeatable exercise at the edge of what they can do, with a source
  of feedback;
- a record of attempts, feedback and adjustments;
- a check in real conditions, with a date, and a written guess at its result
  made beforehand;
- a way to sort any technique they meet by what kind of proof it can give.

With Org Reason as well: skills as records, sessions logged by the tools that
score them, the guess frozen before the check, and one panel that shows what
is due.

## The people

**The main character** has collected years of articles, studies and courses on
cognitive improvement: higher scores, better recall, changes in the brain. The
more promising the reports, the harder starting has become, for two reasons
they can state: they do not know where to enter, and they cannot see how they
would ever know they were improving.

**The mentor** studies expert performance for a living and is careful about
evidence. Privately, and for years, they have also practised things their
colleagues would laugh at: image cycling, and other methods from the edge of
the field. They hold the two to different standards and say which standard
they are using. Their stake: they have never told a colleague. Not called
Jonah.

The mentor's double life is the book's second engine. The main character came
to them as the scientific one, and is shaken when the mentor's answer to the
second complaint is not scientific at all.

## The central tensions

| # | Shared aim | One need | The other need | Felt as |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| P1 | Choose well | Know enough before committing time | Begin, since only practice produces information about oneself | Reading against doing |
| P2 | Practise in a way that works | Have someone who knows guide and correct | Start with what is actually available | Waiting for a teacher against going alone |
| P3 | Aim at the right thing | Define the target before working toward it | Admit one cannot yet understand the ability one lacks | Needing a map of a place one has not been |
| P4 | Really improve | See progress, which keeps one going | Improve at the thing itself, not at its exercise | The score against the ability |
| P5 | Find what works | Demand proof, and keep one's standing | Try what has no proof yet, since the proven is by definition not new | Rigour against exploration |
| P6 | Become broadly more capable | Follow up every promising method | Give one method enough hours to tell | Ten techniques against one hour a day |

P4 is the spine. The main character must be shown rising scores that turn out
to mean nothing in the scene they care about, and must have been proud of
them. P5 is the mentor's tension as much as the main character's.

## The two complaints as conflicts

These go in the appendix as full diagrams and in the story commons. In the
chapters they are conversations.

### "Deliberate practice needs a teacher"

- Aim: become more capable in fact.
- Need 1: practise at the edge of ability with informed, prompt correction.
- Need 2: begin now, with the means available.
- Action for need 1: wait until a qualified teacher is found.
- Action for need 2: practise alone.
- Assumption that can be given up: only a human expert can design the
  exercise and correct the attempt.
- The change: an AI assistant as coach. It proposes exercises, compares an
  attempt with a model, and asks what was noticed.

The mentor adds a harder point, which is accurate and must be kept. Anders
Ericsson used "deliberate practice" for fields that already have known
training methods and teachers, and "purposeful practice" for the rest. For
several techniques on the main character's list there is no such field, so
there is no teacher to find, human or otherwise. There the coach cannot say
what good looks like. The only teacher available is a guess written down in
advance and checked against what happens.

Limits to show in the story: the assistant can be confidently wrong, it sees
only what it is told, and it cannot run the real-world check. What is measured
and what counts as transfer remain the practitioner's decisions.

### "I am grasping in the dark"

- Aim: become substantially more capable than now.
- Need 1: aim practice at a definite target.
- Need 2: stay honest about what one can currently understand.
- Action for need 1: specify the ability precisely before practising.
- Action for need 2: leave it unspecified, and so drift or never start.
- Assumption that can be given up: to aim at an ability one must be able to
  describe it analytically from where one stands.
- The change: picture a concrete scene in which the ability is already in use,
  without describing the ability. The mentor's source for this is the first
  half of image cycling: a list of wishes, each written as a scene of the wish
  already granted, ends and not means.

This gives the volume its key device. **One scene, used twice.** The scene is
an image on the main character's cycling list. The same scene is the
real-world check for the skill: either it happens or it does not. The
speculative practice and the careful one meet in a single sentence the reader
writes.

## Sorting the techniques

| | A tool or a count can score it | Only the practitioner can report it |
|---|---|---|
| **Works for the skill practised** | Spaced repetition, language learning, mnemonics, blindfold chess with the Woodpecker method, juggling | none |
| **Wider benefit disputed or untested** | Dual n-back, SMART relational training | Image cycling, image streaming, multiple mentality |

The cell a technique sits in decides what the reader must do to learn anything
from it.

- Top left: practise, and check the skill in real conditions.
- Bottom left: the score will rise. The only question is whether anything
  else does, so the real-world check and the advance guess are compulsory.
- Bottom right: there is no score. Write a fixed routine, decide beforehand
  what will be reported and what would count against the practice, and guess
  in advance.

The novella treats three in depth, one per occupied cell: spaced repetition in
the service of a language, dual n-back, and image cycling. The other six are
the [casebook](casebook.md).

The placements above are working assumptions. The evidence ledger (package
V3.1) confirms or corrects each one before any chapter relies on it.

## The lesson ladder

Provisional. Sixteen rungs.

| # | The reader learns to | Tension |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Lay out what they have collected and never practised, and count it | P1 |
| 2 | Write one scene they want to be true, with place, people and what they are doing | P3 |
| 3 | Write the wider list of such scenes, and keep it apart from any plan | P3 |
| 4 | Try the scene today and note exactly where it breaks | P1, P3 |
| 5 | Shrink the break into one exercise that can be repeated in minutes | P2 |
| 6 | Name where correction will come from, and how soon after the attempt | P2 |
| 7 | Use an assistant as coach, and say what stays their own judgment | P2 |
| 8 | Record an attempt, the correction and the change for next time | P4 |
| 9 | Space the repetitions instead of massing them | P4 |
| 10 | Tell the score from the ability, and set the real-world check and its date | P4 |
| 11 | Write a guess at the check's result, with a probability, and seal it | P4, P5 |
| 12 | Change the exercise, not the hours, when progress stops | P4 |
| 13 | Give an unscorable practice a fixed routine and decide what would count against it | P5 |
| 14 | Place any technique in the table above and state the proof it owes | P5, P6 |
| 15 | Run the check and record the result whatever it is | P4 |
| 16 | Decide to continue, change or stop, and review the whole practice weekly | P6 |

## Image cycling in this volume

The owner has a written primer on image cycling, based on William Bengston's
*The Energy Cure*. It quotes the book and is not in this repository. Ask the
owner for it before writing rungs 2, 3 and 13, and do not commit it without
their decision.

What the volume takes from it:

- The list has at least twenty wishes the person truly has for themselves.
- Each is a specific scene of the wish already granted: the end, never the
  means. Being on the boat, not earning the money.
- Each scene is entered with all the senses for some minutes when it is made,
  and may be given a one-word cue.
- All scenes count equally, are not joined into a story, and are learned so
  they can be recalled in any order.
- They are then run through faster and faster until they blur, and this is
  done alongside whatever emotion is present, without trying to change it.
- The list is reviewed weekly and kept current.

What the volume must not do:

- Present cycling as a way to make wishes come true. Bengston says the list
  was made as a tool for cycling and is not a manifestation exercise.
- Suggest any medical or healing benefit.
- Cite Bengston's published experiments as evidence that cycling improves
  thinking. They concern healing in mice. The mentor may point to them as an
  example of careful work on an unwelcome question, and should say himself
  what they do not show.
- Let the software assert that the practice works. It records the routine, the
  self-report and the frozen guess, with the tier "untested".

The mentor's line to the main character, in substance: the edge of any field
looks far-fetched from inside it; working there means living with uncertainty
and some ridicule; the choice to do so is the main character's own. The book
does not decide for the reader whether cycling works.

## Evidence ledger

Package V3.1 creates `docs/series/evidence-ledger.md`. One row per technique:

| Column | Content |
|---|---|
| Technique | Name |
| Claim | What it is said to improve |
| Effect on the practised skill | What the sources show |
| Effect beyond it | What the sources show |
| Sources | Primary studies and reviews, each read by the person filling the row |
| Tier | Established, disputed, or untested |
| Feedback | Scored or self-reported |
| Open questions | What the reader's own check could tell them |

Figures in the planning notes are unverified until read. In particular the
notes quote a meta-analysis of SMART relational training (an effect on
nonverbal IQ of g = 0.57 from five studies, 195 participants, all at high risk
of bias). Read the paper before repeating any of it:
<https://bera-journals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/bjet.13192>.

Also read Ericsson and Pool, *Peak* (2016), for the distinction between
deliberate and purposeful practice.
